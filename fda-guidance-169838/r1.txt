15   I.      INTRODUCTION
16
17   Under section 503B of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act) (21 U.S.C.
18   353b), a drug compounded by an outsourcing facility2 qualifies for exemptions from certain
19   statutory requirements if, among other conditions, the drug “will not be sold or transferred by an
20   entity other than the outsourcing facility that compounded such drug.”3 However, this provision
21   “does not prohibit administration of a drug in a health care setting or dispensing a drug pursuant
22   to a prescription executed in accordance with section 503(b)(1).”4 This guidance describes
23   FDA’s interpretation of, and policies concerning, the prohibition on wholesaling in section 503B
24   of the FD&C Act. This guidance also describes examples of how FDA intends to apply section
25   503B’s wholesaling provision.
26
27   In general, FDA’s guidance documents do not establish legally enforceable responsibilities.
28   Instead, guidances describe the Agency’s current thinking on a topic and should be viewed only
29   as recommendations, unless specific regulatory or statutory requirements are cited. The use of
30   the word should in Agency guidances means that something is suggested or recommended, but
31   not required.

34   II.      BACKGROUND
35
36   Section 503B of the FD&C Act describes the conditions that must be satisfied for human drug
37   products compounded by an outsourcing facility to be exempt from section 505 (21 U.S.C. 355)
38   (concerning the approval of drugs under new drug applications or abbreviated new drug
39   applications), section 502(f)(1) (21 U.S.C. 352(f)(1)) (concerning the labeling of drugs with
40   adequate directions for use), and section 582 of the FD&C Act (21 U.S.C. 360eee-1) (concerning
41   drug supply chain security requirements).5
42
43   In contrast to drug products compounded in compliance with section 503A of the FD&C Act,
44   drugs compounded in compliance with section 503B of the FD&C Act are not exempt from
45   current good manufacturing practice (CGMP) requirements in section 501(a)(2)(B) of the FD&C
46   Act. Furthermore, among other requirements under section 501, a drug is deemed to be
47   adulterated under section 501(a)(2)(A) if it has been prepared, packed, or held under insanitary
48   conditions whereby it may have been contaminated with filth, or whereby it may have been
49   rendered injurious to health, regardless of whether the drugs qualify for exemptions set forth in
50   section 503B of the FD&C Act.6 Outsourcing facilities are also subject to FDA inspections
51   according to a risk-based schedule, specific adverse event reporting requirements, and other
52   conditions that help to mitigate the risks of the drugs they compound.7 In addition, outsourcing
53   facilities may or may not obtain prescriptions for identified individual patients.8
54
55   One of the conditions that must be met for a drug compounded by an outsourcing facility to
56   qualify for the exemptions in section 503B of the FD&C Act is that the drug “will not be sold or
57   transferred by an entity other than the outsourcing facility that compounded such drug.”9
58   However, this provision “does not prohibit administration of a drug in a health care setting or
59   dispensing a drug pursuant to a prescription executed in accordance with section 503(b)(1).”10
60
61   The statutory prohibition on wholesaling in section 503B(a)(8) of the FD&C Act helps to ensure
62   that compounding is based on individual patients’ needs, which, in turn, reduces the overall risk
63   of patient harm and helps to preserve the integrity of the U.S. drug approval process. It also
64   helps to preserve the integrity of the U.S. drug supply chain. This prohibition, like other
65   conditions in section 503B, preserves important distinctions between outsourcing facilities,
66   which are intended to compound drugs for patients whose medical needs cannot be met by
67   approved drugs, from conventional manufacturers, which generally engage in mass
68   manufacturing of FDA-approved drug products.11

 70   Compounded drugs present a higher risk to patients than approved drugs. Compounded drugs
 71   are not FDA-approved and have not been reviewed by the Agency for safety, effectiveness, or
 72   quality before they are marketed. Because compounded drugs are subject to a lower regulatory
 73   standard than approved drugs, patients should not receive them unless an approved drug does not
 74   meet their medical needs. Accordingly, although compounding under section 503B of the
 75   FD&C Act need not be based on the receipt of a prescription for an individually identified
 76   patient, Congress nevertheless contemplated a relationship between the outsourcing facility and
 77   prescriber to supply compounded drugs only to such patients.12 The more attenuated the
 78   connection between the outsourcing facility and patient or prescriber, the more the outsourcing
 79   facility resembles a conventional drug manufacturer that distributes drug products to its
 80   customers without regard to individual patient need.
 81
 82   Similarly, the prohibition on wholesaling helps to preserve the integrity of the drug approval
 83   process. Approval of a new drug means that FDA has determined that it is safe and effective and
 84   that it was manufactured by a facility with appropriate methods used in, and facilities and
 85   controls used for, preserving the drug’s identity, strength, quality, and purity to produce a high-
 86   quality product.13 Approval of new, innovative treatments can save lives, and approval of
 87   generic drug products can increase access to critical therapies. If an outsourcing facility could
 88   manufacture drug products for further sale or transfer by, for example, wholesale distributors, the
 89   outsourcing facility would more closely resemble a conventional manufacturer whose drug
 90   products are subject to new drug approval requirements. Section 503B of the FD&C Act
 91   therefore imposes conditions which reduce the risk that conventional manufacturers would forgo
 92   seeking FDA approval of their drug products to instead market unapproved, compounded drugs
 93   as an outsourcing facility. Because the typical supply chain for approved drug products involves
 94   wholesaling, consisting of multiple transfers between the manufacture of the drug and ultimately
 95   dispensing or administering it to a patient, section 503B’s prohibition on wholesaling helps
 96   reduce the incentive to compound drugs as an outsourcing facility rather than seek premarket
 97   approval.
 98
 99   Finally, because drugs compounded by outsourcing facilities in accordance with the conditions
100   of section 503B of the FD&C Act are exempt from drug supply chain security requirements in
101   section 582 of the FD&C Act, the prohibition on wholesaling is an important control to protect
102   the drug supply chain. Conditioning the exemption from section 582 on limitations on the sale
103   or transfer of a compounded drug reduces supply chain concerns such as diversion. The short
104   supply chain required under section 503B also makes it easier to associate the compounded drug
105   with the relevant outsourcing facility in the case of an adverse event or product quality issue.
106   We further note that outsourcing facilities are required to report adverse events to FDA.14

109   III.     POLICY
110
111            A.       Key Terms
112
113            1.       Sold or Transferred
114
115   As discussed above, one of the conditions for a drug compounded by an outsourcing facility to
116   qualify for the exemptions in section 503B of the FD&C Act is that the drug “will not be sold or
117   transferred by an entity other than the outsourcing facility that compounded such drug.”15 This
118   provision “does not prohibit administration of a drug in a health care setting or dispensing a drug
119   pursuant to a prescription executed in accordance with section 503(b)(1).”16 The phrase, “sold or
120   transferred by an entity other than the outsourcing facility,” as used in section 503B(a)(8) of the
121   FD&C Act, encompasses instances when an entity other than the outsourcing facility that
122   compounded a drug has sold or transferred the drug. Transfers, for purposes of this provision,
123   encompass movements of the drug from one entity to another, regardless of whether the drug
124   was sold as part of the transfer. We note that Congress included within the wholesaling
125   prohibition the sale or transfer of compounded drugs, indicating that the provision is intended to
126   capture movements where money does not change hands.
127
128   FDA generally does not intend to apply this provision in instances when a common carrier that
129   has experience in safely and securely delivering drug products to health care providers, which
130   does not take ownership of a product, takes physical possession of a drug compounded by an
131   outsourcing facility but only provides transportation services by delivering the drug to the
132   outsourcing facility’s customers. Additionally, FDA generally does not intend to apply this
133   provision when an outsourcing facility uses an authorized third-party logistics provider (3PL),17
134   which also does not take ownership of products, to provide or coordinate warehousing or other
135   logistics services for the compounded drug on behalf of the outsourcing facility. The
136   compounded drugs would remain subject to CGMP and other requirements, including with
137   regard to proper storage and handling. Outsourcing facilities should only use common carriers
138   and 3PLs that will provide appropriate safeguards for their compounded drugs.
139

157            2.       Entities Other Than the Outsourcing Facility
158
159   Generally, a drug compounded by an outsourcing facility is not eligible for the statutory
160   exemptions under section 503B of the FD&C Act if the drug is sold or transferred by an “entity
161   other than the outsourcing facility that compounded such drug.”23 FDA interprets this phrase to
162   refer to any entity that sells or transfers a drug compounded by an outsourcing facility, other than
163   the outsourcing facility that compounded the drug, regardless of whether that entity has a
164   physical address.
165
166   As discussed in more detail below, examples may include:

169            section III.A.3.)
170        •   Wholesale distributors
171        •   Repackers and relabelers
172        •   Marketing firms
173        •   Website owners and operators
174        •   Pharmacies (including mail order pharmacies) (but see section III.A.3.)
175        •   Clinics (including virtual clinics) (but see section III.A.3.)

178          3.      Administration or Dispensing
179
180   Section 503B(a)(8) of the FD&C Act provides that the prohibition on wholesaling “does not
181   prohibit administration of a drug in a health care setting or dispensing a drug pursuant to a
182   prescription executed in accordance with section 503(b)(1).”
183
184   FDA interprets this provision to mean that a drug compounded by an outsourcing facility may be
185   eligible for the exemptions in section 503B of the FD&C Act where the drug is distributed
186   directly from an outsourcing facility to a health care facility, such as a hospital or clinic, where
187   the drug is administered to a patient, or to a State-licensed pharmacy or Federal facility where
188   the drug is dispensed pursuant to a prescription executed in accordance with section 503(b)(1) of
189   the FD&C Act. As discussed further below, certain activities by a health care facility or
190   pharmacy would not be considered to constitute administration in a health care setting or
191   dispensing to a patient in accordance with section 503(b)(1). In addition, the fact that a
192   compounded drug is ultimately dispensed by a pharmacy or administered in a health care setting
193   is not determinative of whether wholesaling (i.e., sales or transfers prohibited by section
194   503B(a)(8)) has occurred before that point, because ordinarily all prescription drugs are
195   ultimately dispensed by a pharmacy or administered in a health care setting.
196
197          B.      Application of the Wholesaling Prohibition to Examples of Sales and
198                  Transfers
199
200          1.      Activities Prohibited by the Wholesaling Provision
201
202   Drugs compounded by outsourcing facilities must meet the conditions in section 503B of the
203   FD&C Act, including the prohibition on wholesaling under section 503B(a)(8), to be eligible for
204   the statutory exemptions available under that section. This guidance provides FDA’s policies
205   concerning the prohibition on wholesaling, as explained in section III.A. In general, as described
206   in section III.A.1 and 2, the wholesaling provision applies to the sale or transfer of a
207   compounded drug by an entity other than the outsourcing facility that compounded the drug. As
208   described in section III.A.3, the prohibition on wholesaling does not apply to the administration
209   of a compounded drug in a health care setting or dispensing a compounded drug pursuant to a
210   prescription in accordance with section 503(b)(1) of the FD&C Act. None of the scenarios in
211   section III.B.1 involve, among other things, administration of a drug in a health care setting or
212   dispensing pursuant to a prescription in accordance with section 503(b)(1), so the exception to
213   the prohibition on wholesaling would not apply to any of them.
214
215   The following are examples of situations that generally would be subject to the wholesaling
216   prohibition of section 503B(a)(8) of the FD&C Act (i.e., in these situations the drug would be
217   considered sold or transferred by an entity other than the outsourcing facility that compounded
218   such drug):
219
220      (a) An outsourcing facility distributes a drug it compounded to a wholesale distributor that
221          sells or otherwise transfers the compounded drug.

371              C.        Inspections and Regulatory or Enforcement Action
372
373   Pursuant to section 704(a) of the FD&C Act, during inspections of outsourcing facilities, FDA
374   reviews records, such as contractual agreements, distribution data, shipment data, and customer
375   lists to evaluate compliance with section 503B(a)(8) of the FD&C Act.27 If the Agency identifies
376   agreements or procedures concerning a third party’s sale or transfer of the outsourcing facility’s
377   compounded drugs that are not limited to administration in a health care setting or dispensing
378   pursuant to a prescription in accordance with section 503(b)(1), FDA may consider pursuing
379   regulatory or enforcement action against the outsourcing facility because drugs that are not
380   compounded in accordance with the conditions of section 503B of the FD&C Act are subject to
381   the requirements of sections 505, 502(f)(1), and 582 of the FD&C Act. The third party that sold
382   or transferred the outsourcing facility’s drugs may also be subject to regulatory or enforcement
383   action for distributing or causing the distribution of drugs in violation of section 301 of the
384   FD&C Act.
