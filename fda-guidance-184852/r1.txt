I.     Introduction
The Food and Drug Administration (FDA or Agency) plays a critical role in protecting the
United States from threats such as emerging infectious diseases, potential public health
emergencies, and public health emergencies. FDA is issuing this guidance to describe
recommendations for validation of certain in vitro diagnostic devices (IVDs) for emerging
pathogens when the Secretary of Health and Human Services has declared that the circumstances
exist justifying emergency use authorizations (EUAs) for such IVDs under section 564 of the
Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act) (hereafter referred to as an “applicable 564
declaration”), based on an underlying determination under section 564(b)(1) of the FD&C Act.

For the current edition of the FDA-recognized consensus standards referenced in this document,
see the FDA Recognized Consensus Standards Database. For more information regarding use of
consensus standards in regulatory submissions, please refer to the FDA guidance entitled
“Appropriate Use of Voluntary Consensus Standards in Premarket Submissions for Medical
Devices.”

In general, FDA’s guidance documents do not establish legally enforceable responsibilities.
Instead, guidances describe the Agency’s current thinking on a topic and should be viewed only
as recommendations, unless specific regulatory or statutory requirements are cited. The use of
the word should in Agency guidances means that something is suggested or recommended, but
not required.

II. Background
The Emergency Use Authorization (EUA) authority under section 564 of the FD&C Act allows
FDA to help strengthen the nation’s public health protections against chemical, biological,
radiological, and nuclear (CBRN) threats by facilitating the availability and use of medical
countermeasures (MCMs) needed during an actual or potential emergency or material threat.
Under section 564 of the FD&C Act, when the Secretary of Health and Human Services (HHS)
declares that the circumstances exist justifying the issuance of EUAs, FDA may authorize certain
unapproved medical products or unapproved uses of approved medical products to diagnose,
treat, or prevent serious or life-threatening diseases or conditions caused by CBRN agents when
certain criteria are met, including when there are no adequate, approved, and available
alternatives. For information on FDA’s implementation of this and other emergency use
authorities, see FDA’s guidance “Emergency Use Authorization of Medical Products and
Related Authorities.”

Accurate and reliable IVDs are critical to the diagnosis, tracking, treatment, and interruption of
transmission of infectious diseases during outbreaks, as well as for diagnosing and treating
diseases or conditions caused by CBRN threats. In the public health emergencies of COVID-191
and monkeypox (mpox),2 FDA issued guidances that included enforcement discretion policies
for certain unauthorized tests to help rapidly increase national testing capacity early in the
outbreaks. Certain tests were made available prior to or without an EUA as described in those
policies. Regardless of whether a test is issued an EUA or offered as described in an enforcement
discretion policy, it is critical that the test be appropriately validated. FDA may take action, as
appropriate, against violative tests, including those that lack appropriate validation. This
guidance and associated template(s) are intended to help test manufacturers better prepare for
future public health emergencies by including FDA’s recommendations for test validation during
an applicable 564 declaration.

Also, this guidance and associated templates address the recommendations received from two
independent assessments of FDA’s response to COVID-19. Specifically, FDA selected Booz
Allen Hamilton to do such an independent assessment, which culminated in an October 2021
report, “Emergency Use Authorization Assessment – Final Report,” recommending that FDA
“develop a framework for how to conduct validation of diagnostic tests for emerging pathogens
in the setting of a declared PHE.” Similarly, the HHS Office of the Inspector General’s
September 2022 report, “FDA Repeatedly Adapted Emergency Use Authorization Policies To
Address the Need for COVID-19 Testing,” recommended that FDA “develop a suite of EUA
templates for future emergencies involving novel pathogens” and “expand and improve
resources” on the EUA process, among other actions FDA has taken or is taking.

IV. Availability of Templates
FDA has found the use of templates to be beneficial during prior emergencies, for both
manufacturers and FDA reviewers, to help facilitate the preparation and submission of pre-EUAs
and EUA requests to FDA, and any resulting authorization. Templates should be viewed only as
recommendations, and alternative approaches can be used unless specific regulatory or statutory
requirements are cited.

A generic template entitled “General IVD Emergency Use Authorization (EUA) Request/Pre-
EUA Template” is made available through download from our website,7 and it reflects FDA’s
current thinking on data and information that should be submitted in pre-EUAs and EUA
requests. FDA may provide more tailored recommendations for tests for a specific outbreak
through separate guidance or pathogen-specific templates, as appropriate.

Additional templates may be added to our website. For example, FDA may provide updated
templates as appropriate in the event of a specific outbreak.


V.      Validation Study Recommendations
Validation should objectively demonstrate that a finished device can consistently fulfill defined
user needs and its intended use. The validation recommendations discussed here reflect that the
“may be effective” standard for EUAs8 provides for a lower level of evidence than the
“effectiveness” standard that FDA uses for product approvals,9 as discussed in the FDA
guidance, “Emergency Use Authorization of Medical Products and Related Authorities.” As
such, the validation should demonstrate that the device may be effective for its intended use and
that the known and potential benefits of the device when used as intended outweigh the known
and potential risks of the device. This guidance also reflects the understanding that, in rapidly
developing or unexpected emergency circumstances, it may not be possible for a manufacturer to
collect and/or provide all of the recommended data, including in the format suggested in a timely
manner. In such cases, additional information may become available and/or FDA might request
such information at a later date.

Ideally, validation testing is performed using the final design of the device and the proposed
instructions for use. This ensures that validation is most reflective of performance of the device
following authorization, although an equivalency study can sometimes be used to address device
changes following validation. Validation protocols and reports should be provided, including
study objectives, test procedures, and information on the instrument(s), reagents, software, and

                 Clinical Performance Evaluation
A clinical performance evaluation is used to assess the accuracy of a test in detecting the analyte
of interest, often through method comparison or comparison of test results to known positive and
negative specimens. This type of study typically should characterize the performance of the
device as it would be expected to perform in actual clinical use. The number of samples and the
design of the study, such as an appropriate comparator method, will depend on many factors
including the target pathogen, type of test, availability of clinical specimens containing the
pathogen, availability of appropriate comparator methods, the stage of the outbreak, and overall
testing needs.

For example, for a viral nucleic acid test using established technologies known to be capable of
high sensitivity detection, at a stage of the outbreak where clinical samples and comparators are
generally available, at least 30 positive and 30 negative specimens (as defined by the
comparator) from the intended use population and using the intended specimen type are typically
adequate to demonstrate the performance of the test compared to a highly sensitive comparator
method. Evaluation of fewer specimens may not accurately characterize the true performance of
the test.11

For infectious disease indications, a highly sensitive comparator method is often a molecular

          (1) Initial Stages of the Outbreak - Alternative Specimen Types
Natural clinical specimens are the preferred sample type for validation of a diagnostic test.
However, in the early stages of an emerging disease outbreak, natural clinical specimens may not
be readily available. In such cases, the use of archived positive and negative clinical specimens12
could be a reasonable alternative. Ideally, archived specimens should be accompanied by
information to determine sample adequacy, such as the specimen collection date, specimen
storage conditions, and date of onset of symptoms.

When prospective and archived samples are not available, use of contrived (e.g., spiked) samples
may be acceptable. Contrived samples are constructed in the laboratory by placing known
concentrations of a microorganism or analyte into individual (not pooled) human specimens
known to be negative for that microorganism or analyte (i.e., negative clinical matrix13). In
situations where pathogen stocks are not available, such as at the early stages of an outbreak,
FDA would likely consider use of synthetic material to be acceptable.14 When synthetic material
is used, it should closely mimic natural materials. For example, if the pathogen is an RNA virus,
then synthetic RNA, rather than synthetic DNA, should be used in most cases.

Due to limitations of validation with contrived samples, including those prepared using synthetic
or natural materials, emergency use authorization of such tests would likely include a Condition
of Authorization (CoA) requiring a post-authorization clinical performance evaluation with
natural patient specimens when it becomes feasible. When a clinical performance evaluation is
not a prospective study, FDA recommends that manufacturers ensure that their evaluation
includes samples that appropriately represent the range of pathogen levels expected in clinical
specimens in the intended use population.

          (2) Study Design
Ideally, clinical performance should be established through a prospective, all-comers clinical
study in the intended use environment, by the intended user(s), and with natural clinical
specimens from the intended use patient population(s).

In some cases, it might be appropriate for the clinical performance evaluation to include only the
most challenging clinical matrix type proposed in the intended use of the device15 (e.g.,
nasopharyngeal (NP) swabs for common upper respiratory types, sputum for common lower
respiratory types). For atypical specimen types, the clinical performance evaluation should
evaluate each specimen type included in the intended use of the device.16 In addition, specimens
from the same anatomical site but different in collection or transport methods, such as with and
without liquid transportation medium, are considered as two distinct types of specimens and
should each be validated.

Subjects should be enrolled in the clinical performance evaluation to reflect the intended use
population(s). For example, if the test is intended for use with asymptomatic individuals, the
study protocol and report should document how individuals were screened and confirm that all
enrolled individuals were free of any symptoms of the target infection prior to enrollment and
sample collection. In cases where obtaining positive samples from asymptomatic individuals is
challenging, enrichment strategies that minimize potential biases could be considered (e.g.,
conducting the study in an asymptomatic screening population that is under quarantine due to
possible exposure may increase the likelihood of obtaining more positive specimens).
Manufacturers should consult FDA prior to implementing enrichment approaches in your clinical
performance evaluation.

          (3) Clinical Data Analysis
Generally, all samples meeting pre-defined inclusion criteria for the clinical performance
evaluation should be included in the analysis. Justification should be provided for exclusion of
any such samples from data analysis. FDA generally recommends that clinical data analysis
include the calculation of positive percent agreement (PPA) and negative percent agreement
(NPA) with a highly sensitive comparator method, and corresponding 95% confidence intervals
using the Wilson Score method. Target PPA and NPA to support adequate performance of a
diagnostic test depend on the test type and indications for use as well as a benefit/risk assessment
in the context of the emerging outbreak. FDA may consider lower PPAs acceptable for different
test types and intended use populations with certain mitigations, such as reporting of negative
test results as “presumptive” based on benefit-risk considerations. In certain cases, such as for
screening tests for asymptomatic individuals, a higher NPA has generally been expected. If the
test is intended for symptomatic individuals, the data should include time from symptom onset to
test for each enrolled subject. The data analysis should account for performance shifts in relation
to time from symptom onset to support adequate clinical performance.

          (4) Human Subject Protection
Studies involving clinical specimens (human specimens) are subject to applicable requirements
for Institutional Review Board (IRB) review and approval and informed consent (see 21 CFR
parts 50, 56, and 812). In December 2023, FDA published a final rule17 that permits an IRB to
waive or alter informed consent requirements for certain minimal risk clinical investigations that
meet the conditions in 21 CFR 50.22. FDA anticipates that this new provision may be applicable
to certain IVD studies involving clinical specimens. In addition, the FDA guidance “Guidance on
Informed Consent for In Vitro Diagnostic Device Studies Using Leftover Human Specimens that
are Not Individually Identifiable,” describes a policy about informed consent requirements for
certain IVD studies that use leftover, de-identified specimens. Use of such specimens does not
obviate consideration of the other recommendations in this guidance document. Please note that
additional requirements in the Investigational Device Exemption (IDE) regulations (21 CFR part
812) may be applicable to certain IVD clinical studies.
                  Analytical Validation Testing
In addition to the clinical performance evaluation, analytical validation studies assessing
analytical performance should also be provided, as recommended in this section.

          (1) Limit of Detection (LoD) (Analytical Sensitivity)
The LoD provides a measure of the analytical sensitivity of a test for a particular target analyte
and is defined as the lowest concentration of target analyte that is consistently detected by the
test in 95% of the specimen replicates.18 The LoD results guide additional validation studies,
including the clinical performance evaluation. LoD should be determined using the entire test
from specimen preparation and extraction through detection and the result interpretation
algorithm. In some cases, it may be appropriate to determine LoD only for the most challenging
negative clinical matrix type included in the intended use of the device. For atypical specimen
types, the LoD should be determined with each specimen type included in the intended use of the
device.

In situations where neither live stocks, inactivated stocks, nor known positive clinical specimens
are available, use of synthetic material19 may be considered for use in the LoD evaluation in real
clinical matrix. When synthetic material is used, it should closely mimic the natural target
analyte.20 Manufacturers should discuss potential use of alternative matrices for unique
circumstances with FDA. As more specimens become available, FDA generally recommends
that LoD be evaluated by spiking individual or pooled natural negative clinical matrix with well

Generally, the preliminary LoD is determined by testing a 2-3-fold dilution series of three
replicates per concentration. The lowest concentration at which all tested replicates are positive
is considered the preliminary LoD. The preliminary LoD study should include at least one
concentration that does not yield 100% positive results. The LoD should then be confirmed by
testing a minimum of 20 individual replicates of the concentration determined to be the
preliminary LoD. The final LoD is the lowest concentration resulting in positive detection of at
least 95% of the replicates (e.g., at least 19 out of 20 replicates). In the case where the final LoD
study achieves a positivity of 100%, a lower concentration (using a 3-fold dilution) should be
tested (with 20 replicates) until < 95% positivity is obtained. While the LoD for an entire analyte
is most critical for test validation, FDA might also request the LoD for individual targets for
multi-target tests to help the Agency evaluate the performance of the device.21

          (2) Inclusivity (Analytical Reactivity)
An inclusivity study shows reactivity of the test with additional related (e.g., taxonomic,
immunological, and genetic composition) target species or isolates.

For molecular-based tests, FDA generally recommends 100% nucleotide sequence identity,
meaning that the test reagents have no mismatches with known published sequences and
therefore can likely detect all known species or isolates. If a test has less than 100% nucleotide
sequence identity to a significant number of published sequences, FDA recommends performing
a risk assessment on how such mismatches may impact the performance of the test. Inclusivity of
molecular-based tests should be evaluated through in silico analysis of the test primer and probes
with all known sequence variants. In silico data should be supplemented with wet testing of
currently circulating variants, when available, by testing clinical isolates and/or inactivated
materials spiked into clinical matrix at or near the test LoD.

Inclusivity of antigen-based tests should be evaluated through wet testing of currently circulating
variants. The methods and results of this analysis should show the extent to which variation in
the target genome may affect clinical and analytical sensitivity. FDA recommends monthly
evaluation of inclusivity to account for new and emerging clinically significant mutations. This
could include, for example, assessing the prevalence of mutations in well-established publicly
available sequence databases and monitoring for credible reports that a given variant or mutation
has the potential to increase pathogenicity, increase transmission, or otherwise increase the risk
to public health. FDA might request additional testing based on its own monitoring. The
aggregate impact of the mutations should be evaluated and should not result in the clinical
performance point estimates for the test dropping below the clinical performance
recommendations described in Section V.A. If a greater than 3-fold reduction in analytical
sensitivity is observed when comparing pathogens with and without the mutation, manufacturers
should conduct a risk analysis for the observed difference in performance, consider further risk
mitigations, and assess whether the known and potential benefits of the test continue to outweigh
the known and potential risks.

          (3) Specimen Stability
Degradation of a specimen prior to testing can lead to false results. The Centers for Disease
Control and Prevention (CDC) often communicates acceptable specimen stability conditions for
certain specimen types to be tested for specific analytes.22 In such cases, when a manufacturer
uses CDC’s specifications for specimen stability, additional data are likely not needed to support
validation. For additional or extended specimen stability conditions not supported by CDC’s
specifications, or in cases where CDC has not communicated specifications, the manufacturer
should validate their proposed specimen stability conditions in a specimen stability study, as
described in this section.

When such a specimen stability study is performed, the stability of specimens collected and
stored should be evaluated in real-world conditions.23 The study should include several time
points throughout the duration of the proposed storage time and at least one time point beyond
the proposed stability, as well as temperatures at the upper and lower limits of the proposed
temperature ranges for storage and transportation (e.g., 15°C and 30°C for extremes of
temperature ranges when stored at room temperature). When a test is intended to be performed
on the specimen immediately or shortly after obtaining the specimen, the specimen stability
testing timeframe should reflect a shorter storage time (e.g., 2 hours at room temperature).

The study should include contrived positive specimens prepared by spiking live or inactivated
pathogen into an individual or pooled negative clinical matrix around the LoD and a minimum of
10 negative specimens. If live or inactivated pathogen are not available, we recommend
manufacturers contact FDA to discuss potential options.

Specimen stability should be demonstrated for each proposed transport method (e.g.,
VTM/UTM, saline, and dry swabs). Specimen stability should be demonstrated for each atypical
specimen type included. Specimens from the same anatomic site but transported in different
ways are considered different specimen types. If a test is intended for use with multiple
commonly used specimen types, specimen stability could be demonstrated using the most
challenging intended specimen type (e.g., NP swabs for common upper respiratory types, sputum
for common lower respiratory types).

          (5) Usability, User Comprehension, and Flex Studies
For Point-of-Care (POC) home collection, and home use tests, a usability study should be
conducted to ensure lay users can complete all steps of the workflow in an actual or simulated
use environment.29 For tests that do not involve a healthcare provider, a user comprehension
study should also be conducted to assess risks associated with misinterpretation and misuse of
test results. This study should evaluate the lay user's understanding and comprehension of critical
elements and concepts in the labeling. 30 It may be possible to combine the usability study, user
comprehension study, and/or the clinical performance evaluation study since these all include
representative operators under intended use settings.

Flex studies are intended to demonstrate the robustness of a test, including the test’s ability to
maintain performance through environmental and usage variations under conditions of stress.
These studies should be designed to identify the maximum deviation in conditions reasonably
expected for POC or home use environments that will still generate accurate results (e.g.,
operating temperature). A hazard risk analysis should first be conducted to identify the most
common or likely sources of error based on the use locations and test procedures.31

As discussed earlier in this guidance, FDA recognizes that validation recommendations may be
adjusted based on circumstances at different stages of an outbreak, such as emergent public
health needs, availability of validation materials, and the important role of POC tests and home
testing options for infectious diseases. FDA generally will work interactively with a
manufacturer during the development and review of an EUA request to help ensure appropriate
validation of a test, particularly given potential changing circumstances of an outbreak.

          (6) Potential Additional Testing
In addition to the recommended analytical validation discussed throughout this guidance,
additional testing may be recommended in certain circumstances. Some examples are outlined
briefly here: Antigen tests should generally be evaluated for high-dose hook effect, which occurs
when the presence of very high levels of the target analyte in the patient specimen interferes with
the binding ability of the capture antibody and causes false negative results. If the clinical
evaluation does not use real clinical specimens, then cross-reactivity, microbial interference, and
endogenous/exogenous interference studies should generally be performed, typically with wet
testing using the most sensitive extraction/instrument combination and the Limit of Detection
(LoD) with the lowest analyte concentration. FDA may also find it appropriate to request studies
such as these in other circumstances where there may be a question or concern about accuracy or
reliability, such as when novel approaches are used or where there are signals of potential
impacts on test performance. Cross-reactivity should be evaluated to establish that the test does
not react with related non-target microorganisms, high prevalence disease causing agents, and
commensal or pathogenic flora that are likely to be in the clinical specimen. In addition,
microbial interference should be evaluated to establish test performance when the target analyte
is present in a clinical specimen with other relevant non-target microorganisms.
Endogenous/exogenous interference studies assess the effects of endogenous and exogenous
substances on test performance. Tests at risk for cross-contamination from high-positive
specimens adjacent to negative specimens (e.g., automated liquid handling systems) should
undergo carry-over testing to check for false positive results. Comparable performance between
fresh and frozen specimens should be demonstrated for tests intended for use, or otherwise
validated using, frozen specimens. For some tests with optional/additional
components/workflows (e.g., multiple thermocyclers, multiple extraction methods, different
collection media, extraction and/or PCR instruments), FDA may recommend an analytical
equivalency study to reduce the likelihood of additional clinical performance evaluation being
requested.

VI. Additional Considerations for Certain Test Types
               Multi­Analyte Panels
During an outbreak it may be beneficial to have a multi-analyte panel using one specimen to
detect and differentiate among pathogens that can cause similar symptoms. Multi-analyte tests
may alleviate the need for multiple samplings and may decrease use of medical supplies,
reducing pressure on supply chains during an emergency. Each analyte of a multi-analyte test
should be validatedVValidation Study Recommendations. Manufacturers should address the
potential for cross-reactivity and microbial interference (including competitive inhibition)
between analytes. Generally, the validation needed to support EUA issuance for multi-analyte
panels depends on several factors, including, but not limited to:
    · State of scientific knowledge for each pathogen,
    · Whether target analytes have been the subject of previously FDA
        cleared/approved/authorized tests,
    · Whether the test is a modification of a multi-analyte test previously FDA
        cleared/approved/authorized for other pathogens,
    · Test format [e.g., individual wells used to test for each target analyte or one single well
        used to test for all target analytes together (i.e., multiplex reaction)],
    · Specimen types the test will be used with, and
    · Disease prevalence/availability of clinical specimens.
               Home Specimen Collection and Home Use Tests
Home specimen collection and home use tests may be beneficial during an outbreak by
increasing patient access to testing and by reducing potential exposures. Home specimen
collection involves the use of a home collection kit to collect a specimen and send it to a
laboratory for testing. A home use test involves collecting a specimen and testing it at home,
without needing to send it anywhere else. FDA recommends that manufacturers of home
collection kits and home use tests consider incorporating design features to address user
accessibility (e.g., for vision or hearing deficits). Home collection kits and home use tests should
use only non-invasive specimen collection methods that require no specialized training to be
safely and correctly performed. The home collection kit or home use test should be safe for home
use and for lay-user collection of specimens from the intended anatomical site. Home collection
kits and home use tests should be assessed for toxicology. Home collection kits or home use tests
that contain hazardous materials may not be appropriate without adequate risk mitigations such
as safety features to limit the risk of patient exposure. Labeling should inform users of the risks
associated with using the device. Instructions for lay users should be written as Quick Reference
Instructions (QRI) limited to one to two pages, at no higher than a 7th grade reading level, and

Home collection kits can be paired with a single test or multiple tests. Validation of home

interpret. Home use tests can be prescription use or over the counter (OTC) and can be used in
non-laboratory settings, such as offices, sporting events, airports, schools, where a lay person
collects a sample, performs the test, and interprets test results themselves. The clinical
performance evaluation of home use tests should be conducted at U.S. sites representative of the
intended use setting (i.e., that mimic a home use environment) and with users who represent the
intended use population (e.g., including different socioeconomic and educational backgrounds
and range of ages). Generally, for OTC tests, the intended use patient population includes adults
(and older pediatric individuals) who can perform self-collection and testing, pediatric
individuals who may be able to self-collect and perform the test under supervision of an adult,
and younger pediatric individuals (and some adults) who need their specimen collected and
tested by an adult caregiver. Each patient population proposed should be evaluated following the
validation recommendations discussed elsewhere in this guidance. The entire workflow should
be performed by each individual participant including, as applicable, test registration, specimen
collection, testing, and results interpretation. Testing sites should be set up in a way that
precludes a user from seeing or hearing other users performing the test (e.g., in separate rooms).
Specimens collected for use with the comparator methods should be collected according to
comparator device labeling (e.g., by a health care provider).

               Point­of­Care (POC) Tests
Point-of-Care (POC) tests are tests intended for use in professional near patient settings, such as
hospitals, urgent care centers, and emergency rooms. POC tests can be beneficial during an
outbreak because they provide more immediate results compared to laboratory-based tests.

Clinical performance evaluation of POC emergency use tests that are intended for use in a CLIA
Certificate of Waiver setting should be conducted at one or two U.S. sites representative of
anticipated real-world settings, with four to six operators without laboratory training, and who
are representative of intended operators. For example, this may include: using the device in a
healthcare setting, such as at a hospital bedside, by non-laboratorian healthcare professionals; in
a non-traditional healthcare setting, such as at a school, by untrained users who are not
healthcare professionals; or in a temporary testing site setting, such as a tent set up at a non-
healthcare workplace, by users who have limited or no training or hands-on experience in
conducting laboratory testing. The clinical performance evaluation for POC tests should include
all steps in specimen collection and handling described in labeling, including addition of the
specimen to the specimen port/well of the test. Testing should be performed in real time after
specimen collection. Study operators should only rely on QRI and should receive no additional
training or intervention on how to use the device. The QRI should be written for untrained users
at no higher than a 7th grade reading level, limited to one to two pages, and include pictures and
diagrams to facilitate use.

In addition to the clinical performance evaluation described above in Section V.A, for POC tests,
performance around the LoD should be evaluated by the intended POC users with contrived
specimens in real clinical matrix. Testing should include 10 samples near the LoD (e.g., ≤ 3-fold
of the LoD), and 10 negative specimens per site. All contrived specimens should be blinded,
randomized, and tested as part of the normal workflow of the site by the intended POC users,
each of whom tests at least three positive samples near the LoD and three negative samples.
Results that do not match the expected result should be investigated. Testing should demonstrate
positive and negative agreement of at least 95%. If this is not achieved, the LoD should be re-
evaluated.
