12   I.      INTRODUCTION
13
14   This document is intended to provide recommendations to sponsors and clinical investigators
15   developing early-phase trials, which include first-in-human (FIH) protocols or protocols for
16   drugs and biologic products 2 with limited human experience, submitted under an Investigational
17   New Drug Application (IND) to the appropriate disease-specific division located within the
18   Center for Drug Evaluation and Research’s Office of Oncologic Diseases (CDER/OOD) 3 and the
19   Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research’s Office of Clinical Evaluation (CBER/OCE) 4.
20   This guidance is specific to solid tumors and does not address protocols for investigational drugs
21   intended to treat hematologic malignancies.
22
23   In general, FDA’s guidance documents do not establish legally enforceable responsibilities.
24   Instead, guidances describe the Agency’s current thinking on a topic and should be viewed only
25   as recommendations, unless specific regulatory or statutory requirements are cited. The use of
26   the word should in Agency guidances means that something is suggested or recommended, but
27   not required.
28

43   III.      RECOMMENDATIONS FOR EARLY-PHASE TRIALS
44
45             A. Eligibility Criteria
46
47   Unnecessarily restrictive eligibility criteria may slow patient accrual, limit patient access to
48   early-phase trials, and lead to trial results that have limited applicability in the patient population
49   that will ultimately use the drug. Sponsors should reference FDA eligibility criteria guidances for
50   trials enrolling patients with cancer for additional early-phase trial considerations to ensure
51   patient safety and to ensure that access to clinical trials is not unnecessarily restricted. Many of
52   these guidances are cited in this section.
53
54             1. Prior therapy 5
55
56   •      Eligibility criteria should include the requirement that patients have received available
57          therapy(ies) that offer the potential for cure, or such available therapy be administered to all
58          patients in the trial, where the investigational drug is added to such therapy (i.e., add-on
59          trial).
60   •      When conducting clinical trials in a non-curative setting, patients may be eligible for
61          inclusion in trials, including early-phase trials, regardless of whether they have received
62          available therapy in the non-curative setting.
63   •      Sponsors must ensure the elements of informed consent, as required by 21 CFR 50.25, are
64          addressed, including “a disclosure of appropriate alternative procedures or courses of
65          treatment, if any, that might be advantageous to the subject.” The informed consent must
66          clearly state whether other treatment options known to confer clinical benefit exist and must
67          include discussion of reasonably foreseeable risks and any potential benefits associated with
68          the administration of investigational drug. For disease settings in which there are available
69          therapies that have demonstrated prolongation in survival (i.e., an overall survival benefit),
70          these therapies should be explicitly stated in the informed consent document by name.

 72              2. Age
 73
 74   •      In general, protocols should not impose upper age limitations. If an upper age limitation is
 75          proposed, adequate justification should be provided. 6
 76   •      For FIH or dose escalation protocols that investigate drugs primarily intended to treat adult
 77          cancers, restricting enrollment to adult patients is appropriate. Moreover, FDA encourages
 78          the timely inclusion of pediatric patients with solid tumors that have no curative options in
 79          early-phase trials when compelling nonclinical data or early adult clinical data indicate
 80          evidence of activity.
 81              o When an oncology drug offers a prospect of direct clinical benefit to pediatric
 82                  patients, FDA recommends that sponsors prospectively plan for pediatric inclusion in
 83                  select early-phase studies once adequate data on dosing and safety in adults are
 84                  available to support safety and identification of the appropriate starting dosage(s) in
 85                  the pediatric population. 7
 86              o Absent development-specific safety concerns, given similarities in drug exposure
 87                  between adolescents and adults (based on similar body weight and metabolic
 88                  processes), adolescents may be enrolled concurrently with adult patients after some
 89                  initial adult pharmacokinetics (PK) and toxicity data are obtained.
 90              o Sponsors should consult with the relevant FDA review division to determine the type
 91                  and amount of adult data needed before enrolling pediatric patients. 8
 92   •      For certain FIH or dose escalation protocols that investigate drugs primarily intended to treat
 93          pediatric cancers, substantive nonclinical evidence of activity may, on its own, provide
 94          sufficient justification for enrolling pediatric patients without prior experience in adults; this
 95          is appropriate only when the risks to pediatric patients can be adequately mitigated. In such
 96          cases, FDA recommends that sponsors consult with the relevant review division to discuss
 97          the planned study before the protocol is finalized.
 98   •      In select cases, staggered enrollment of older children or adolescents before younger children
 99          may be appropriate to minimize the risk to pediatric patients.

106   investigational drug while maintaining adequate patient safety. Clinical judgement should be
107   exercised on whether it is appropriate to include patients with treated and stable metastases,

116            4. HIV, Hepatitis B, or Hepatitis C 10
117
118   Thoughtful consideration should be given to the potential inclusion of patients infected with
119   human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), hepatitis B virus (HBV), and hepatitis C virus (HCV) in
120   cancer clinical trials. Eligibility criteria that address requirements regarding relevant concurrent
121   antiviral and other therapies (e.g., antibiotic prophylaxis) and degree of immunocompetence in
122   patients with HIV, HBV, or HCV infections should be designed in a manner that is appropriate
123   for a given cancer, investigational drug (for instance taking into account immunosuppressive
124   potential, drug-drug interaction, etc.), and intended use population. Inclusion of these patients in
125   early phase cancer clinical trials allows for evaluation of safe and effective use of the product
126   across a broader patient population likely to use the drug in clinical practice.
127
128            5. Washout Periods 11
129
130   Unless scientifically justified or necessary to achieve the goals of the clinical investigation, FDA
131   generally does not recommend time-based wash-out and recovery periods. Eligibility after prior
132   therapy/intervention should be based on recovery from clinically significant adverse events from
133   the therapy or intervention preceding study enrollment.
134
135            6. Renal Function
136
137   •    Use a contemporary, widely accepted and clinically applicable equation to estimate renal
138        function and base inclusion criteria for renal function on the characteristics of the
139        investigational drug (e.g., molecular weight, mechanism of elimination) such as:
140           o For small molecules and antibody-drug conjugates (ADC), consider a creatinine
141                clearance (CLcr) or estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 60 mL/min or
142                greater; however, lower values may be acceptable if the disease or population
143                typically presents with renal insufficiency.
144           o For biological products with a molecular weight greater than 69 kDa, consider a CLcr
145                or eGFR of 30 mL/min or greater given that these larger molecules are less likely to
146                undergo significant renal excretion.

152           7. QT Interval 12
153
154   •    Include QTc interval exclusion criteria that are appropriate for the study population and the
155        investigational drug's potential for QT prolongation such as:
156            o If there is a risk of QT prolongation, consider excluding concomitant medications
157                with a risk of QT prolongation and patients with medical conditions that could
158                increase the risk of QT prolongation.
159            o For small molecules and ADCs, consider excluding patients with a QTc interval >
160                470 milliseconds. For small molecules with high potential for QTc prolongation
161                based on observed data, including results from nonclinical studies or known drug
162                class effects, consider excluding patients with QTc interval > 450 milliseconds. In
163                protocol amendments or subsequent trials, the exclusion criterion may be modified if
164                supported by available clinical data.
165            o For biological products, with the exception of ADCs, consider excluding patients
166                with QTc interval > 480 milliseconds given the lower risk of QTc interval
167                prolongation.
168   •    FDA does not recommend different QTc cutoffs based on sex, as available data show less
169        differences in baseline QTc between sexes in patients with cancer than in healthy
170        participants. 13
171
172           8. Additional Considerations for Autologous Cell and Gene Therapy Products
173
174   For autologous cell and gene therapy (CGT) products, Sponsors should specify eligibility criteria
175   to be confirmed at the time of lymphodepletion and product administration to account for
176   potential changes in the patient's condition during the manufacturing period. Additionally,
177   patients should not be eligible to begin lymphodepletion until it is known that the CGT product is
178   available. Refer to FDA guidance Considerations for the Development of CAR T Cell Products
179   (January 2024) for additional considerations.

182
183   Sponsors should monitor the FDA guidance document website 14 for other cancer clinical trial
184   eligibility guidances, such as Laboratory Values 15 and Performance Status 16, and for other FIH

189             B. Dose Escalation Trial Design
190
191             1. Study Objectives
192
193   •     For a FIH trial, the primary objective(s) should be assessments of safety and tolerability.
194   •     Other objectives can be secondary or exploratory and can include dose identification,
195         pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, immunogenicity (if applicable), biomarker analyses,
196         dose-exposure-response relationships, food effect/drug-drug interactions (if applicable), and
197         preliminary measures of activity (e.g., response rate).
198
199             2. Dose Escalation Methods and Procedures
200
201                     a. Dose Escalation Design
202
203    •     A rule-based or model-based design should be prespecified to guide the decisions regarding
204          the dosage of the investigational agent in subsequent cohorts to ensure adequate control of
205          the rate of excessive toxicity and the selection of a safe dose for further investigation.
206    •     For dose escalation designs using decision criteria based on data from a single dose level
207          (e.g., Bayesian Optimal Interval [BOIN] design), ensure that the upper boundary allowing a
208          dose retention decision is specified and does not lead to an excessive observed toxicity rate.
209    •     For dose escalation designs using decision rules based on data across all dose levels for
210          which a decision table cannot be pre-tabulated (e.g., Bayesian Logistic Regression Model
211          [BLRM]), a description of the data that support the assumptions for the model should be
212          provided and simulation studies should be conducted to evaluate the operating
213          characteristics of the design. The simulation studies should demonstrate a low probability of
214          selecting a dose with excessive toxicity and a low percentage of patients exposed to an
215          excessively toxic dose. The operating characteristics should be evaluated for robustness with
216          different parameter settings which violate the model assumptions.

218                  b. Accelerated Titration
219
220   For accelerated titration (e.g., single patient) dose-escalation cohorts:
221    • The acceptability of this approach depends on the safety margin proposed, the mechanism of
222        action of the product and any known data on products of the same class, and the dose
223        increments proposed between cohorts during titration.
224    • Single patient dose escalation cohorts may not be suitable for certain drugs depending on
225        their mechanism of action. For example, because tumor burden differs between patients, a
226        given dose that may be safe in one patient with lower tumor burden may cause considerable
227        toxicities at the same dose in patients with higher tumor burden.
228    • When an accelerated titration approach is acceptable, switch to a dose escalation design with
229        a larger cohort size (e.g., minimum 3 patients) following observation of any Grade 2 or
230        higher adverse event regardless of attribution, unless clearly related to underlying disease or
231        extraneous cause.
232
233                  c. Intra-patient dose escalation (IPDE)
234
235   •   IPDE should occur only if ≤ Grade 2 toxicity was observed during the previous treatment
236       cycle.
237   •   The decision to allow for IPDE should be made after considering the potential safety profile
238       of the investigational drug, including cumulative drug toxicity. The maximal intra-patient
239       dose escalation level should only be at the highest cleared dose level.
240   •   IPDE may not be suitable for certain drugs depending on their mechanism of action. For
241       example, because tumor burden differs between patients, a given dose that may be safe in
242       one patient with lower tumor burden may cause considerable toxicities at the same dose in
243       patients with higher tumor burden.
244
245                  d. Dose Increments
246
247   •   In general, the maximum dose increment for small molecules and ADCs should not exceed
248       100% and for biologics (with exception of ADCs) should not exceed half-log. Alternative
249       proposals should have adequate justification.
250   •   Dose increments of less than 100% (e.g., modified Fibonacci or 50%) for small molecules
251       and ADCs, or less than half-log for biologics (with exception of ADCs and T-cell engagers
252       [TCE]) may be warranted if the following situations occur:
253           o If at least two ≥ Grade 2 treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAE) or one dose-
254               limiting toxicity (DLT) are observed across the cohort with at least three patients
255               enrolled.
256           o If a single ≥ Grade 2 adverse event occurs in a single patient cohort. The protocol
257               should be modified to require expansion of single patient cohorts (e.g., minimum 3
258               patients per cohort) for any ≥ Grade 2 adverse event, regardless of attribution.
259   •   For TCE, dose increments should be limited to 100% or less for both step-up and full
260       treatment doses if one or more incidences of ≥ Grade 2 cytokine release syndrome (CRS)
261       during the DLT period and to 50% or less if two or more incidences of ≥ Grade 2 CRS or one
262       or more DLT is observed during the DLT period. Optimize the step-up dosing regimen (e.g.,
263       step-up dose(s) and dosing schedule) that can minimize the risk of ≥ Grade 3 CRS.

265                    e. Staggering Patient Enrollment
266
267   •    For first-in-human trials, investigational drug(s) may have certain serious expected toxicities
268        based on non-clinical studies, existing human data, or both on similar products. Depending
269        on the expected toxicities and their severity, dosing several patients simultaneously may
270        represent an unreasonable risk. To address this issue, sponsors may need to provide a plan to
271        stagger administering the product to successive patients to limit the number of patients who
272        might be exposed to risk within a cohort, followed by staggering between cohorts. The plan
273        to stagger patients should include:
274            o Justification showing that the interval is long enough to monitor for acute and
275                subacute events
276            o Consideration of the time course of adverse events that were observed in animal
277                studies and in previous human experience with related products
278            o Consideration of the expected duration of the drug’s activity
279
280                    f. Duration of dose delay prior to resumption of therapy
281
282   Any adverse event that requires the investigational drug to be held should return to Grade 1 or
283   baseline within 14 days before resuming therapy at the current dose or a reduced dose. Delays
284   longer than 14 days may be considered with justification (e.g., immunotherapies necessitating
285   corticosteroid tapering).

293           C. Dose-Limiting Toxicity Criteria
294
295   All adverse events that occur or worsen after one treatment dose should be considered at least
296   possibly related to the study drug regardless of investigator/sponsor/safety review committee
297   causality assessment, excluding toxicities or death clearly related to disease progression or
298   extraneous causes.
299
300   The DLT evaluation period (i.e., DLT window) should generally be at least one cycle (e.g., 21 or
301   28 days). For immunotherapy products, this DLT evaluation period may be reasonable for the
302   purposes of patient enrollment into escalating dose cohorts. However, for certain cellular
303   therapies with known toxicities that may occur after 28 days of patients’ receiving the product,
304   longer DLT observation period may be needed (e.g., 42 days). In addition, all patients should be
305   monitored for approximately 90 days after treatment discontinuation, or longer based on the

344              D. Early-Phase Studies with Dose Expansion Cohorts 19
345
346   •      To minimize the number of subjects exposed to a potentially ineffective drug or drug
347          associated with substantial toxicity, the statistical analysis plan for expansion cohorts in
348          early-phase studies or protocols should include a justification of the maximum sample size. It
349          should also include stopping rules based on observation of excessive toxicity or lack of
350          activity.
351              o For randomized cohorts used for dose optimization, the sample size should still be
352                  sufficient to characterize different dosages. 20
353              o Safety stopping rules should be included for the study overall and for expansion
354                  cohorts.
355              o An adaptive design (e.g., Simon two-stage design) may be considered to initiate
356                  efficacy cohorts using smaller initial sample size.
357   •      Protocols with dose expansion cohorts (e.g., backfill cohorts, expansion cohorts in specific
358          tumor types, protocols with Phase 1b or Phase 2 elements) should include dose modification
359          guidelines and other information that is relevant to maintaining patient safety. In cases where
360          there is insufficient information to ensure the safety of patients enrolling into these cohorts at
361          the time of IND submission, sponsors should consider restricting their early-phase IND
362          protocol to include only dose escalation, with consideration for adding expansion cohorts as
363          an amendment when more information is available.
364   •      As development proceeds and activity or safety signals are identified in a specific tumor,
365          sponsors should consider submitting protocols to a new IND to the division responsible for
366          reviewing that solid tumor indication. 21 This is preferred over expanding disease specific
367          cohorts under the existing IND.
368              o For example, an early-phase study may include exploratory cohorts across various
369                  tumor types. If activity and safety signals are identified in a specific tumor type

400
401              1. Patient Stopping Rules
402
403   In addition to trial stopping rules, protocols should include patient stopping rules based on
404   observed toxicity. For early-phase dose escalation trials, patients should generally discontinue
405   therapy when they experience a DLT. Sponsors may consider continuing patients on therapy at
406   reduced doses (e.g., lower than the dose at which the DLT occurred) with a strong rationale for

466           I. Administration Relative to Food 27
467
468   The recommended approach for early-phase trials is typically a modified fasted condition (such
469   as taking on an empty stomach, defined as at least 1 hour before and 2 hours after a meal). This
470   approach avoids confounding dose-finding assessments while maintaining patient safety and
471   tolerability. Alternative food intake instructions may be supported with adequate justifications,
472   such as anticipated gastrointestinal intolerability. If the investigational drug is to be administered
473   with food, protocols should provide adequate PK and safety justification and detailed meal
474   composition. In subsequent trials, food administration requirements may be modified based on
475   emerging pharmacokinetic and safety data, including results from preliminary or dedicated food
476   effect studies.

478           J. Sampling and Analysis Plans
479
480           1. Pharmacokinetic
481
482   Protocols should include pharmacokinetic (PK) sampling plans that adequately characterize the
483   PK profile of the investigational drug and support dosage selection for subsequent trials. The
484   sampling plan should clearly specify which analyte or analytes will be measured.
485
486   For early-phase trials, FDA recommends including intensive PK sampling following the first
487   dose and at steady state or after multiple doses. Additionally, it is recommended to include
488   sparse sampling throughout the study to support population pharmacokinetic modeling and
489   exposure-response analyses. The sampling plan should include samples around the anticipated
490   time of the maximum concentration, if applicable and extend sufficiently to characterize the
491   elimination phase, with the duration informed by the anticipated elimination half-life based on
492   nonclinical data or data from related compounds. In subsequent trials, PK sampling frequency
493   and timing may be adjusted based on emerging pharmacokinetic and safety data.
494
495   Protocols should include a comprehensive PK analysis plan that describes the analytical
496   approaches to be used. The analysis plan may include non-compartmental and population
497   pharmacokinetic modeling to characterize PK variability across the patient population and
498   identify covariates that may influence drug exposure, as well as exposure-response analyses to
499   evaluate relationships between drug exposure and both activity and safety outcomes.

514              3. Immunogenicity 29
515
516   For biological products, include immunogenicity sampling plans to assess immune responses
517   against the investigational product, such as the development of anti-drug antibodies (ADAs).
518   FDA recommends collecting immunogenicity samples pre-dose to minimize drug interference
519   with immunogenicity assays, as circulating drug can affect the sensitivity and accuracy of ADA
520   detection. For early-phase trials, the recommended sampling frequency includes collection at
521   each cycle for at least the first three cycles, followed by sampling every other cycle for three to
522   four cycles, then every four cycles thereafter. Consider collecting additional samples at the end
523   of treatment and at safety follow-up visits, if feasible, and an earlier timepoint for products with
524   a short elimination half-life to capture potential early onset of immunogenicity response. In
525   subsequent trials, immunogenicity sampling frequency may be adjusted based on emerging
526   pharmacokinetic and safety data.
