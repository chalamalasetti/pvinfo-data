 8   I.       INTRODUCTION
 9
10   This guidance is intended to assist sponsors in identifying an optimized dosage(s) 2
11   (administered activity and schedule) for radiopharmaceutical therapies (RPTs) 3 for oncology
12   indications during clinical development and prior to submitting a marketing application for a
13   new indication and usage.
14
15   This guidance should be considered along with the FDA guidance Optimizing the Dosage of
16   Human Prescription Drugs and Biological Products for the Treatment of Oncologic Diseases
17   (August 2024). 4 Some of the recommendations outlined in that guidance may be applicable to
18   RPTs; however, this guidance is more specific to RPTs.
19
20   This guidance does not address selection of the initial RPT administered activity in first-in-
21   human trials. For current FDA thinking regarding this topic, refer to the FDA guidance for
22   industry Oncology Therapeutic Radiopharmaceuticals: Nonclinical Studies and Labeling
23   Recommendations (August 2019).

       For the purpose of this guidance, dosage refers to the administered activity and schedule (i.e., the recommended
     interval between administrations and number of administrations/cycles) and administered activity refers to the
     radiation dose of the radiopharmaceutical. An optimized dosage is the administered activity and schedule that can
     maximize the benefit/risk profile or provide the desired therapeutic effect while minimizing toxicity.
     3
       For the purpose of this guidance, a radiopharmaceutical therapy, otherwise known as an oncology therapeutic
     radiopharmaceutical or RPT, is a radioactive drug or biological product that is used to treat cancer or palliate tumor-
     related symptoms (e.g., pain). In 21 CFR 310.3(n), such products are referred to as “radioactive drugs.”
     Recommendations in this guidance are applicable to products that are administered systemically and undergo alpha,
     beta, and/or gamma decay.

35   II.    BACKGROUND
36
37   RPTs, typically administered systemically to treat cancer, share characteristics with both external
38   beam radiation therapy (EBRT) (e.g., cell killing through ionizing radiation) and systemic drug
39   therapy (e.g., systemic biodistribution and pharmacologic targeting). Administered activities of
40   RPTs have historically been chosen to adhere to absorbed dose limits to critical organs derived
41   from prior experience with EBRT. However, differences in physical properties and treatment
42   delivery between RPTs and EBRT, such as dose rate and distribution of radiation, lessen the
43   applicability of EBRT-derived organ absorbed dose limits to RPTs. Therefore, the optimized
44   dosage of an RPT may be greater than or less than a dosage limited by EBRT organ tolerances.
45   Furthermore, radiation absorbed dose limits may differ between different isotopes or RPTs based
46   on their physical properties. Empiric determination of RPT-specific organ tolerances, or full
47   assessment of dose-response relationships for a specific RPT, may not be possible if the
48   evaluated dosages are limited based on EBRT organ tolerances.
49
50   A particular challenge for dose-escalation and dosage optimization of RPTs is that standard dose-
51   escalation designs include a finite dose-limiting toxicity (DLT) window and emphasize detecting
52   and limiting acute and subacute toxicities. However, RPTs have the potential to lead to persistent
53   long-term or delayed onset toxicities that depend on cumulative administered activity received
54   (e.g., renal toxicity, xerostomia, xerophthalmia, bone marrow failure). Long-term or delayed
55   toxicities are unlikely to be fully characterized within the DLT window of a standard dose-
56   escalation design, and therefore methods of overdose control in standard dose-escalation designs
57   are not sufficient to prevent cumulative overdosing. Furthermore, selecting dosages for
58   evaluation in subsequent larger trials without an adequate duration of safety follow-up may
59   expose increasing numbers of participants to unacceptable long-term toxicity. Long-term toxicity
60   may manifest through specific adverse reactions, inability to receive subsequent effective
61   therapies, and detrimental effects on clinical endpoints such as overall survival.
62
63   A limit on cumulative exposure is a standard approach for cancer treatments with long-term,
64   potentially irreversible toxicities that depend on cumulative dose, such as EBRT (e.g., various
65   normal organ toxicities that correlate with distribution and radiation absorbed dose, as described
66   by established normal tissue complication probability models), platinum chemotherapy (e.g.,
67   renal toxicity, peripheral neuropathy, and hearing loss), and anthracycline chemotherapy (e.g.,
68   cardiac toxicity). Administering RPTs for fixed durations that are supported by available
69   evidence, in addition to dosage optimization strategies such as those detailed below, may help

 70   obtain the additional data needed to define RPT-specific organ tolerances while managing the
 71   risk of long-term and/or cumulative toxicity.
 72
 73   The FDA guidance for industry Optimizing the Dosage of Human Prescription Drugs and
 74   Biological Products for the Treatment of Oncologic Diseases (August 2024) contrasts the
 75   historical dosage selection for cytotoxic chemotherapies, where the maximum tolerated dose
 76   (MTD) was identified in dose-finding trials and frequently administered in subsequent clinical
 77   trials, to that of modern targeted oncology drugs where dosages below the MTD may have
 78   similar efficacy to the MTD with fewer toxicities. RPTs share characteristics with both cytotoxic
 79   chemotherapies and modern targeted oncology drugs, and the MTD of many RPTs with respect
 80   to either acute or long-term toxicities has not been established at least in part due to prior
 81   adherence to EBRT organ tolerances. Therefore, it is not yet clear whether identifying an MTD
 82   (or dosing up to normal tissue tolerances) is the optimal approach for determining the
 83   recommended dosage for an RPT. Furthermore, the appropriateness of an MTD-based dosing
 84   strategy may be product- and situation-specific and could depend on numerous factors (e.g.,
 85   target expression, ligand specificity, physical properties of the isotope and construct [stability,
 86   half-life, and decay scheme]; and slopes of the dose-response and dose-toxicity relationships for
 87   a given participant population). Although determination of the MTD may provide useful
 88   information, dosages selected for trials intended to support a future marketing application should
 89   be based on a totality of available data from a range of dosages, and not based solely on the
 90   MTD or normal organ tolerances.

 93   III.       CONSIDERATIONS FOR DOSAGE OPTIMIZATION
 94
 95   Administered activities per cycle and/or cumulative RPT administered activities that result in
 96   tissue absorbed doses that exceed EBRT organ tolerances or previously characterized RPT
 97   dosages may be studied in clinical trials of RPTs when there is adequate rationale that the
 98   optimized dose of an RPT may not be identified at lower dosages (i.e., dosages that do not
 99   exceed EBRT organ tolerances or previously characterized RPT dosages). Proposals to expose
100   participants to RPT dosages that exceed EBRT organ tolerances or previously characterized RPT
101   dosages could be justified based on existing clinical data or other approaches, as applicable, and
102   should be discussed with FDA during formal meetings, including early in clinical development.
103   Trials should include safeguards such as appropriate participant selection, trial design, safety
104   monitoring, and radiation dosimetry evaluation.
105
106              A.     Participant Population
107
108          •   Trials that include an objective of defining the MTD of an RPT, or that include exceeding
109              EBRT-defined dose limits or previously characterized RPT dosages, should be conducted
110              in participants who have limited life expectancy due to cancer-related mortality and for
111              whom the potential for delayed permanent organ failure is considered an acceptable risk.
112              The exact participant population for which such studies are appropriate is disease-specific
113              and should be discussed with the appropriate FDA review division.
114

115   •   RPT dosages, and thus the corresponding organ absorbed doses delivered by RPT, in
116       participants at lower risk of morbidity and/or death from their disease (e.g., earlier stage
117       disease, diseases that may be curable, and diseases that have long natural history and
118       expected survival times) should not exceed dosages that have been characterized in
119       participants at greater risk for cancer-specific mortality (e.g., later stage
120       advanced/metastatic disease, diseases with short expected survival times) with an
121       adequate duration of follow-up. This is because participants with a lower risk of
122       morbidity and/or death may have an increased risk of experiencing long-term toxicity
123       from treatment compared to participants with more advanced disease. Dosages and
124       delivered organ absorbed doses lower than those associated with serious and irreversible
125       toxicities may be appropriate in earlier disease settings, and further dosage optimization
126       may be needed in these participant populations.
127
128   •   For participants treated previously with EBRT, eligibility for RPT trial enrollment should
129       be determined by clinical variables such as pre-existing baseline toxicities and organ
130       function. Receipt of prior EBRT should generally not be used to exclude participants
131       from participation in RPT trials, except when there is a specific clinical concern about co-
132       localization of toxicity from prior EBRT and RPTs.
133
134   •   For participants treated previously with RPT, eligibility for RPT trial enrollment should
135       be determined by clinical variables such as pre-existing baseline toxicities and organ
136       function, in addition to consideration of prior radiation absorbed doses. Sponsors should
137       prespecify a maximum cumulative radiation absorbed dose to critical organs from prior
138       and current RPTs that is based on available data. Sponsors should provide justification for
139       any proposal that exceeds these organ tolerances.
140
141   •   Sponsors should consider evaluating participants who have received prior RPT in
142       different cohorts from participants who have not received prior RPT, given that safety
143       considerations may differ between these populations.

146       B.     Trial Design
147
148   •   RPTs should generally be administered for a fixed number of cycles to mitigate the risk
149       of delayed or cumulative toxicity. Protocols should pre-specify a limit to the cumulative
150       administered activity, and corresponding radiation absorbed doses to critical organs, that
151       is justified based on available data on RPT-specific organ tolerances. The appropriate
152       limit may be product- and indication-specific, and the appropriate limits may be revised
153       based on emerging data. In situations where there is relatively high uncertainty (e.g., a
154       novel product with no clinical data, alpha-emitter RPTs, RPTs that are predicted to have
155       heterogeneous sub-organ distribution of radiation, or existence of uncertainties about
156       additive toxicity from prior RPT treatment), a lower limit to cumulative activity delivered
157       in a particular protocol may be appropriate as a starting point. In situations where there is
158       less uncertainty (e.g., established product with substantial clinical data, high-energy beta-
159       emitting isotopes, RPTs with relatively homogeneous sub-organ distribution of radiation,

160       participants with no prior RPT treatment), more flexibility in the upper limit may be
161       appropriate.
162
163   •   Consistent with the FDA guidance for industry Oncology Therapeutic
164       Radiopharmaceuticals: Nonclinical Studies and Labeling Recommendations (August
165       2019), the EBRT literature should be used as a starting point for the dosage of RPTs,
166       which means that the first dosage studied in humans (administered activity level per cycle
167       summed across the number of cycles that is planned for therapeutic purposes) should
168       generally not exceed EBRT limits.
169
170   •   In situations where initial clinical data suggest limited toxicity at the prespecified
171       maximum administered activity of a dose-escalation trial of RPTs, and preliminary
172       efficacy data (tumor absorbed dose data and/or clinical data) suggest the optimized
173       dosage has not been reached, sponsors should request a meeting with the appropriate
174       FDA division to discuss protocol amendments allowing further dose-escalation. Detailed
175       initial data, including safety, preliminary efficacy dosimetry, and pharmacokinetic data,
176       should be provided in the meeting package to justify the higher administered activities.
177
178   •   Dosages chosen for trials intended to support a future marketing application should be
179       based on all relevant data, including but not limited to clinical data regarding safety,
180       preliminary efficacy, pharmacokinetics and/or pharmacodynamics, and patient-reported
181       outcomes; the MTD (if established); estimated organ tolerances; and tumor and normal
182       organ radiation dosimetry.
183
184       Additional considerations for selection of the optimized dosage(s) for trials intended to
185       support a future marketing application include:
186
187          — While a DLT period of one cycle may be sufficient to allow escalation to a higher
188            dose in initial dose-finding trials, sponsors should incorporate longer-term safety
189            and tolerability data into their decision-making to justify the recommended
190            dosage(s) for subsequent trials. If multiple cycles of an RPT are intended, safety
191            data obtained with one cycle will not be sufficient to support a dosage for future
192            development; in this case, safety data from participants who received multiple
193            cycles and from multiple dose levels should be provided to assess potential
194            cumulative toxicity. The choice of a recommended dosage(s) for trials intended to
195            support a future marketing application should be informed by a duration of safety
196            follow-up that corresponds to the timeframe of the anticipated toxicities, and/or
197            the median life expectancy of the participant population (if less than the
198            timeframe of anticipated long-term toxicities).
199
200          — Dose-escalation cohorts may lack a sufficient number of participants to identify
201            dosages to be further explored. It may be useful to evaluate additional dose-level
202            cohorts or add more participants to existing dose-level cohorts (i.e., backfill
203            cohorts) in dose-finding trials to provide additional safety and activity data for
204            dosages which are being considered for further development and to increase the

206               the optimized dose for trials intended to support a future marketing application.

208            — Sponsors should compare multiple dosages, including in randomized dose-
209              response trials, prior to selecting a dosage(s) for trials intended to support a future
210              marketing application, given that the optimized dosage may or may not
211              correspond to the highest dose studied. Randomized dose-response trials may be
212              particularly important when evaluating a dosage(s) that exceeds EBRT-defined
213              organ tolerances, or a dosage(s) that corresponds to the MTD of an RPT.
214              Randomized dose-response trials should be sized to allow for adequate
215              assessment of activity, safety, and tolerability for each dosage. Refer to the FDA
216              guidance for industry Optimizing the Dosage of Human Prescription Drugs and
217              Biological Products for the Treatment of Oncologic Diseases (August 2024) for
218              general information on randomized parallel dose-response trials. The dosage(s)
219              selected for trials intended to support a future marketing application should be
220              based both on data from randomized dose-response trials, including preliminary
221              efficacy and shorter-term toxicity data, as well as longer-term toxicity data
222              available from the initial dose-finding trial(s), including any backfilled cohorts.
223
224   •   Sponsors should consider the cumulative administered activity received from any prior
225       RPT when choosing levels of administered activity to study for an investigational RPT.
226       Lower dosages (starting administered activity and maximum cumulative administered
227       activity) of the investigational RPT may be appropriate in participants who have received
228       prior RPT.
229
230   •   The informed consent process and documents for dose-finding trials of RPTs should
231       communicate appropriate information regarding the potential risks of long-term, late-
232       onset, and cumulative radiation toxicity and that such information may not be well-
233       characterized for a product during initial development stages.
234
235       C.      Safety Monitoring
236
237   •   Because the onset of radiation toxicity may be delayed by months to years, sponsors
238       should perform monitoring against a pre-specified list of late radiation adverse events of
239       special interest (rAESI) for at least 5 years after the last dose or until death in cases where
240       life expectancy is typically expected to be less than 5 years. The rAESI list and
241       monitoring schedule should explicitly reflect the drug-specific dosimetry results and a
242       corresponding detailed critical radiation safety analysis plan. RPTs granted approval for
243       cancer indications may be subject to a postmarketing requirement to provide such long-
244       term safety follow-up data for FDA review.
245
246   •   For participants who have received prior radiotherapy (EBRT and/or RPT), sponsors
247       should record the dose to critical organs from the previous radiotherapy to further inform
248       whether cumulative radiation absorbed dose from previous radiotherapy and the
249       investigational product could cause added toxicity where co-localized. Organ-specific
250       safety data for the participant should be correlated with their prior radiotherapy dose as

259       D.     Dosimetry for Dosage Optimization Trials
260
261   •   Per 21 CFR 312.23(a)(10)(ii), phase 1 studies of radioactive drugs must include studies
262       which will obtain sufficient data for dosimetry calculations. Each novel molecular entity
263       should have dosimetry studies performed early in its clinical development program to
264       provide measurements of potential toxicity/efficacy associated with exposure to radiation
265       (e.g., delivered organ absorbed dose and related dosimetric quantities). Dosimetry studies
266       provide a radiobiological context for the clinical efficacy and safety data and support
267       dosage optimization. Given that the properties (e.g., physical and effective half-life,
268       decay products and associated energies) of different radioisotopes vary widely, the
269       appropriate design of the dosimetry studies may be isotope- or product-specific. Direct
270       imaging of the therapeutic product is preferred, when possible, although this may be
271       supplemented by dosimetry data from surrogate analogs when direct imaging is not
272       possible or has substantial limitations.
273
274   •   If an Investigational New Drug (IND) submission is supported by data obtained from
275       studies that were not conducted under an IND and did not obtain dosimetry data, FDA
276       expects that dosimetry data will be obtained during the trial included in the original IND
277       submission to verify assumptions made in dosage selection. This could be accomplished
278       via a dedicated imaging study or a lead-in cohort. In these situations, sponsors should
279       request a meeting with the appropriate FDA division to discuss the dosimetry data prior
280       to enrolling a large number of participants in a trial intended to support a future
281       marketing application.
282
283   •   Sponsors should implement a direct imaging approach of alpha-emitters when possible.
284       Alternatively, sponsors may consider a combination of surrogate-imaging based
285       dosimetry and pharmacokinetic modeling. The preferred imaging approach may depend
286       on the specific alpha-emitter and current available imaging technology. Sponsors should
287       submit their proposed dosimetry approach and any supporting data for FDA review.
288       Sponsors should consider performing micro-scale dosimetry for alpha-emitters to account
289       for the short range and highly heterogenous dose deposition in order to obtain sub-organ
290       level absorbed dose estimates.
291
292   •   Sponsors should provide radiation dosimetry protocols that include sufficient description
293       of the imaging acquisition methods and image processing (e.g., reconstruction,
294       corrections, etc.) for dosimetry calculations. Radiation dosimetry protocols should
295       include detailed information of all steps taken to produce estimated organ absorbed dose
296       results: camera and administered activity calibration procedures, organ and tumor

308       •   In general, estimated organ absorbed doses should be accompanied by estimated
309           uncertainties accounting for all assumptions made in dosimetry calculations or other

311
312       •   Sponsors should provide, as applicable, justifications for dose-escalation portions of the
313           dose-finding trial, including estimates of upper limits for cumulative administered
314           activity. Estimates of upper limits may include radiation biological considerations,
315           including consideration of biological effective dose (BED) estimates corresponding to a
316           well-defined effect of clinical interest from study across a range of radiation delivery
317           methods. Sponsors should submit supporting data, including explanation of applicability
318           and modeling details and assumptions, for FDA review.
319
320       •   Regardless of the primary method used for estimating an upper limit for cumulative
321           administered activity, sponsors should consider including both absorbed dose and
322           derivative BED calculations when reporting dosimetry study results. BED-based dose-
323           response analysis may aid with aggregation and analysis across studies as part of ongoing
324           efforts to better define RPT-specific organ tolerances.
