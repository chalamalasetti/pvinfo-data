41   II.      BACKGROUND
42
43   FDA recognizes that drug shortages pose a significant public health threat because they can
44   delay, and in some cases even deny, critically needed care for patients. Recent publications have
45   explained that proactively assessing risks to drug manufacturing processes and supply chains,
46   coupled with an understanding of market vulnerabilities, have enabled stakeholders to support
47   robust manufacturing operations that help to maintain the availability of a high-quality drug
48   through the drug’s life cycle. 9 The publications further explain that proactive risk assessment
49   has also enabled some stakeholders to predict and prevent supply disruptions that could
50   potentially lead to a drug shortage. 10

52   In July 2012, with the enactment of the Food and Drug Administration Safety and Innovation
53   Act (FDASIA), Congress empowered FDA with tools to work in collaboration with industry to
54   help prevent or mitigate drug supply disruptions and drug shortages, and clarified current good
55   manufacturing practice requirements relevant to the oversight and controls over the manufacture
56   of drugs to ensure quality. 11 In 2011 and 2012, FDA assisted in mitigating 368 new drug
57   shortages and preventing 473 drug shortages. In contrast, in the 6-year span after FDASIA was
58   enacted, FDA assisted in mitigating 222 new drug shortages and preventing 806 drug
59   shortages. 12 Although the number of new drug shortages has declined significantly since the
60   peak in 2011, reaching a low in 2015 and 2016, this downward trend did not continue in
61   subsequent years. Drug shortages continue to occur and at roughly the same levels since 2018.
62   Further, drug shortages have grown more persistent (i.e., increased length of active drug
63   shortages). 13 Many of the reasons for drug shortages are, for example, issues related to drug
64   quality, disruptions to supply chain manufacturing operations (e.g., caused by natural disaster or
65   discontinuation of components by suppliers), limitations in forecasting future demand, and
66   market withdrawals of drug products. 14 Additionally, in recent years, FDA has observed a rise in
67   number of cyberattacks on drug manufacturers and is increasingly concerned about the effect of
68   such attacks on the drug supply chain. 15
69
70   In March 2020, with the enactment of the Coronavirus Aid, Relief, and Economic Security Act
71   (CARES Act), Congress added section 506C(j) to the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act
72   (FD&C Act), 16 which requires certain manufacturers to develop, maintain, and implement, as
73   appropriate, a “redundancy risk management plan that identifies and evaluates risks to the supply
74   of the drug, as applicable, for each establishment in which such drug or active pharmaceutical
75   ingredient of such drug is manufactured.” Section 506C(j) became effective on September 23,
76   2020 (see section III. in this guidance).

 78   FDA believes that its efforts in navigating drug shortage issues through the statutory and
 79   regulatory framework and its partnership with stakeholders have contributed to the reduction in
 80   the number of new drug shortages as well as a reduction in the time to resolve existing drug
 81   shortages. However, the Agency acknowledges that shortages and shortage mitigation efforts
 82   pose a significant financial and resource burden on stakeholders and FDA. FDA views RMPs as
 83   an important mechanism that stakeholders can use to proactively identify, assess, and mitigate
 84   the risks that might lead to a disruption in the supply of drug products, and thus preemptively
 85   reduce the financial and resource burden associated with resolving a shortage and problems that
 86   may lead to a shortage.
 87
 88   RMPs involve the systematic application of quality management policies, procedures, and
 89   practices to the tasks of assessing, controlling, communicating, and reviewing risk. These
 90   approaches and techniques are commonly used by multiple industries to manage a broad range of
 91   risks that have the potential to adversely affect their businesses. For the pharmaceutical industry,
 92   drug manufacturers use quality risk management, a systematic process for the assessment,
 93   control, communication, and review of risks to drug product quality, across the drug’s life cycle.
 94   Quality risk management is based on two principles: 17
 95
 96          (1) The assessment of the risk to quality should be based on scientific knowledge and
 97              ultimately link to the protection of the patient
 98
 99          (2) The level of effort, formality, and documentation of the quality risk management process
100              should be commensurate with the level of risk
101
102   The pharmaceutical industry has taken incremental steps to implement quality risk management
103   principles since the publication of ICH Q9 in 2006. As a result, the Agency believes that many
104   stakeholders have already established processes and procedures to proactively identify, assess,
105   and mitigate the effect of a broad spectrum of risks to their business processes using these risk
106   management principles. The Agency encourages the use of these approaches to proactively
107   manage the risk of supply disruptions that may lead to drug shortages.

110   III.      RISK MANAGEMENT PLANS: STAKEHOLDERS AND PRODUCTS
111
112   This section defines stakeholders in the manufacturing supply chain for drugs and describes
113   those products for which an RMP is required under section 506C(j) of the FD&C Act, as well as
114   those products for which an RMP is recommended.
115
116             A.       Stakeholders in the Manufacturing Supply Chain
117
118   The content of an RMP, including the steps needed to reduce risks of a disruption in drug supply,
119   may vary among the different stakeholders in the supply chain for a given drug product. For the
120   purposes of this guidance, stakeholders in the drug supply chain are categorized as follows.

235   IV.     RMP FRAMEWORK AND DEVELOPMENT STRATEGY
236
237   FDA recommends using the quality risk management process and principles described in ICH
238   Q9 as the framework to develop an effective RMP. This guidance is intended to add to the
239   elements of the framework described in ICH Q9, to describe considerations for stakeholder
240   oversight for RMPs, and to assist with the interpretation of the quality risk management process
241   within the context of developing RMPs.45 Further, stakeholders may benefit by integrating the
242   RMP with other aspects of their operations.
243
244           A.       Stakeholder RMP Development Strategy
245
246   RMPs help to ensure that risks with the potential to disrupt the drug manufacturing process and
247   the drug supply chain have been identified, assessed, and mitigated. An individual stakeholder’s
248   role in developing and implementing an RMP varies with that stakeholder’s level of involvement
249   with the drug manufacturing process or supply chain. Nonetheless, FDA believes that risk
250   management principles should be applied throughout the drug supply chain to help mitigate the
251   risk of drug shortages.
252
253   A primary stakeholder RMP should contain a broader strategy that establishes overarching
254   approaches to consistently identify, assess, and mitigate risk across the organization or a
255   subsector of the organization. This approach is consistent with institutionalized quality
256   management maturity 46 that results in understanding the risk of supply disruptions that may lead
257   to shortages across the supply chain, integrates redundancy into the supply chain, improves the
258   forecasting of demand changes at all stages of production, maintains sustainable compliance,
259   improves overall incentives between purchasers and manufacturers, and fosters collaboration
260   with regulators. FDA recommends that the primary stakeholder RMP also include plans to
261   repair the supply chain after a disruption, as appropriate. Further, FDA recommends that the
262   primary stakeholder initiate RMP development as early as possible in the drug product’s
263   regulatory life cycle.
264
265   The Agency recommends that the primary stakeholder share as much of its RMP as possible with
266   secondary and other stakeholders of the drug product to enable secondary and other stakeholders
267   to incorporate the broad strategies of the primary stakeholder’s RMP into their own plans and
268   also contextualize the risks identified in the primary stakeholder’s RMP, specifically for the
269   manufacturing facility. Sharing the primary stakeholder’s RMPs with secondary and other
