16   I. Introduction
17   Personalized medicine (also referred to as “precision medicine”) relies on the use of in
18   vitro diagnostic (IVD) devices1 to detect and measure biomarkers and other individual
19   characteristics of disease or other conditions with the goal of better directing patient
20   treatment. With the continued growth of personalized medicine, an increasing number of
21   clinical investigations of therapeutic products (also referred to here as therapeutic product
22   trials or studies) are using investigational IVDs to guide the management of subjects in
23   such investigations.2 In some cases this has led to the development of an in vitro
24   companion diagnostic device that is essential to the safe and effective use of the
25   therapeutic product, once approved.3 The information generated by the use of
26   investigational IVDs in therapeutic product trials may affect important aspects of
27   treatment for the enrolled subjects and, by doing so, directly influence the types of
28   therapeutic products or therapeutic management strategies the subjects may be exposed to
29   during the study. Therefore, use of an investigational IVD in a therapeutic product trial
30   may pose significant risk to subjects. FDA is concerned that sponsors (including sponsor-

55   II. Background
56   In contrast to other types of devices that act on or in a patient, IVDs are usually used on
57   samples that are removed from or originate in the patient’s body.5 Despite their use ex
58   vivo, IVDs may pose risk to patients, e.g., when they provide inaccurate test results that
59   misinform patients’ healthcare management decisions. This risk extends to the use of
60   investigational IVDs when their results are used to guide the management of subjects in
61   therapeutic product trials, e.g., to select or classify subjects, assign subjects to therapeutic
62   product arms or doses, or monitor responses to treatment. Such uses have become more
63   common given the increasing number of targeted therapeutic product development
64   programs and potential response biomarkers.

66   An investigational IVD is an IVD (i.e., a reagent, instrument, or system intended for use in
67   the diagnosis of disease or other conditions6) that is the object of an investigation,7 and thus
68   is subject to the IDE regulation (21 CFR Part 812). Compliance with the IDE regulation
69   allows the shipment of investigational IVDs and their use in investigations while at the same
70   time providing measures to protect the safety of study subjects. Under the IDE regulation, an
71   approved IDE is required for an investigational device unless it is exempted under 21 CFR
72   812.2(c). Investigational IVDs are often exempt under 21 CFR 812.2(c) from most of the
73   IDE regulation.
74
75   As specified in 21 CFR 812.40, the sponsor is responsible for ensuring IRB review and
76   approval of an investigation.8 Sponsor-investigators,9 who serve as both sponsors and
77   investigators10 in an investigation, also assume the responsibility for ensuring IRB review
78   and approval. Sponsors may fail to appropriately recognize and identify to their IRBs the
79   investigational status of IVDs used in therapeutic product trials. FDA is also concerned
80   that sponsors and/or IRBs may not adequately assess and/or describe the risks associated
81   with investigational IVDs. The following sections are intended to provide additional
82   information to sponsors and IRBs to clarify issues relating to the investigational status of
83   IVDs, risk assessment(s) that should be undertaken, and the requirements applicable to
84   investigational IVDs in 21 CFR Parts 50, 56, and 812.

110            B. IDE Regulation and Investigational IVD Risk in
111            Investigations
112   Investigational devices are subject to the IDE regulation. The regulatory requirements for
113   an investigational IVD are determined by the risk posed to subjects by use of that IVD.
114   The IDE regulation describes three categories of device studies: significant risk (SR)
115   studies, non-significant risk (NSR) studies, and exempt studies. It is important for
116   sponsors and IRBs to understand the differences between these categories and
117   appropriately evaluate the risks associated with the use of investigational IVDs in
118   therapeutic product trials. Each investigational IVD (including those that are legally
119   marketed for a different intended use and those that are legally marketed but significantly
120   modified) should be assessed to determine if it is SR, NSR, or exempt.
121   The intended use of an investigational IVD in a therapeutic product trial, which is needed
122   to determine if the IVD is SR, NSR, or exempt, depends upon how that IVD is
123   incorporated in the clinical protocol, including how test results will drive treatment
124   assignment or otherwise influence the clinical management of study subjects. When
125   describing the intended use of an investigational IVD in a study risk determination Q-
126   submission (see section III.H for additional information on submitting a study risk

140                   1) Significant Risk (SR) Devices
141   As defined in 21 CFR 812.3(m), a significant risk (SR) device means an investigational
142   device that:

143              (1) Is intended as an implant and presents a potential for serious risk to the health,
144              safety, or welfare of a subject;

145              (2) Is purported or represented to be for a use in supporting or sustaining human life
146              and presents a potential for serious risk to the health, safety, or welfare of a subject;

147              (3) Is for a use of substantial importance in diagnosing, curing, mitigating, or treating
148              disease, or otherwise preventing impairment of human health and presents a potential
149              for serious risk to the health, safety, or welfare of a subject; or

150              (4) Otherwise presents a potential for serious risk to the health, safety, or welfare of a
151              subject.

152   Sections 812.3(m)(3) and (4) are especially relevant to the use of investigational IVDs in
153   therapeutic product trials as described in this guidance. Incorrect test results can present
154   significant risk when they lead to misdiagnosis and/or mismanagement of subjects’ care. For
155   example, if the treatment is a drug that has significant toxicity and efficacy is expected only
156   in the marker-positive population, false positive test results may lead subjects without the
157   marker to be treated with the drug, and therefore risk experiencing serious adverse events
158   without any expectation of benefit. Note that under 21 CFR 812.3(m), the relevant
159   consideration is whether there is a potential for serious risk, but not the likelihood for serious
160   harm occurring. Invasive sampling16 (e.g., certain biopsies or sampling procedures) may also
161   introduce significant risk, even if the selection of therapy is not guided by results from the
162   investigational IVD.

167                2) Non-Significant Risk (NSR) Devices
168   FDA may consider certain non-exempt investigational IVDs in a therapeutic product trial to
169   present risks that are not considered significant (21 CFR 812.3(m)). In such cases, the
170   investigational IVD is considered to be non-significant risk (NSR).
171
172   If an investigational IVD in a therapeutic product trial is considered to be NSR, the trial is
173   considered to have an approved IDE, i.e., the sponsor does not need to submit an IDE
174   application and obtain FDA approval before starting the trial, if the sponsor complies with
175   the abbreviated IDE requirements described in 21 CFR 812.2(b), including obtaining IRB
176   approval of the trial and complying with the informed consent requirements under 21 CFR
177   part 50.17
178
179   There is no requirement to inform the relevant FDA device review Center (whether the
180   Center for Devices and Radiological Health (CDRH) or the Center for Biologics Evaluation
181   and Research (CBER)) of NSR studies, even when the device is used in a therapeutic product
182   study.18 When the sponsor determines that an investigational IVD used in a therapeutic
183   product trial is NSR and submits the investigation to the IRB, the IRB provides initial
184   review, approval if appropriate, and continuing review of the trial (see Section III.D of this
185   guidance). If information becomes available that the investigational IVD represents an
186   unreasonable risk to the safety of the subjects (for example, the information indicates that
187   significant harm has been incurred as a result of using an investigational IVD that was
188   considered NSR in a therapeutic product trial, even if the IRB concurred with the sponsor
189   that the device in question was NSR), FDA may place a clinical hold on the trial,19 and may
190   require submission and approval of an IDE prior to continuation of the trial (21 CFR
191   812.20(a)).

193                3) IDE Exempt Investigations
194   Trials involving investigational IVDs are exempt from most requirements found under the
195   IDE regulation if they meet the criteria outlined in 21 CFR 812.2(c).20 These trials are
196   considered “exempted investigations.” Most pertinent to this guidance, to be an exempted
197   investigation under 21 CFR 812.2(c)(3), the investigational IVD used in the study must be
198   labeled in accordance with 21 CFR 809.10(c) and the testing (i) must be noninvasive, (ii)
199   must not require an invasive sampling procedure that presents significant risk, (iii) must not
200   by design or intention introduce energy into a subject, and (iv) must not be used as a
201   diagnostic procedure without confirmation of the diagnosis by another, medically established
202   diagnostic product or procedure.21 Under 21 CFR 809.10(c)(2)(ii), for such a device, when
203   being shipped or delivered for product testing prior to full commercial marketing, all labeling
204   must bear the following statement, prominently placed: "For Investigational Use Only. The
205   performance characteristics of this product have not been established."
206
207   Even if a device investigation is exempt under 21 CFR 812.2(c) from most requirements of
208   the IDE regulation, such studies are subject to applicable requirements under 21 CFR
209   812.119 (disqualification of a clinical investigator), 21 CFR Part 50 (informed consent) and
210   21 CFR Part 56 (IRB).22,23 For more information on exempted investigational IVD
211   investigations, see the guidance entitled “In Vitro Diagnostic Device Studies – Frequently
212   Asked Questions”
213   (https://www.fda.gov/downloads/MedicalDevices/DeviceRegulationandGuidance/Guidance

215           C. Evaluating Whether an Investigational IVD Used in a
216           Clinical Investigation of a Therapeutic Product is
217           Significant Risk
218   Because the regulatory requirements for use of a non-exempt investigational IVD differ
219   based on the risk of its use, sponsors should assess IVD risk during the planning phase of a
220   clinical investigation. In large part, the risk will depend on the clinical consequences of
221   erroneous or inaccurate results from the IVD (e.g., false positives or false negatives). Risk
222   determinations should take into consideration factors that may be unique to each trial and the
223   investigational therapeutic product and investigational IVD used.

225   The sections below present questions to help sponsors and IRBs evaluate critical factors
226   when making risk determinations for investigational IVDs in therapeutic product trials and
227   describe how clinical trial designs generally affect the risk of investigational IVD use.
228
229              1) Factors to Consider in Making a Risk Determination
230   This section presents key questions that sponsors and IRBs should consider when assessing
231   the risk of investigational IVD use in a therapeutic product study. These questions are
232   focused on considerations regarding how results from the IVD will be used, the potential
233   consequences of such use, and a number of other factors pertaining to the specific
234   circumstances of the study. Answers to these questions will assist sponsors and IRBs in
235   assessing whether the trial involves a significant risk device as defined in 21 CFR 812.3(m).
236                         a. Will use of the results from an investigational IVD lead to some
237                         study subjects foregoing or delaying a treatment that is known to
238                         be effective?
239
240               When the result from an investigational IVD directs subjects to an
241               investigational therapeutic product, the availability of alternative therapies and
242               the nature of those alternatives may influence the level of risk to the subject from
243               an erroneous result. For example, in an investigation for a population that has no
244               other treatment options, has exhausted all other treatment options, or for which
245               standard of care provides only marginal benefit, the potential harm caused by an
246               erroneous result from the investigational IVD use may be lower, because the
247               investigational therapeutic product may not present greater risks than the
248               alternatives available to the subject. Likewise, if treatment with the
249               investigational therapeutic product will not interfere with the likely effectiveness
250               of later treatment with a known effective therapy (i.e., a delay in receiving the
251               known effective therapy will not irreversibly degrade the condition of the
252               subject), the risk of an erroneous investigational IVD result may be lower. If,
253               however, the standard of care or other alternatives are reasonably effective, or a
254               delay in receiving known effective therapy would irreversibly degrade the
255               condition of the subject, then the potential harm caused by an erroneous
256               investigational IVD result may be greater.
257
258                         b. Will use of the results from an investigational IVD expose study
259                         subjects to safety risks (e.g., adverse events from the
260                         investigational therapeutic product) that exceed the risks
261                         encountered with the control arm therapy or non-trial standard of
262                         care?
263
264               The risk(s) of the investigational therapeutic product should be considered in
265               assessing the risk of investigational IVD use. When a drug has minimal side
266               effects, for instance, the risk associated with use of the investigational IVD for
267               enrollment or assignment to treatment arms will generally be lower because an

269               study of an investigational therapeutic product with significant toxicity, the risk

274                         c. Is it likely, based on existing knowledge about the relationship

276                         product, that incorrect results from the investigational IVD would

278
279               Existing knowledge about the relationship of the result obtained from the
280               investigational IVD and the potential safety and efficacy of the investigational
281               therapeutic product should be considered independently of the answers to
282               questions posed in sections III.C.1.a and b. For instance, if there is strong (e.g.,
283               clinical) evidence that an investigational therapeutic product with serious side
284               effects may be effective only in a marker-positive population, then an
285               investigational IVD used to identify marker-positive subjects is likely to be of
286               higher risk, regardless of the relative safety and efficacy of the standard of care
287               or alternative therapies.
288
289               A determination that the investigational therapeutic product should be restricted
290               to a test-defined population may rely on preclinical studies. However, evidence
291               needed to define a biomarker-positive or –negative population is oftentimes
292               obtained during early clinical trials or in the course of an ongoing trial. For this
293               reason, an investigational IVD that is initially NSR (e.g., used for stratification)
294               could become SR in a later phase investigation of the same therapeutic product,
295               or even in the middle of a particular trial. Surveillance and changing risk is
296               discussed in section III.C.3 below.
297
298                         d. Does use of the investigational IVD require invasive sampling
299                         that is not part of standard of care?
300
301               If invasive sampling is used to collect samples for testing with the investigational
302               IVD, and that sampling is not part of the standard of care (for example, if extra
303               biopsies of a tumor or an additional surgical procedure to obtain a specimen for
304               testing is required), then the IVD may be SR, regardless of the risks associated
305               with erroneous results.

307              2) Investigational IVD Risk and the Design of Clinical
308                 Investigations for Therapeutic Products
309   This section describes some intended uses for investigational IVDs in the context of some
310   commonly used therapeutic product trial designs. To assess risk, it is important to understand
311   how the intended use of the investigational IVD affects trial subjects’ medical treatment.

312
313   Note that the risks associated with the trial designs and other uses described in the examples
314   below are presented without reference to other information that may affect the overall risk
315   from an erroneous result. As discussed in the previous section (section III.C.1), predicted
316   side effects of the investigational therapeutic product and other factors should also be taken
317   into account when making a final risk determination.
318
319      Study enrollment. Investigational IVDs can be used to identify subjects for enrollment
320       eligibility. The goal is usually to identify for inclusion those subjects most likely to
321       benefit from therapy (efficacy), or to identify for exclusion those subjects most likely to
322       suffer toxicity without significant benefit. Misclassification of subjects due to erroneous
323       investigational IVD results can lead to treatment that might unnecessarily expose them to
324       toxicities or suboptimal treatment.
325
326      Predicting serious adverse reactions. An investigational IVD can be used to identify
327       study subjects likely to be at increased risk for serious adverse reactions as a result of
328       treatment with an investigational therapeutic product, and who, thus, should be subjected
329       to additional or different monitoring or therapeutic procedures. Similarly, the
330       investigational IVD may be used to identify subjects at decreased risk for adverse
331       reactions, and thus results from that IVD may justify reduced monitoring. This use of the
332       IVD, given its role in predicting risk for serious adverse reactions to the investigational
333       therapeutic product, is potentially of higher risk due to the probable harm that may be
334       incurred from acting on incorrect test results. The potential use of an investigational IVD
335       to predict the risk of serious adverse reactions may be identified while the trial of an
336       investigational therapeutic product is already underway. This may lead to a change in the
337       use of the investigational IVD and its associated risk from lower risk at the start of the
338       trial to higher risk during the course of the trial (see section III.C.3 below).
339
340      Dosing. Safety and efficacy of a therapeutic product is often closely related to the dose
341       administered if that product has a narrow therapeutic window and/or has harmful effects
342       outside of the efficacy window. When over- or under-dosing may pose serious risks and
343       an investigational IVD is used to determine the dose a study subject should receive, that
344       use is likely to be of higher risk.
345
346      Monitoring. An investigational IVD generally has higher risk when it is used to
347       determine response to an investigational therapeutic product for the purpose of adjusting
348       that treatment (e.g., schedule, dose, discontinuation) to maintain an appropriate
349       therapeutic or safety margin.
350
351      Assigning subjects to study arms. An investigational IVD can be used to assign
352       enrolled subjects to a particular study arm. The risks associated with assignment depend
353       on the study design (some examples noted below). However, if a study design involves a
354       combination of different trial designs, the investigational IVD will be of higher risk if its
355       use is of high risk within any particular component of the trial.
356

357           o     A stratification study design utilizes an investigational IVD result to classify
358                 subjects into test-positive or test-negative subgroups, followed by
359                 randomization to the investigational therapeutic product or control group within
360                 each of the subgroups. When each subject has an equal chance of being in the
361                 investigational therapeutic product or control group, the investigational IVD is

364                 particular treatment, but is not the sole determinant. In such cases, the degree of
365                 risk related to investigational IVD use will depend on the degree to which the

374                 IDE application will be required for continued use of the investigational IVD in
375                 the trial (see section III.C.3 below).
376
377       Retrospective studies. Retrospective studies involve the analysis of specimens after
378        subjects are enrolled in the trial or the trial is complete. In most cases, if the
379        investigational IVD result does not influence treatment, that IVD would be considered
380        lower risk and may be exempt from most IDE regulation requirements if the criteria in 21

382        where specimens are collected expressly for the purpose of retrospective analysis, would
383        carry the risk associated with specimen collection occurring outside of standard patient
384        care.

391               3) How Investigational IVD Risk May Change During the Course
392                  of a Clinical Investigation
393   When an investigational IVD is NSR at the beginning of a clinical investigation but becomes
394   SR at a later stage, or when a SR investigational IVD is introduced into a clinical
395   investigation, FDA approval of an IDE application, in accordance with 21 CFR 812.20(a)(2)
396   and 21 CFR 812.30(a), is required prior to use of the SR investigational IVD in the clinical
397   investigation. There are several ways in which an investigational IVD may be introduced into
398   a clinical investigation or an investigational IVD’s use may change during the course of a
399   clinical trial.
400
401          Safety and outcomes data, and other information gathered during the trial may change
402           the risk of the investigational IVD. For example, if results from early subjects in the
403           trial indicate more severe side effects or less improvement in a biomarker-positive
404           population than expected, the assessment of risk from use of an investigational IVD
405           to detect that biomarker may change.
406
407          Adaptive clinical trial designs that incorporate pre-planned and conditional alterations
408           in trial conduct may change the use of an investigational IVD as the trial progresses.
409           Risk posed by altered use of the IVD should be considered.
410
411          A new or amended study protocol for an existing investigational new drug application
412           (IND) may introduce or alter the risk of an investigational IVD. For instance, a Phase
413           1 trial may not include an IVD or it may include an IDE exempt or a NSR IVD.
414           However, a later Phase 2 trial conducted under the same IND may add a SR device or
415           may change the use of an investigational IVD in such a way as to make the IVD SR.
416           In such a case, the sponsor would be required to submit an IDE application to FDA in
417           accordance with 21 CFR 812.20; the investigational IVD could not be used in the
418           Phase 2 trial until FDA approves the IDE application (21 CFR 812.20(a)(2) and
419           812.30(a)).
420
421   Ongoing surveillance during a clinical investigation is recommended to monitor the risk of
422   the investigational IVD. For a SR investigation, the timing of an IDE application is at the
423   sponsor’s discretion and should be planned to allow sufficient time for FDA review before
424   the use of the SR investigational IVD in the clinical investigation. FDA recommends
425   interacting with the applicable Center (CDRH or CBER) via the Q-Submission process (see
426   section III.H) prior to initiating the first stage of the trial. This interaction may facilitate FDA
427   review of a future IDE application if required and may provide the sponsor with greater
428   predictability in regard to the IDE process.

776           B.       FDA Review of IDE Applications
777   Sponsors should submit their signed IDE application together with accompanying materials
778   to CDRH’s or CBER’s Document Control Center (DCC) in accordance with the guidance
779   entitled “eCopy Program for Medical Device Submissions”
780   (http://www.fda.gov/downloads/medicaldevices/deviceregulationandguidance/guidancedocu
781   ments/ucm313794.pdf). Upon receipt of an IDE application, sponsors are notified in writing
782   of the date that FDA received the original application and the IDE number assigned. The 30
783   day review clock for the IDE application begins on the date stated in the acknowledgement
784   letter to the sponsor.
785   Each IDE application received in CDRH’s DCC or CBER’s DCC is routed to the appropriate
786   Division, and assigned to a reviewer or review team. The IDE application is first screened for
787   required content. If the IDE application contains adequate information for review, the review
788   team then reviews the IDE application within 30 days of receipt. FDA will inform the
789   sponsor of its decision, or notify the sponsor that the investigation may not begin, within 30
790   days from the date of receipt of the IDE application.39
791
792   If an IDE application is approved or approved with conditions, the sponsor may begin subject
793   enrollment, up to the number of subjects and investigational sites specified in FDA’s decision
794   letter, once allowed to proceed under IND, if applicable, and upon receipt of IRB approval,
795   which may occur prior to FDA approval. If FDA does not have outstanding issues that the
796   sponsor needs to address to support the study of the subject cohort under the proposed
797   investigational plan, the IDE will be approved without conditions. Alternatively, if FDA has
798   identified issues that need to be addressed in a timely manner but do not preclude initiation of
799   subject enrollment in the clinical investigation, the IDE will be approved with conditions. In
800   the case of approval with conditions, approval is granted and study enrollment may begin
801   immediately on the condition that, within 45 days from the date of FDA’s decision letter, the
802   sponsor submits information addressing the issues identified in FDA’s letter.
803
804   In certain instances, resolution of outstanding issues may be necessary before initiation of
805   subject enrollment. In these instances, the IDE will be disapproved in accordance with 21
806   CFR 812.30, meaning that the sponsor may not initiate subject enrollment in the
807   investigation until the sponsor addresses the issues identified in FDA’s letter and receives an
808   approval or approval with conditions letter. In cases of disapproval of the IDE, a sponsor has
809   the opportunity to respond to the deficiencies by submitting an amendment or, in accordance
810   with 21 CFR 812.30(c)(1), to request a regulatory hearing under 21 CFR Part 16.

814   (http://www.fda.gov/downloads/MedicalDevices/DeviceRegulationandGuidance/GuidanceD
