14   I.       Introduction
15   This draft guidance provides recommendations for premarket submissions for certain robotically-
16   assisted surgical devices (RASDs). RASDs are teleoperated, software-controlled systems that
17   integrate robotic technologies and subassemblies that are designed to assist qualified
18   practitioners in precisely positioning and controlling one or more surgical instruments to safely
19   and effectively perform open, minimally invasive, or endoluminal procedures. RASDs are used
20   under direct control or supervision of a qualified practitioner. RASDs utilize robotic technologies
21   comprised of a leader/follower control system architecture and may contain some level of
22   automated functions to enhance and augment user capabilities.
23
24   RASDs generally have the following three subassemblies: 1
25
26        •   An operator console (i.e., surgeon console), where a qualified practitioner controls the
27            system. The operator console is the control center of the system and allows a qualified
28            practitioner to view the surgical field and to control movement of the surgical instruments
29            and cameras (e.g., laparoscope);

58   II. Scope
59   The scope of this document includes premarket submissions for RASDs except as follows:
60
61   Note that pre-operative planning systems and stereotaxic systems are not RASDs and are outside
62   the scope of this document. Examples of pre-operative planning and stereotaxic systems include
63   systems that consist of a computer user interface, tracking sensor, navigated instruments, and a
64   patient reference to detect the 3D position of the navigated instruments and patient reference in
65   relation to patient’s anatomical landmarks (e.g., orthopedic stereotaxic instruments [product code
66   OLO], neurological stereotaxic instruments [product code HAW], and ear, nose, and throat
67   stereotaxic instruments [product code PGW] under 21 CFR 882.4560).

 89   III. Glossary
 90   The following terms are defined for the purposes of this guidance:
 91   Autonomy – The ability of a robotic device system to perform tasks and make decisions based
 92   on current state and sensing without user intervention.
 93   Bedside/Patient Subassembly – A subassembly of the RASD with a sterile-nonsterile interface
 94   that has working arms that control, activate, or actuate surgical instruments, cameras (e.g.,
 95   laparoscope), accessories, and/or third-party devices inside the patient used to assist in
 96   performing surgical procedures. The bedside/patient subassembly typically has controls,
 97   connections, attachments, and safety features accessible to a scrubbed surgical assistant to allow,
 98   for example, aseptic surgical instrument exchange or aseptic movement of the working arm.
 99   Covered Procedure – A surgical procedure in the same surgical specialty (e.g., general surgery)
100   as the umbrella procedure, but one that is of lesser complexity and risk than its umbrella
101   procedure. Covered procedures leverage data from the cited umbrella procedure, and additional
102   in vivo performance testing data for a covered procedure may not be needed to support the
103   authorization of the RASD for that use.
104   Haptics – Any feature that provides some form of tactile, force, or other sensory feedback to the
105   user during the operation and that is intended to mimic or simulate the feel of manual surgical
106   instruments.
107   Local Teleoperation – A locally teleoperated system consists of an operator console that is co-
108   located in the same operating room as the bedside/patient subassembly. Using the operator


                                                                                                        7

109   console, a qualified practitioner can remotely control the bedside/patient subassembly by wired
110   or wireless signal.
111   Operating Room (OR) Staff Subassembly – A subassembly of the RASD that contains
112   supporting hardware and software components, such as an electrical surgical unit (ESU),
113   suction/irrigation pumps, a secondary vision display, an insufflation unit, and light source for the
114   endoscope.
115   Operator Console – A subassembly of the RASD where a qualified practitioner controls the
116   RASD. The operator console allows the qualified practitioner to view the surgical field and to
117   control movement of the surgical instruments and cameras (e.g., laparoscope).
118   Robotically-assisted Surgical Device (RASD) – A teleoperated, software-controlled system that
119   integrates robotic technologies and subassemblies that are designed to assist qualified
120   practitioners in precisely positioning and controlling one or more surgical instruments to safely
121   and effectively perform open, minimally invasive, or endoluminal procedures. RASDs are used
122   under direct control or supervision of a qualified practitioner. RASDs utilize robotic technologies
123   comprised of a leader/follower control system architecture and may contain some level of
124   automated functions to enhance and augment user capabilities.
125   Remote Teleoperation – A remotely teleoperated system is comprised of an operator console
126   that is not co-located in the same operating room as the bedside/patient subassembly. Remote
127   teleoperation systems may have variable distances between the operator console and the
128   bedside/patient subassembly.
129   Service Life – The time period specified by the manufacturer during which the RASD is
130   expected to perform as intended.
131   Singularity – A configuration of a robotic manipulator in which the kinematic Jacobian matrix
132   loses rank, resulting in the loss of one or more degrees of freedom in the end-effector’s motion
133   and limiting the robot’s ability to move or exert forces in certain directions.
134   Umbrella Procedure – A surgical procedure of sufficient complexity and risk such that
135   performance data derived from the umbrella procedure may be leveraged to support the use and
136   authorization of the RASD for covered procedures (i.e., procedures of lesser complexity and
137   risk).
138   Use Life – The pre-defined duration of active use or number of repeated uses during which
139   instruments, accessories and compatible devices are expected to perform as intended.

273          D.      RASD-Specific Non-Clinical Performance Testing
274   Significance: Well-designed non-clinical performance testing should be used for design
275   verification and validation prior to in vivo testing. Non-clinical performance testing for RASDs
276   should comprehensively evaluate the system-level performance characteristics that are critical to
277   safe and effective device operation. These characteristics typically include teleoperation
278   functionality, system control behavior, kinematic and dynamic motion performance,
279   visualization and display capabilities, additional RASD-specific features, and system and
280   surgical instrument reliability.
281
282   Recommendation: Non-clinical performance testing activities may include in vitro, in silico, ex
283   vivo, cadaver, mechanical, electrical, thermal, radiation, and functional testing. Where possible,
284   tests may be combined to reduce burden.
285
286   Non-clinical performance testing should test all functional components and compatibility of the
287   RASD; therefore, we recommend that you perform testing for each subassembly, and for the
288   system as a whole (including all the major components, surgical instruments, and cameras), and
289   the system when used with accessories and compatible devices.
290
291   You should provide full test reports for each test conducted. A full test report includes the
292   objective of the test, a description of the test methods and procedures, sample size, pre-defined
293   pass/fail criteria with relevant clinical justification (e.g., literature) for the set criteria,
294   justification for the specific surgical instrument tested, test results, and conclusions. For 510(k)
295   submissions, you should also provide an explanation of how the data generated from the test
296   support a finding of SE to the predicate device, as appropriate. For information on recommended
297   content and format of test reports for non-clinical performance testing in premarket submissions,
298   refer to FDA’s guidance, “Recommended Content and Format of Non-Clinical Bench
299   Performance Testing Information in Premarket Submissions.”
300
301   Non-clinical performance test conditions should simulate the worst-case conditions that your
302   device is likely to encounter during clinical use. When designing your device-specific
303   performance testing, you should consider the use(s) being sought for authorization and the
304   device’s design specifications. Bench performance testing should be representative of typical
305   clinical use to demonstrate full device functionality and verify design inputs and outputs.
306   Cadaveric studies may be useful for evaluating system fit and function within the human
307   anatomy.
308

310   and for the entire system. This list may not be comprehensive, as your RASD may include
311   additional features and functions not specifically addressed in this guidance.

313                  (1)     System Teleoperation
314   Significance: RASD is a leader-follower, teleoperated system that is controlled by wired
315   electrical signals over cables or wireless transmission. Timely and accurate information
316   exchange between the user interface and working arms/surgical instruments is essential for safe
317   device performance.
318
319   Recommendation: Factors contributing to teleoperation performance include signal transmission
320   integrity, system latency, and accurate translation of user input into scaled and filtered
321   instrument motion. Your performance testing should demonstrate connection integrity and timely
322   control signal transmission. RASDs should have fail-safe operation in the event of teleoperation
323   faults (e.g., cable disconnection, electromagnetic interference). If wireless signal transmission is
324   implemented for user interface controls, please refer to additional recommendations in Section
325   IV.G on Wireless Technology.
326
327   As applicable, you should design and conduct performance testing for each of the following
328   system teleoperation characteristics using representative surgical configurations consistent with
329   the intended use. This list may not be comprehensive, as your RASD may include additional
330   teleoperation features or functions not specifically addressed in this guidance.
331
332                          a)     Total System Latency
333   Significance: Total system latency refers to the cumulative delay from the surgeon’s hand input
334   at the operator console to instrument motion, and from instrument motion to the corresponding
335   visual feedback displayed on the main visualization and display system. Latency testing ensures
336   there is no significant time delay between the hand-input device, the instrument response, and the
337   visual feedback from the camera system. Latency is critical for RASDs because it directly affects
338   the operators’ ability to perform precise, safe movements in real-time.
339
340   Recommendation: Latency testing should demonstrate that the total system latency between the
341   hand-input device, instrument motion, and displayed visual feedback remains within pre-defined,
342   clinically justified limits. It is recommended that latency be measured under representative and
343   worst-case operating conditions, including maximum processing loads and typical clinical
344   configurations. Testing should account for translational and rotational motion (i.e., roll, yaw,
345   pitch) and end-effector actuation (e.g., open/close) of the surgical instruments. Testing should
346   verify that the system provides real-time, synchronized feedback sufficient to support safe and
347   effective surgical performance. FDA recommends testing a minimum of three RASDs. If a
348   smaller sample size is proposed, we recommend that a rationale be provided for the number of
349   samples on which these tests are performed to demonstrate consistent performance across
350   different manufacturing productions.

352                          b)    Motion Enhancement and Operator Input Processing
353                          Functions
354   Significance: RASDs typically incorporate computational processing of operator inputs to
355   enhance precision, stability, and control of instrument motion. These functions modify or
356   condition the surgeon’s hand inputs before transmission to the working arms and surgical
357   instruments. Common examples include motion scaling (i.e., reducing the magnitude of operator
358   inputs to allow finer instrument movement) and tremor reduction (i.e., filtering physiological

361   filtering functions enable the operator to execute complex tasks, including suturing and knot
362   tying, in confined anatomical spaces while minimizing complications from involuntary

367   predictable operation within predefined tolerances.
368
369   For motion scaling, testing should measure the scaling ratio between operator console inputs and
370   instrument outputs across the full range of scaling conditions, verifying that the device maintains
371   accurate and consistent scaling throughout the workspace. Testing should include assessment at
372   various speeds, directions, and positions within the surgical envelope to ensure linearity and
373   predictability of the scaling function.

376   to attenuate unintended input frequencies (e.g., physiological tremor) while preserving
377   intentional surgical movements. This may be accomplished by introducing controlled sinusoidal

379   measuring the corresponding output at the surgical instrument tips.

577                          g)     Motion Safety and Singularity Avoidance
578   Significance: A singularity is a configuration of a robotic manipulator in which the kinematic
579   Jacobian matrix loses rank, resulting in the loss of one or more degrees of freedom in the end-
580   effector’s motion and limiting the robot’s ability to move or exert forces in certain directions. At
581   or near a singularity, the operator may experience a loss of control where small movements of
582   the operator console controls result in unexpectedly large or unpredictable movements at the
583   instrument tip, or conversely, where desired movements become impossible to execute. These
584   sudden, uncontrolled movements can cause tissue damage, bleeding, or injury to critical
585   structures. In addition to kinematic singularities, unexpected or uncontrolled end-effector motion
586   may also occur due to software failures, electromagnetic interference, or system faults. These
587   conditions may result in unintended instrument motion that could cause tissue damage, bleeding,
588   or injury to critical structures, and therefore should be appropriately mitigated.
589
590   Recommendation: You should provide a kinematic diagram identifying all singularity points
591   within the system and conduct comprehensive testing to demonstrate how potential issues arising
592   from kinematic singularities are adequately resolved. Additionally, you should describe the
593   mitigation strategies implemented to prevent unexpected motion near these critical
594   configurations. You should also verify that the system avoids configurations where the end
595   effector may move in an unexpected manner due to software failure, electromagnetic
596   interference, or fault conditions. Testing should evaluate end-effector motion during fault
597   conditions to demonstrate that unintended or hazardous movements are prevented or mitigated.
598   The testing should encompass all components used to perform surgery, including working arms,

876   Significance: Use-related hazards are hazards that might result from aspects of the user interface
877   design that cause the user to fail to adequately or correctly perceive, read, interpret, understand
878   or act on information from the RASD. They generally do not involve specific failure modes

1509   Appendix A. Umbrella and Covered Procedure Paradigm
1510
1511   The umbrella and covered procedure paradigm is intended to be a least burdensome approach for
1512   developing the minimum amount of data necessary to support a finding that a RASD is
1513   appropriately safe and effective for its uses.
1514
1515   An umbrella procedure is a surgical procedure of sufficient complexity and risk such that
1516   performance data derived from the umbrella procedure may be leveraged to provide support for
1517   the safe and effective use of the RASD for covered procedures. A covered procedure is a surgical
1518   procedure in the same surgical specialty (e.g., general surgery) as the umbrella procedure, but
1519   one that is of lesser complexity and risk than its umbrella procedure. Specifically, in vivo data
1520   from a procedure with sufficient complexity and higher risk (i.e., umbrella procedure) often can
1521   be leveraged to support the authorization of the device for procedures of lesser complexity and
1522   risk (i.e., covered procedures) within the same surgical specialty without additional specific
1523   performance data for that procedure. In general, the benefit-risk profile of the RASD for
1524   umbrella procedures should be similar or greater than for covered procedures. Note that this
1525   paradigm may be appropriate to support premarket submissions to add specified surgical
1526   procedures but not for seeking marketing authorization for other changes in indications for use,
1527   such as for the treatment of a specific disease or condition.
1528
1529   There is no set list of appropriate umbrella and covered procedures. However, umbrella
1530   procedures generally contain identical or very similar procedural steps as the associated covered
1531   procedures. Additionally, umbrella and covered procedures are generally under the same surgical
1532   specialty as stated in the indications for use and may be performed in similar anatomic locations.
1533   You should consider designing your performance testing strategy based on how the procedures
1534   chosen may be representative of the device uses being sought for authorization. Illustrative
1535   examples for how data from umbrella procedures could be leveraged to support the authorization
1536   of the RASD for specific covered procedures are provided in the tables below and broken down
1537   by surgical specialties. You should note these examples do not represent the only ways to apply
1538   the umbrella and covered procedure paradigm for a given surgical specialty.
1539
1540   Although the provided examples of umbrella and covered procedures are based within the same
1541   surgical specialties, in the future, there may be circumstances where one umbrella procedure may
1542   be able to cover more than one anatomical area or surgical subspecialty (e.g., if the procedural
1543   complexity, risks, and tasks are similar within the different anatomical areas). Additionally, a
1544   combination of umbrella procedures and/or covered procedures within a surgical specialty or
1545   subspecialty may be appropriate to support the marketing authorization of a RASD for an entire

1620   Appendix B. 510(k) Summary (for 510(k)s only)
1621
1622   Significance: The 510(k) Summary is a public document that summarizes the basis for a
1623   determination of SE (see 21 CFR 807.92(a)). As noted in the FDA guidance, “The 510(k)
1624   Program: Evaluating Substantial Equivalence in Premarket Notifications [510(k)],” in an effort
1625   to improve the transparency and predictability of the 510(k) program and to ensure that the
1626   510(k) Summary reflects the information provided in a 510(k) submission to support a SE
1627   determination, FDA intends to verify the accuracy and completeness of the information included
1628   in a 510(k) Summary.
1629
1630   Recommendation: We recommend that you include a complete 510(k) Summary for review in
1631   your premarket notification. Since RASDs are complex devices, it is important to list all major
1632   technological features in the 510(k) Summary. 11 This may include a table similar to Table 1 in
1633   Section IV.B of this guidance document. It is of equal importance to clearly and accurately
1634   summarize all performance testing conducted, 12 including:
1635       • A list of each bench performance testing performed
1636               o A list of each consensus standard to which the device conforms, when a
1637                    Declaration of Conformity is used,
1638       • A summary of human factors validation studies performed
1639       • Cybersecurity information in accordance with section 524B of the FD&C Act and the
1640           FDA guidance, “Cybersecurity in Medical Devices: Quality Management System
1641           Considerations and Content of Premarket Submissions.”
1642       • A summary of any in vivo non-clinical studies performed, which includes a summary of
1643           the study protocol(s), an evaluation of the primary and secondary endpoints, and the
1644           gross necropsy and histopathology results.
1645       • For any in vivo clinical evaluation performed, a summary of the study protocol(s) and
1646           state if the evaluation included clinical performance testing, clinical literature, and/or
1647           real-world evidence. The summary should include the number and type of subjects and
1648           procedures performed in each study. The summary should include results for the nine
1649           primary surgical endpoints described in Section IV.Q(2) Clinical Data.
