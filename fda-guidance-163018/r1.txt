14   I.       INTRODUCTION
15
16   This guidance is intended to assist applicants preparing an abbreviated new drug application
17   (ANDA) by providing recommendations on demonstrating sameness 2 between the active
18   ingredient in a proposed generic drug product 3 and its reference listed drug (RLD) 4 as required
19   under section 505(j)(2)(A)(ii) of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act) (21
20   U.S.C. 355(j)(2)(A)(ii)) and FDA’s regulations in § 314.94(a)(5) (21 CFR 314.94(a)(5)).
21
22   In general, FDA’s guidance documents do not establish legally enforceable responsibilities.
23   Instead, guidances describe the Agency’s current thinking on a topic and should be viewed only
24   as recommendations, unless specific regulatory or statutory requirements are cited. The use of
25   the word should in Agency guidance means that something is suggested or recommended, but
26   not required.

66   III.       ACTIVE INGREDIENT SAMENESS EVALUATIONS
67
68   FDA has broad discretion to determine whether an ANDA applicant has submitted information
69   sufficient for the Agency to reasonably conclude that the proposed generic drug product’s active
70   ingredient is the same as the active ingredient of the RLD. 15 The statutory provisions outlining
71   the contents of an ANDA do not describe the type or amount of information that an ANDA
72   applicant must submit to demonstrate sameness. In the preamble to the final rule that
73   implemented the Hatch-Waxman Amendments (1992 Final Rule), FDA specifically declined to
74   require that applicants show active ingredient sameness by demonstrating that their active
75   ingredients “exhibit the same physical and chemical characteristics [as the RLD’s], that no
76   additional residues or impurities can result from the different manufacture or synthesis process;
77   and that the stereochemistry characteristics and solid state forms of the drug have not been
78   altered.” 16 Instead, FDA adopted a more flexible approach in assessing whether a proposed
79   generic drug product contains the same active ingredient as that of the RLD. 17
80
81   The preamble to the 1992 Final Rule indicated that “FDA will consider an active ingredient to be
82   the same as that of the RLD if it meets the same standards for identity. In most cases, these
83   standards are described in the U.S. Pharmacopeia [(USP)].” 18 If there is no USP identity
84   standard for an active ingredient in an official monograph, FDA recommends an applicant
85   establish the identity of the active ingredient by using appropriate analytical methods (e.g., high-
86   performance liquid chromatography, titration, etc.). In other cases, “FDA may prescribe
87   additional standards that are material to the ingredient’s sameness.” 19 FDA’s determination of
88   active ingredient sameness is based on relevant scientific information and is made as to the
89   individual active ingredient(s) presented in the proposed drug product.

 95              A.       General Considerations for Determining the Active Ingredient
 96
 97   To assist prospective applicants in evaluating and demonstrating sameness, this section provides
 98   general information on active ingredient sameness issues that FDA generally intends to
 99   consider.
100
101   This guidance provides recommendations on establishing the identity of an active ingredient to
102   demonstrate active ingredient sameness. As part of the identity of an active ingredient, we
103   generally consider the chemical form of an active ingredient to be the entire molecule, including
104   those portions of the molecule that cause the drug to be an ester or salt. For the evaluation of
105   sameness, the identity of the active ingredient may also encompass noncovalent derivatives (such
106   as a complex, chelate, or clathrate, with some limitations described below) of the molecule as it
107   exists in the drug product (i.e., in the finished dosage form 20). Instances where a complex,
108   clathrate, or chelate of an active ingredient may or may not be part of the chemical form of the
109   active ingredient are discussed in section III.C.
110
111   The same active ingredient can exist in more than one physical form, such as polymorphs 21 or
112   co-crystals. 22,23 Polymorphs are different crystalline forms of the same active ingredient; they
113   differ in internal solid-state structure but not in chemical structure. This may include solvate or
114   hydrate forms (also known as pseudopolymorphs) and amorphous forms. Co-crystals are
115   crystalline materials composed of two or more different molecules—the active ingredient and co-
116   crystal formers (coformers)—in a defined stoichiometric ratio within the same crystal lattice that
117   are associated by nonionic and noncovalent bonds. Co-crystals may be viewed as a special case
118   of solvates and hydrates, wherein the coformer is not a solvent (including water) and is typically
119   nonvolatile. In general, differences in physical form will not prevent a prospective ANDA

124
125              1. Synthetic Peptides 25
126
127   In general, FDA considers any amino acid polymer composed of 40 or fewer amino acids to be a
128   peptide regulated under the FD&C Act, rather than a protein regulated under the Public Health
129   Service Act. 26 Currently, FDA generally considers appropriate for submission in an ANDA
130   certain highly purified synthetic peptides that reference a previously approved synthetic peptide
131   drug product or a peptide of rDNA origin. 27 The sameness of the active ingredient of a proposed
132   generic synthetic peptide drug product (proposed generic synthetic peptide) can generally be
133   established through physicochemical characterization and biological evaluation. 28 Although
134   compendial standards may be available for some peptides for which an ANDA may be
135   appropriate, it is recommended that applicants also perform comparative testing of the proposed
136   generic synthetic peptide to the RLD. 29 For example, FDA recommends that ANDA applicants
137   apply orthogonal analytical methods to characterize the following properties:
138

140          •   Secondary structure
141          •   Oligomer/aggregation states

144              2. Complex Mixtures
145
146   For an active ingredient in a complex mixture, FDA evaluates all relevant data in the ANDA and
147   other relevant scientific information to determine whether an active ingredient of a complex
148   mixture has been adequately characterized for the purposes of assessing active ingredient
149   sameness. FDA recommends orthogonal methods to characterize components of the complex
150   mixture and will consider the totality of evidence submitted to characterize the complex mixture.
151
152   In the past, FDA has identified several different categories of complex mixtures, for example: (1)
153   naturally derived mixtures with multiple components (e.g., fish oil, conjugated estrogens); (2)
154   synthetic polymers (e.g., sevelamer, colesevelam); and (3) synthetic or semi-synthetic mixtures
155   (e.g., pentosan polysulfate, low-molecular weight heparins). In most instances, complex
156   mixtures are treated as a single active ingredient because it is not possible to distinguish each and
157   every specific constituent component in the complex mixture that is intended to furnish
158   pharmacological activity or other direct effect in the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, or
159   prevention of disease, or to affect the structure or any function of the body of man or other
160   animals. 31 For certain complex mixtures, however, it may be possible to identify each and every
161   constituent component in the mixture that is intended to furnish pharmacological activity or other
162   direct effect in the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease, or to affect the
163   structure or any function of the body of man or other animals. For such complex mixtures, if
164   there is more than one such constituent component identified in the complex mixture, FDA
165   generally will treat each constituent component as a separate active ingredient. At the same
166   time, FDA will generally not consider such mixtures to be fixed-combination drug products,
167   since fixed-combination drug products contain separate active ingredients that have been
168   intentionally combined together. 32
169
170   To support an active ingredient sameness determination for complex mixtures, we recommend
171   that ANDA applicants characterize constituent components of the active ingredient in multiple
172   batches of both the proposed generic drug product (test product) and reference product under
173   similar conditions. In addition, to the extent a complex mixture contains well-characterized

179           C.       Characterization of the Active Ingredient in the Drug Product
180
181   Typically, the chemical form of the active ingredient in the RLD is the same as the chemical
182   form introduced at the start of drug product manufacture. However, in certain circumstances, the
183   active ingredient may undergo a change in chemical form during drug product manufacture such
184   that the active ingredient is converted to a different chemical form through an intentional or
185   obvious change such that the converted chemical form is present as the active ingredient in the
186   RLD drug product. In such cases, it is the converted chemical form of the active ingredient, as
187   present in the finished dosage form of the RLD, that generally should be used for comparison
188   when demonstrating active ingredient sameness. 33
189
190   When developing a generic drug product, a prospective ANDA applicant generally should look
191   to the approved RLD labeling (e.g., the DOSAGE FORMS AND STRENGTHS,
192   DESCRIPTION, CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY, and HOW SUPPLIED sections of the
193   labeling) to gain an understanding of the chemical form of the active ingredient in the approved
194   drug product. However, FDA is aware of certain cases in which there may be ambiguity as to
195   the chemical form of an RLD’s active ingredient compared to what is described in that RLD’s
196   labeling, 34 which may, as a scientific matter, result in additional investigations by and
197   information from the prospective ANDA applicant (e.g., comparative testing and scientific
198   rationale) to determine the chemical form of the active ingredient.
199
200   The following is a non-exhaustive list of examples of active ingredient characterization for
201   certain drug products. These examples, and our recommendations, assume there is no ambiguity
202   in the RLD labeling as to the chemical form of the active ingredient, as described above.
