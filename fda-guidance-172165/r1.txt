I.      INTRODUCTION

This guidance describes how FDA intends to apply its drug labeling authorities to certain
software outputs that are disseminated by or on behalf of a drug sponsor 2 for use with a
prescription drug product or a prescription drug-led, drug-device combination product (referred
to as a combination product), 3,4 i.e., a drug-device combination product that is assigned to the
Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER) or the Center for Biologics Evaluation and
Research (CBER) as the lead center.

In this guidance, prescription drug use-related software 5 generally includes software that (1) is
disseminated by or on behalf of a drug sponsor and (2) produces an end-user output that
supplements, explains, or is otherwise textually related to one or more of the sponsor’s drug
products regardless of whether the software is regulated as a device 6 under the Federal Food,
Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act). 7 A software function is a distinct purpose of the
prescription drug use-related software. End-user output is any material (content) that the
prescription drug use-related software presents to the end user (e.g., a patient, caregiver, or
health care practitioner). As discussed in this guidance, FDA considers end-user output from
prescription drug use-related software to be a type of prescription drug labeling. 8 Prescription
drug labeling includes a variety of communications by sponsors about their drug products. FDA
generally recognizes two broad categories of prescription drug labeling: (1) FDA-required
labeling and (2) promotional labeling. As used in this guidance, the term FDA-required labeling
includes the labeling reviewed and approved by FDA as part of a new drug application (NDA),
an abbreviated new drug application (ANDA), or a biologics license application (BLA), as well
as supplemental applications (21 CFR 314.50(c)(2) 9 and 314.94 (a)(8); 21 CFR 601.2 (a)). The
term promotional labeling is generally any labeling other than FDA-required labeling.

Generally, the recommendations provided in this guidance are intended to align with ongoing
Agency initiatives across all product centers, including digital health initiatives at the Center for
Devices and Radiological Health (CDRH). 13 In developing this guidance, the Agency has
considered existing Agency policies for the regulation of software to ensure consistent regulation
of device software and efficient, coordinated review in instances when prescription drug use-
related software is reviewed by the Agency as a device. This guidance does not alter the
statutory or regulatory framework for devices or the applicability of such framework to
prescription drug use-related software. Rather, it focuses on the application of drug labeling
authorities to the end-user output of prescription drug use-related software, regardless of whether
the software is regulated as a device under the FD&C Act. In addition, this guidance does not
take a position on the types of software functions that a sponsor may propose to include in
prescription drug use-related software. 14

While the end-user output of a software function is subject to FDA drug labeling authorities,
some software functions may meet the definition of device 15 in the FD&C Act and would be
subject to device requirements. Determining whether a software function meets the definition of
device in the FD&C Act is outside the scope of this guidance. In the case of a software function
that also meets the device definition, please refer to applicable CDRH policies18, as this guidance
solely addresses the drug labeling associated with claims regarding the use of such software
function with the drug . Device requirements may also apply to the software function in
accordance with CDRH’s risk-based approach to regulation of medical devices, including
medical device software. Examples and terminology provided throughout this guidance should
not be interpreted as FDA taking a position on whether the software function meets the definition
of a device or FDA’s intent to enforce applicable device requirements under the FD&C Act. 16

In general, FDA’s guidance documents do not establish legally enforceable responsibilities.
Instead, guidances describe the Agency’s current thinking on a topic and should be viewed only
as recommendations, unless specific regulatory or statutory requirements are cited. The use of
the word should in Agency guidances means that something is suggested or recommended, but
not required.

II.        BACKGROUND

FDA recognizes that the use of software with prescription drugs has the potential to offer new
opportunities for patient care, and the Agency is working to promote responsible, risk-based
oversight of such software. Various software functions, including those associated with mobile
applications (mobile apps 17) or webpages, may have a variety of health-related uses, such as
assisting patients with tracking their own drug administration, providing individualized dosing
recommendations, predicting treatment response, and managing side effects. To the extent that
sponsors of drug products or combination products disseminate software for use with one or
more of their products, FDA intends to implement its policies and exercise its authorities,
including drug labeling authorities, according to an evidence-driven, risk-based framework. As
noted above, FDA generally recognizes two broad categories of prescription drug labeling: (1)
FDA-required labeling and (2) promotional labeling.

For prescription drug products and prescription CDER-led or CBER-led, drug-device
combination products, FDA-required labeling is the labeling that is reviewed and approved by
FDA as part of an NDA, ANDA, or BLA, as well as supplemental applications (21 CFR
314.50(c)(2) and 314.94(a)(8); 21 CFR 601.2(a)). The PI is part of the FDA-required labeling
and must contain a summary of the essential scientific information needed for the safe and
effective use of the drug. 18 Additionally, the PI must be informative, accurate, up to date, and
neither promotional in tone nor false or misleading in any particular. 19,20

Promotional labeling is generally any labeling other than the FDA-required labeling.
Promotional labeling can include printed, audio, or visual matter descriptive of a drug product
that is disseminated by or on behalf of a drug’s manufacturer, packer, or distributor. 21
Promotional labeling must be truthful and non-misleading, convey balanced information about a
drug’s efficacy and its risks, and reveal material facts about the drug, including facts about the

III.       PRESCRIPTION DRUG USE-RELATED SOFTWARE AND END-USER
           OUTPUT

As mentioned in the Introduction, prescription drug use-related software generally includes
software that (1) is disseminated by or on behalf of a drug sponsor and (2) produces an end-user
output that supplements, explains, or is otherwise textually related to one or more of the
sponsor’s drug products, regardless of whether the software is regulated as a device under the
FD&C Act. When a sponsor proposes to disseminate prescription drug use-related software for
use with a drug product, FDA intends to analyze several factors to determine whether the end-
user output should be treated as FDA-required labeling or promotional labeling and how, or if,
the corresponding software function should be described in the PI. These factors include (1)
whether the prescription drug use-related software is essential to the safe and effective use of the
product, (2) whether FDA determines based on evidence provided by the sponsor that there is a
meaningful improvement in a clinical outcome (see section IV.A of this guidance) when the
prescription drug use-related software is used with the drug, and (3) whether the prescription
drug use-related software relies on data from a device or device constituent part.

To identify the labeling regulatory considerations for end-user output of prescription drug use-
related software, it is important to appropriately describe the software. In this guidance,
prescription drug use-related software can be characterized using the following terminology that
relates to the software’s design and intended use:

       •   Software function: A software function 23 is a distinct purpose of the software—in this
           case, prescription drug use-related software. Prescription drug use-related software can
           have one or more software functions:

           - Device-connected prescription drug use-related software functions (device-connected
             software functions) rely on data from the device or device constituent part of a
             combination product.

           - All remaining prescription drug use-related software functions (e.g., those software
             functions that rely on user-entered data) would be considered standalone prescription
             drug use-related software functions (standalone software functions).

When a prescription drug use-related software function receives data from a device constituent

should provide information to support that the software function is not expected to result in
medication errors, such as inappropriate administration of extra doses, when used with the
combination product.

          PRESCRIBING INFORMATION AND CONSIDERATIONS RELATED TO END-
          USER OUTPUT

its functions in the PI. 25,26 One of the purposes of the PI is to allow the health care practitioner to
make an informed decision about the benefits and risks of prescribing the drug. This prescribing
decision could be informed by knowledge about a product’s prescription drug use-related
software. For example, device-connected software functions may have additional features that
would be helpful to a prescriber when choosing the product.

of this information in the PI also may help to differentiate between a combination product with
and without device-connected software functions. In these examples, the PI (e.g., in the HOW
SUPPLIED/STORAGE AND HANDLING section) should provide a brief description of the
device constituent part and the associated software function(s) (see Appendix B for an example),
while the end-user output itself would be considered promotional labeling. In these examples,
information beyond describing the device constituent part and associated software function(s)
should not be included in the PI. In general, regardless of whether the sponsor has submitted
evidence of a meaningful improvement in a clinical outcome, FDA recommends that when the
prescription drug use-related software is not considered necessary for the safe and effective use
of the drug product, the sponsor should include a statement in the PI where the software function
and device constituent part are described noting that use of the software is not necessary for the
safe and effective use of the product.

           2.      Standalone Prescription Drug Use-Related Software Functions

Standalone prescription drug use-related software functions may be described in the PI (e.g.,
CLINICAL STUDIES section) if they are considered essential to the safe and effective use of a
product or they have demonstrated meaningful improvement in a clinical outcome through one
or more adequate and well-controlled studies. Sponsors may also develop standalone software
without generating evidence showing that use of the drug product with the standalone software
function leads to a meaningful improvement in a clinical outcome, and FDA may determine that
its use is not necessary for the safe and effective use of the drug product. In this case, the end-
user output generally would be considered promotional labeling and would not be described in
the PI. 30

APPENDIX B: EXAMPLE OF DESCRIBING THE DEVICE CONSTITUENT PART
AND ASSOCIATED DEVICE-CONNECTED SOFTWARE FUNCTION(S) IN THE
PRESCRIBING INFORMATION

The example below is for illustrative purposes only and shows how a sponsor might briefly
describe the device constituent part and associated software function(s) in the HOW
SUPPLIED/STORAGE AND HANDLING section of the Prescribing Information (PI) when (1)
the sponsor does not submit evidence demonstrating a meaningful improvement in a clinical
outcome associated with the use of the prescription drug use-related software, and (2) the output
is not essential to the safe and effective use of the product.

Example 1

Example 1 is a mobile app intended to connect to and receive data directly transferred from an
autoinjector and as such is considered a device-connected software function. Specifically, a drug
sponsor develops an insulin autoinjector, AUTOINJECTOR-XYZ, that can record the dose of
insulin injected and the time of the injection. The data are transmitted to a mobile app where the
information is displayed and transferred from the mobile app to a cloud-based web application.
The sponsor does not submit evidence demonstrating that the use of the mobile app leads to a
meaningful improvement in a clinical outcome, and the output is not essential to the safe and
effective use of the drug.

The product in Example 1 is reviewed under a biologics license application (BLA). The Center
for Devices and Radiological Health may be consulted, as appropriate. Under this situation,
FDA would generally recommend including the following or similar statement in the HOW
SUPPLIED/STORAGE AND HANDLING section of the PI:

       “… For each injection, AUTOINJECTOR-XYZ can record the dose of DRUG-X injected
       and the time of the injection. Such data may be transferred using Bluetooth wireless
       technology to a mobile app and subsequently to a cloud-based web application for patient
       or health care practitioner use. Use of the associated mobile app is not necessary for safe
       and effective use of DRUG-X.” (Include other required information in the HOW
       SUPPLIED/STORAGE AND HANDLING section (21 CFR 201.57(c)(17)).)

The end-user output includes any material (content) presented on the mobile app and on the web
application to the end user. The end-user output would be considered promotional labeling and
should be submitted on Form FDA 2253 at the time of initial dissemination.

APPENDIX C: EXAMPLE OF DESCRIBING THE STANDALONE PRESCRIPTION
DRUG USE-RELATED SOFTWARE FUNCTION(S) IN THE PRESCRIBING
INFORMATION WHEN FDA DETERMINES THERE IS EVIDENCE
DEMONSTRATING A MEANINGFUL IMPROVEMENT IN A CLINICAL OUTCOME

The example below is for illustrative purposes only and shows how a sponsor might describe
standalone software functions in the CLINICAL STUDIES section of the PI when FDA
determines there is evidence from adequate and well-controlled studies that demonstrates a
meaningful improvement in a clinical outcome associated with the use of the prescription drug
use-related software.

Example 2

Example 2 is a mobile app intended for use with a drug approved to improve glycemic control in
adult and pediatric patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus. The mobile app does not connect to or
receive data directly transferred from a device constituent part and as such is considered a
standalone software function. Specifically, a drug sponsor develops a mobile app for use with
DRUG-Y, an oral drug approved to improve glycemic control in adult and pediatric patients with
type 2 diabetes mellitus. The mobile app allows users to program dose reminders, manually
input and track health information (e.g., blood glucose levels, weight), and receive behavioral
coaching designed to support diabetes self-management behaviors (e.g., healthy eating, physical
activity). FDA determines that there is evidence from adequate and well-controlled studies that
demonstrated the use of the mobile app with DRUG-Y led to a meaningful improvement in a
clinical outcome (e.g., improved glycemic control as measured by HbA1c reduction) compared
to use of DRUG-Y without the mobile app.

The end-user output includes any material (content) presented on the mobile app to the end user,
including dose reminder notifications, health information displays, and behavioral coaching
content.

                                          GLOSSARY

Constituent Part: A drug, device, or biological product that is part of a combination product
(21 CFR 4.2).

End-User Output: Any material (content) that the prescription drug use-related software
presents to the end user (e.g., a patient, caregiver, or health care practitioner).

Prescription Drug Use-Related Software: Software that (1) is disseminated by or on behalf of
a drug sponsor and (2) produces an end-user output that supplements, explains, or is otherwise
textually related to one or more of the sponsor’s drug products, regardless of whether the
software is regulated as a device under the FD&C Act. For purposes of this guidance,
dissemination "by or on behalf of" a drug sponsor could include situations where a drug
company or a software developer working under an agreement with the drug company,
distributes or directs end users to the software.

Software Function: A distinct purpose of the prescription drug use-related software.

Device-Connected Prescription Drug Use-Related Software Function: Any software
function that relies on data from the device or device constituent part of a combination product.
The term “device-connected” describes the function’s data source and connectivity and not
whether it is the focus of FDA’s device regulatory oversight.

Standalone Prescription Drug Use-Related Software Function: Any software function that is
not a device-connected prescription drug use-related software function (e.g., those software
functions that rely on data inputted by the user). The term “standalone” describes the function’s
data source and connectivity and not whether it is the focus of FDA’s device regulatory
oversight.
