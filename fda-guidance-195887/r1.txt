14   I.       INTRODUCTION
15
16   Human genome editing (GE) is a process by which DNA sequences are added, deleted, altered or
17   replaced at specified location(s) in the genome of human somatic cells, ex vivo or in vivo, using
18   nuclease-dependent or nuclease-independent GE technologies. 1 GE is a type of gene therapy that
19   relies on GE components 2 to edit DNA. When a monogenic disease 3 is caused by gene variants
20   that differ among patients – including rare gene variants that are unique to a patient or shared by
21   only a few patients – the GE components can be tailored to target each patient’s specific
22   pathogenic gene variant. An individualized GE product 4 can be designed to include multiple
23   product variants, 5 each tailored to target a specific pathogenic gene variant in the gene of
24   interest.
25
26   This guidance is intended for sponsors and applicants developing individualized GE products. It
27   addresses how an individualized GE product that includes multiple product variants can be
28   submitted in a single Biologics License Application (BLA) under section 351(a) of the PHS

42   The scope of this guidance is limited to individualized GE products that meet all of the following
43   criteria: (1) the product is intended to prevent, treat, or cure a monogenic disease or condition

64   II.      BACKGROUND
65
66   GE technologies offer a transformative approach to addressing monogenic diseases by directly
67   targeting pathogenic gene variants at the DNA level. Although there are numerous types of GE
68   technologies, individualized GE products often use a Cas-based editor that is directed to a
69   specific location in the genome by a guide RNA (gRNA). After binding to DNA, the Cas-based
70   editor enzymatically modifies or replaces the pathogenic DNA sequence, or introduces breaks
71   that disrupt it. Editing can occur either inside the patient's body (using an in vivo product) or in
72   cells that are removed from the patient, edited outside the body, and then returned to the patient
73   (an ex vivo product).
74
75   In monogenic diseases, affected patients may have different pathogenic gene variants causing the
76   disease. Individualized GE products address these gene variants using GE components whose
77   roles differ depending on the product type. For in vivo products, the GE components comprise
78   the active ingredients. 10 For ex vivo products, the GE components are used during the
79   manufacturing process to create the active ingredient (the edited cell).
80
81   Because a single product variant typically targets only one gene variant, a GE product intended
82   to address a range of gene variants may include a set of modified GE components. The physical
83   characteristics of these GE components that differ among product variants are termed variable
84   features. 11 A BLA for an individualized GE product may therefore contain many product
85   variants, each sharing the same core design and differing only in the GE components used to
86   address each patient's specific gene variant. Defining the allowable limits of feature variation in
87   the BLA serves two purposes: establishing the boundaries of the product and facilitating the
88   post-licensure addition of new product variants that fall within those boundaries. Importantly,
89   this framework provides a way for applicants to add new product variants to a BLA based on in
90   vitro data without additional clinical studies.

 93   This guidance introduces the concept of the individualized feature space 12 (IFS), which describes
 94   the allowable limits of variation for the GE components of an individualized GE product. For
 95   example, for a GE product whose only variable feature is a gRNA spacer sequence, the IFS
 96   should describe which types of spacer sequences are allowable in the BLA – such as any spacer
 97   sequence that targets a defined class of pathogenic gene variants in a specified gene and results
 98   in the same editing outcome (e.g., correcting the gene so that it encodes the wild-type protein). A
 99   new product variant should be considered a manufacturing change for purposes of the BLA –
100   rather than a new product – provided that: (1) the variable features of the product variant’s GE
101   components fall within the established IFS; and (2) the resulting product variant is predicted to
102   have adequate safety and activity based on clinical and/or in vitro data. Section III describes the
103   IFS in detail and provides additional examples of how it applies to individualized GE products.

106   III.    THE INDIVIDUALIZED FEATURE SPACE
107
108   The IFS describes the boundaries of the variable features of the GE components. Other aspects of
109   the product should remain constant across all product variants, such as the strength, the
110   formulation, the delivery vehicle or vector for in vivo products (e.g., lipid nanoparticle, viral
111   capsid), and the specific cell type for ex vivo products.
112
113   Applicants should propose an IFS in the BLA that clearly delineates the boundaries of allowable
114   variation for each variable feature of each GE component. Applicants may describe these
115   boundaries using rules, menus of pre-specified options, or other appropriate means. For example,
116   an applicant could propose a rule-based IFS for a gRNA spacer sequence and a menu-based IFS
117   for an editor, as illustrated in Table 1. Importantly, the IFS should be supported by clinical data
118   and coupled with explicit criteria for the in vitro safety and activity of the product variants.
119   These criteria should include, for example, acceptable off-target editing and a minimum
120   acceptance criterion for on-target editing (see Section V). Any product variant that does not meet
121   these in vitro safety and activity criteria should not be included in the IFS.
122
123   All product variants included within a single BLA should share the same mechanism of action
124   and intended editing outcome, thereby establishing a predictable connection between the
125   product’s mechanism of action and the clinical safety and efficacy data from the product variants
126   evaluated in clinical studies. For instance, base editors that have been modified to recognize
127   different protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) sequences may be grouped within the same BLA,
128   provided they share the same editing mechanism. Conversely, a GE product employing a
129   fundamentally different mechanism (e.g., relying on a reverse transcriptase) should not be
130   included in the same BLA as a base editor, because it would be a different product with distinct
131   off-target and immunogenicity risks.

181   A new product variant is not considered a new product – and adding it to the BLA does not
182   change the established IFS – if its GE components fall within the boundaries of the IFS and the
183   product variant meets the established in vitro safety and activity criteria. For instance, in the
184   example shown in Table 1, a new gRNA-005 that targets a different gene variant within the
185   same gene X could be incorporated into a new product variant within the IFS, provided that the
186   new product variant meets the established in vitro safety and activity criteria.
187
188   In contrast, a new gRNA that does not meet the established in vitro safety and activity criteria
189   should not be added to the BLA unless adequate additional clinical data is submitted to support
190   the use of the product variant. Adding such a product variant involves amending the in vitro
191   safety and activity criteria associated with the IFS, which should be supported by clinical data
192   from investigational studies conducted with that specific product variant.
193
194   Altering the boundaries of an IFS to include previously unallowable product variants is
195   considered an IFS expansion. The implications of an IFS expansion depend on the nature and
196   risk profile of the proposed change, for example:
197
198      •   Adding a new editor protein with the same editing mechanism: This constitutes an
199          IFS expansion that adds a new menu item to the editors allowed in the IFS (see Table 1).
200          Applicants should perform a comprehensive risk assessment – including in vitro safety
201          and activity data for the new editor – to determine whether additional clinical evaluation
202          is needed.
203
204      •   Adding a new editor that fundamentally changes the product’s mechanism or
205          introduces significant new or unknown risks: Product variants that use such an editor
206          constitute a different product, and clinical data supporting the safety and effectiveness of
207          the new product should be submitted in a new BLA rather than as a supplement to the
208          existing BLA.

214   IV.      MANUFACTURING PROCESS AND CONTROL STRATEGY
215
216            A.       Manufacturing Process Design and Control
217
218            During process design, applicants define the commercial manufacturing process based on
219            knowledge gained through development and scale-up activities. For individualized GE
220            products, the goal is to design a process capable of reliably manufacturing all current and
221            future product variants that fall within the established IFS. In doing so, applicants should
222            distinguish between the IFS, which describes the allowable variable features of the GE
223            components, and the design space, 13 which describes the allowable limits for input
224            variables and process parameters in the manufacturing process.
225
226            Applicants should use process development studies and formal risk assessments 14 to
227            evaluate how the variable features of the GE components influence critical quality
228            attributes and critical process parameters. To avoid the need to revalidate the
229            manufacturing processes for each new GE component and product variant, the processes
230            should be robust enough to accommodate variable features across the entire IFS. In
231            particular, applicants should consider how GE components across the full range of the
232            IFS might affect material attributes, manufacturing parameters, and product quality.
233
234            Manufacturing controls should be sufficiently robust to accommodate the full range of
235            variable features within the IFS. Some of these controls will differ between in vivo and ex
236            vivo products. For in vivo RNA-based products (where manufacturing control is primarily
237            achieved through chemical/biochemical synthesis parameters), variations in RNA
238            sequence length, GC content, or secondary structure may affect in vitro transcription
239            yields or solid-phase synthesis efficiency. The process should be controlled to ensure
240            consistent yield regardless of the specific sequence. Furthermore, changes to a gRNA
241            spacer sequence may alter the impurity profile or chromatographic retention time,
242            potentially requiring dynamic control of chromatography parameters (e.g., elution
243            gradients) to ensure consistent removal of product-related impurities. For lipid
244            nanoparticle products that contain RNA, if the length or secondary structure of the RNA
245            varies, process controls around mixing rates, flow rates, and lipid-to-RNA ratios should
246            be sufficiently robust to ensure consistent encapsulation efficiency and particle size.
247
248            For ex vivo products (where manufacturing control is primarily achieved through cell
249            processing parameters), the editing efficiency will likely be affected by the variable

290           B.       Manufacturing Process Validation
291
292           Expectations for manufacturing process validation apply to individualized GE products as
293           they do for all other products. 17 For individualized GE products, however, applicants
294           should adapt the traditional process validation approach to accommodate product
295           variability. At each stage, including process design, an applicant should address how
296           variable features affect manufacturing performance. There are also considerable
297           opportunities to leverage process validation information across product variants in an
298           individualized GE product.
299
300           Process performance qualification (PPQ) studies should be designed based on a risk
301           assessment, and PPQ for individualized GE products should account for how variable
302           features and variable manufacturing conditions could affect process performance and
303           product quality. If certain feature variations or manufacturing variables are identified as
304           posing a high risk to product quality or process performance, then those aspects should be
305           evaluated during the PPQ campaign. For example, batch size for an individualized GE
306           product may range from large scale (for product variants intended for patients with
307           relatively common gene variants) to small scale (for product variants intended for only
308           one patient). For such a product that may be manufactured at different scales, PPQ
309           studies should include representative runs that bracket the extremes of process scale. In
310           general, however, if FDA determines that data from the process design stage is adequate
311           to ensure process performance across all potential product variants and GE components
312           in the IFS and across the anticipated range of manufacturing conditions, it should not be
313           essential for the PPQ campaign to include all variable features in the IFS or all potential
314           manufacturing conditions.
315
316           During continued process verification, the applicant should track and trend process
317           performance and product data across all lots and for each product variant to monitor how
318           variable features impact process performance and product quality. When adding new
319           product variants to a BLA, if the initial process validation was properly designed to cover
320           future product variants and GE components within the IFS, additional process validation
321           will generally not be necessary.

325   V.      ACTIVITY, POTENCY, AND SAFETY OF PRODUCT VARIANTS
326
327           A.       Activity and Potency Assurance
328
329           As for all gene therapy products, FDA recommends that the BLA for an individualized
330           GE product include a potency assurance strategy. 18 Lot release testing must include one
331           or more potency assays as part of the DP release specification. 19 Demonstration of
332           potency for each lot should include confirmation that the GE components achieved the
333           desired editing outcome at or above an acceptance criterion based on data from clinical
334           studies. The assay(s) should be capable of quantitatively assessing the specific
335           contribution of the variable features of the GE components to product potency.
336
337                •   For in vivo individualized GE products, potency assays may warrant developing
338                    cell lines that include sequences that match the gene variants targeted by the
339                    product. With such cell lines, for example, potency can be measured by
340                    transducing the cell with the product, sequencing the targeted site, and measuring
341                    successful editing of the gene variant to the intended sequence. Consideration
342                    should be given to how the cell lines can be modified to include additional gene
343                    variants as new product variants are added to the IFS.
344
345                •   For ex vivo individualized GE products, the pathogenic gene variant is present in
346                    the cellular starting material, and the editing outcome should be measured directly
347                    in the cellular DP. In addition, a potency assay should assess the intended
348                    downstream biological modification (e.g., restoration of enzymatic activity).
349                    Applicants should also establish assays to measure activity of the GE components
350                    used during manufacturing and appropriate acceptance criteria.
351
352           As discussed in Section VI.A, FDA may approve the addition of product variants to the
353           BLA based on in vitro safety and activity studies of GE components that have been
354           manufactured by representative processes. The activity studies can potentially use the
355           same potency and/or activity assays discussed in this section. Applicants should establish
356           appropriate acceptance criteria for these assays during clinical development, based on
357           potency data from product lots demonstrated to be clinically effective. For GE
358           components and in vivo products, expressing activity or potency as a relative value
359           against a thoroughly characterized reference material can facilitate the establishment of
360           meaningful acceptance criteria. These acceptance criteria create a well-defined threshold
361           that links in vitro potency to clinical evidence of effectiveness. This link enables
362           applicants to add new product variants within the established IFS to the BLA based on in
363           vitro data and previously submitted clinical data, without conducting clinical studies for
364           each new product variant.

366           B.      Safety Evaluation
367
368           Individualized GE products submitted in a BLA should have defined pharmacokinetic,
369           pharmacodynamic, immunogenicity, biodistribution (where applicable), and toxicological
370           profiles supported by nonclinical and clinical studies. Individualized GE product variants
371           included within an established IFS are expected to have the same profiles. Section VI
372           provides criteria for determining when clinical data are expected to be provided in a BLA
373           to support a product variant, for a product that includes multiple product variants.
374
375           Safety in the context of off-target or unintended on-target edit outcomes for each product
376           variant should be assessed by the applicant using appropriate in vitro methods. The
377           unique gRNA spacer sequence for each product variant creates a distinct off-target
378           editing profile; therefore, applicants should assess the off-target editing risk for each
379           gRNA/editor combination. Applicants should assess unintended editing activities for each
380           product variant using pre-defined next-generation sequencing (NGS)-based methods. 20
381           These methods may be leveraged across product variants. 21 The applicant should conduct
382           the safety assessment in appropriate target human cells and other human cell types
383           selected based on data obtained from prior nonclinical biodistribution studies, as
384           applicable. 22 A protocol detailing the specific methods (e.g., NGS-based nomination,
385           confirmatory testing, etc.) for in vitro assessment of the safety of product variants should
386           be submitted with the BLA, which will help streamline the process of adding future
387           product variants without additional clinical data (also see Section VI).
388
389           The results from off-target assessment studies should include reports of unintended
390           editing outcomes at the on-target edit site, off-target editing activity(ies), and genomic
391           rearrangement event(s). For all identified and confirmed off-target edit sites, the BLA or
392           BLA supplement should include the corresponding empirical/in silico/sequence
393           homology data (as applicable) and risk assessments discussing clinical significance of all
394           confirmed off-target edit sites.

407           A.       Adding a New Product Variant Within an Established IFS
408
409           Adding a new product variant to an existing BLA is a change to the product’s
410           manufacturing, and the applicant must submit a BLA supplement demonstrating that the
411           change does not adversely affect the identity, strength, quality, purity, or potency of the
412           product as they may relate to the safety or effectiveness of the product. 23 When
413           submitting a BLA supplement to add one or more new product variants within the
414           established IFS, the submission should contain in vitro activity data demonstrating the
415           ability of each new product variant to achieve the intended edit at or above a threshold
416           established in clinical studies. Additionally, the supplement should include in vitro safety
417           data for each new product variant, including comprehensive on-target and off-target
418           editing analyses, demonstrating that off-target editing profiles remain within acceptable
419           limits and risk profile. The supplement must update the manufacturing information to
420           include the new product variants and must update the labeling to list the new gene
421           variants targeted. 24
422
423           The in vitro safety and activity studies submitted in a BLA supplement may use GE
424           components manufactured by representative processes (including scaled-down processes)
425           prior to the manufacture of lots produced in accordance with Current Good
426           Manufacturing Practice (CGMP). However, the purity, chemistry, and structure of the GE
427           components used in the in vitro studies (e.g., modified RNA bases and backbone
428           linkages) should be equivalent to those of the intended commercial GE components. If
429           the BLA supplement is approved, the product variant must be manufactured in
430           compliance with CGMP requirements and meet all established release specifications prior
431           to commercial distribution. 25
432
433           If the relationship between the product’s in vitro data and clinical safety and effectiveness
434           is well-understood and predictable, FDA generally does not expect clinical data to be
435           included in BLA supplements that add new product variants. Applicants should justify
436           how the variants evaluated in the initial clinical studies represent the full range of
437           variability and anticipated risk factors across the entire established IFS. If the relationship
438           between the product’s in vitro data and clinical safety and effectiveness is not well-
439           understood or predictable for any specific new product variant, the BLA supplement
440           should include adequate clinical data from subjects who received that product variant. It
441           is important that applicants discuss their specific plan with FDA prior to data collection
442           and BLA supplement submission to get FDA feedback on the evidence package
443           necessary to support addition of new product variants to the BLA.

458
459           An applicant may seek to expand the boundaries of an established IFS in an approved
460           BLA to allow inclusion of product variants that do not fit within the established IFS.
461           Expanding the IFS has a substantial potential to adversely affect the identity, strength,
462           quality, purity, or potency of the product as they may relate to the safety or effectiveness
463           of the product, and therefore this type of change must be submitted to the BLA as a prior
464           approval supplement. 28

467           informed by a thorough risk assessment. In certain circumstances, FDA expects that the
468           safety and effectiveness of a product with an expanded IFS can be assured by in vitro
469           studies (in addition to clinical data already submitted for other variants), and in these
470           cases additional clinical studies will not be expected. Notably, the risk assessment and the
471           scope of any clinical data provided to support an IFS expansion should be informed by an
472           understanding of how the product’s variable features relate to safety and effectiveness.
473           For example, FDA would not expect clinical data to be provided for the addition of a new
474           editor with conservative changes in its PAM-recognition sites if a risk assessment and in
475           vitro data demonstrate that the effects of these changes on product variant safety and
476           activity are well understood and predictable.

491

493           statutory and regulatory requirements. 29 The labeling of such products must include the

558   VIII. DEFINITIONS
559
560   Genome editing component: Any material that is essential for the intended genomic
561   modification, including those that may not appear in the final drug product. Genome editing
562   components may include, but are not limited to, the editor, DNA targeting elements (i.e.,
563   elements used to dictate the target DNA sequence, such as guide RNA) and a donor DNA
564   template (i.e., DNA sequence provided to repair the target sequence), if applicable.
565
566   Individualized feature space (IFS): The allowable limits of variation for one or more variable
567   features of a product’s genome editing components. An IFS includes only features or
568   combinations of features known or predicted to have adequate safety and activity. Adding a new
569   product variant within a pre-defined IFS should not be considered to result in a new product.
570
571   Individualized genome editing product: A genome editing product where the genome editing
572   components are designed to have variable physical features that are matched to specific patients
573   or patient subgroups to address patient-specific gene variants.
574
575   Monogenic disease: A disease caused by a pathogenic gene variant in a single gene, also known
576   as a single-gene disorder or Mendelian disorder.
577
578   Pathogenic gene variant: A permanent change in the wild-type DNA sequence of a gene in the
579   human genome that causes a monogenic disease.
580
581   Product variant: A specific instance of an individualized GE product where one or more
582   physical features have been deliberately selected or modified to target a gene variant. A product
583   variant maintains the core design of the product and shares the same IND or BLA with other
584   product variants.
585
586   Variable feature: A physical characteristic of a genome editing component that differs from lot
587   to lot, within defined limits, to address specific gene variants.
