CHAPTER FOUR

SAFETY COMMUNICATION



4.0 Introduction
Safety communication is a fundamental component of pharmacovigilance and a critical mechanism for
protecting patients and the public from preventable medicine-related harm. Pharmacovigilance does
not end with the collection of adverse event reports or the scientific assessment of safety signals; it
culminates in the translation of those findings into informed action. That action is enabled by effective
communication.

Where important new or evolving safety concerns are identified, the quality, timing and clarity of
communication can directly influence clinical decisions, patient adherence, risk minimization
behaviour, adverse event reporting and public confidence in medicines regulation. A poorly timed or
poorly framed message may create avoidable panic, encourage inappropriate discontinuation of
beneficial treatment, or fail to achieve the intended change in behaviour.

4.1 Purpose
Chapter 4 provides guidance on the communication of important safety information relating to
medicinal products. It applies to the Agency and to Marketing Authorization Holders (MAHs) and
should be read as part of the broader GVP framework governing quality systems, signal management,
risk management planning, product information changes, periodic safety reporting and additional risk
minimization measures.

This chapter defines the roles and responsibilities and procedural aspects of safety communication
process in Nigeria. The guidance is consistent with internationally recognised standards for safety
communication and aligns with established best practices as described in EMA GVP Module IX. It is
adapted to support effective implementation within the Nigerian regulatory framework.

4.2 Scope of Safety Communication
Safety communication covers statutory product information, approved regulatory notices and the
communication of new or emerging safety information. The primary focus of this chapter is the
communication of new or emerging safety information that may affect the benefit–risk balance,
approved conditions of use, or the need for additional risk minimization measures.

4.3 Definitions
 Adverse Drug Reaction (ADR): A response to a medicinal product which is noxious and
    unintended, including medicine-related harms captured under applicable pharmacovigilance
    definitions.
 Agency: The National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC),
    referred to in this document as 'the Agency'.
Effective Date: 16-07-2026                                         Doc. Ref. No: PV-GDL-004-02
Review Date: 15-07-2031

   Benefit–Risk Balance: The balance between the beneficial therapeutic effects of a medicinal
    product and the risks associated with its use.
   Direct Healthcare Professional Communication (DHPC): A targeted communication used to
    rapidly inform healthcare professionals of an important safety issue requiring awareness,
    monitoring or immediate action.
   Emerging Safety Information: New information on a previously known or unknown risk that has
    or may affect the benefit–risk balance, conditions of use or risk minimization requirements of a
    medicinal product.
   Good Pharmacovigilance Practice (GVP): The standards and processes governing the
    monitoring, assessment, understanding and prevention of adverse effects and other medicine-
    related problems.
   Marketing Authorization Holder (MAH): The legal entity responsible for the authorized
    medicinal product.
   Med Safety App: A digital platform that supports the reporting of suspected adverse reactions and
    may be used as part of the national pharmacovigilance reporting ecosystem.
   Package Leaflet (PL): The patient-facing written product information supplied with a medicinal
    product.
   Risk Minimization: Measures intended to prevent or reduce the occurrence, severity or impact of
    medicine-related risks.
   Summary of Product Characteristics (SmPC): The regulator-approved technical product
    information intended primarily for healthcare professionals.



4.4 Objectives of Safety Communication
Safety communication should aim to:

a. Provide timely, evidence-based and non-promotional information on the safe and effective use of
medicinal products;

b. Facilitate changes in healthcare practice, dispensing practice, prescribing practice, administration
practice and self-medication behaviour where such changes are necessary to reduce risk;

c. Support informed decisions by healthcare professionals, patients, caregivers and the public regarding
the rational use of medicinal products;

d. Promote and reinforce risk minimization measures, including additional risk minimization activities
where required;

e. Encourage appropriate monitoring, early recognition and reporting of suspected adverse reactions,
medication errors, misuse, abuse, overdose, product quality defects with safety implications and other
medicine-related problems;
Effective Date: 16-07-2026                                           Doc. Ref. No: PV-GDL-004-02
Review Date: 15-07-2031

f. Ensure that important safety concerns are communicated in a manner that preserves therapeutic
continuity where appropriate and avoids unnecessary alarm or inappropriate discontinuation of
treatment;

g. Maintain and strengthen public confidence in the regulatory system through transparency,
consistency and scientific credibility.

4.5 General Principles of Safety Communication
4.5.1 The following principles should apply to all safety communication activities undertaken by the
Agency and MAHs:

4.5.1.1. The need for safety communication should be considered throughout the pharmacovigilance
and risk management process and should form part of risk assessment and risk minimization planning;

4.5.1.2. Safety communication should be scientifically justified, proportionate to the level of risk, and
aligned with the current evidence base and regulatory position;

4.5.1.3. There should be adequate coordination and cooperation between the Agency, MAHs, public
health institutions, healthcare professional bodies, patient organizations and other relevant stakeholders
involved in issuing or amplifying safety communications;

4.5.1.4. Safety communication should deliver relevant, clear, accurate, balanced and consistent
messages and should reach the right audiences at the right time to enable appropriate action;

4.5.1.5. Safety communication should be tailored to the intended audience by using language, format
and level of technical detail appropriate to patients, caregivers, healthcare professionals, journalists or
the general public, while maintaining scientific accuracy;

4.5.1.6. Information on risks should be presented in the context of the medicinal product’s benefits and
should include relevant information on seriousness, severity, frequency, risk factors, time to onset,
reversibility, expected time to recovery, and where relevant, the consequences of non-treatment;

4.5.1.7. Where there is uncertainty, the communication should explain the nature of the uncertainty,
what is known, what remains under evaluation, and what interim actions are advised pending final
conclusions;

4.5.1.8. Quantitative measures should be used where appropriate when describing risks, including
absolute risks, frequencies and comparable denominators, rather than relying only on relative risk
statements;

4.5.1.9. Graphical tools, summary tables, question-and-answer formats and other visual or simplified
formats may be used where they improve comprehension without distorting scientific meaning;

4.5.1.10. Where feasible, messages should be pre-tested or reviewed with healthcare professionals,
patients or relevant organizations, especially for complex or high-impact safety concerns;
Effective Date: 16-07-2026                                         Doc. Ref. No: PV-GDL-004-02
Review Date: 15-07-2031

4.5.1.11. Follow-up communication should be provided where appropriate, including updates on the
outcome of a review, revised recommendations, or closure of a previously communicated safety
concern;

4.5.1.12. The effectiveness of safety communication should be evaluated where appropriate using pre-
defined objectives and suitable indicators;

4.5.1.13. All safety communications should comply with applicable requirements relating to data
protection, patient confidentiality, fair balance, and the prohibition of promotional content in
regulatory safety messages.

4.6 Roles and Responsibilities

4.6.1 Responsibilities of the Agency
The Agency is responsible for:

a. Assessing safety information and determining whether a regulatory communication is required;

b. Deciding the urgency, scope, target audience, timing and appropriate communication channel(s) for
a safety issue;

c. Reviewing, approving, requesting amendment of, or directing the dissemination of DHPCs and other
safety communications prepared by MAHs;

d. Issuing regulatory safety alerts, website notices, press statements, public advisories and other
official communications where necessary;

e. Ensuring consistency between the safety communication, regulatory action, product information
updates, risk minimization measures and any ongoing review;

f. Requiring harmonized communication across multiple MAHs where more than one product or brand
containing the same active substance is affected;

g. Maintaining accessible national reporting channels for adverse event and quality defect reporting;

h. Monitoring the quality, appropriateness and effectiveness of communications issued under its
authority or at its request;

i. Maintaining records of regulatory communications and related approvals within the
pharmacovigilance quality system.

4.6.2 Responsibilities of MAHs
MAHs are responsible for:

a. Continuously monitoring safety data from all relevant sources and identifying emerging safety
concerns that may require communication;
Effective Date: 16-07-2026                                        Doc. Ref. No: PV-GDL-004-02
Review Date: 15-07-2031

b. Promptly notifying the Agency of significant safety concerns and proposed communication actions
where required;

c. Preparing draft DHPCs, public notices, website updates, frequently asked questions, educational
materials and other communication tools where requested by the Agency or where required by
regulation;

d. Ensuring that all proposed safety communications are scientifically accurate, non-promotional,
consistent with the current evidence base, and aligned with approved or proposed product information
changes;

e. Submitting proposed communications to the Agency for review and approval before dissemination
where regulatory approval is required;

f. Implementing the agreed communication plan, including dissemination to approved recipient groups
within the agreed timeline;

g. Maintaining documented evidence of dissemination, including recipient lists, dates, distribution
channels and copies of final approved materials;

h. Evaluating dissemination and, where required, communication effectiveness, and informing the
Agency of outcomes, limitations or implementation difficulties;

i. Ensuring that relevant pharmacovigilance, medical, regulatory and quality personnel are trained on
applicable communication procedures.

4.7 Target Audiences
The primary target audiences for safety communication should be patients, caregivers and healthcare
professionals who prescribe, dispense, administer, recommend, handle or use medicinal products.

Healthcare professionals and patients/caregivers constitute the primary target audiences for safety
communication, given their direct role in the safe use of medicinal products, implementation of risk
minimisation measures, monitoring, and reporting of safety-related concerns.

Secondary audiences, including patient groups, professional associations, academic institutions, and
relevant supply chain stakeholders, serve an important supportive role in facilitating wider
dissemination and reinforcing consistent regulatory messages.

The media is also important for major safety concerns, as accurate and timely information helps ensure
correct public understanding and supports the Agency’s regulatory position.

4.8 Content of Safety Communication
4.8.1 Safety communication should contain, as applicable and proportionate to the issue:

a. The name of the medicinal product(s), including generic name, brand name(s), active substance(s),
strength and dosage form where relevant;
Effective Date: 16-07-2026                                           Doc. Ref. No: PV-GDL-004-02
Review Date: 15-07-2031

b. A clear description of the safety concern or emerging information;

c. The reason for initiating the communication, including the regulatory or clinical context;

d. A summary of the evidence supporting the communication, including what is known and, where
relevant, what remains under evaluation;

e. The population(s) at risk and any identified risk factors;

f. The implications for the product’s benefit–risk balance or approved conditions of use;

g. Specific recommendations to healthcare professionals, patients and other relevant users on what
actions to take;

h. Any changes proposed or required to the product information, including the SmPC, package leaflet,
labelling or other risk minimization materials;

i. A statement of alignment or agreement between the MAH and the Agency where applicable;

j. References to further information, supporting documents or official sources where more detailed
information can be found;

k. A reminder of the obligation or recommendation to report suspected adverse reactions and quality
problems through approved national channels;

l. Contact information for follow-up questions, where appropriate.

4.8.2 The information contained in a safety communication should not be misleading and should be
presented objectively. Safety communication should not include any material, wording, imagery or
statement that could constitute advertising, promotion or product endorsement.

4.8.3 Where applicable, DHPCs and other relevant safety communications should include a standard
reporting reminder such as the following:

“Healthcare professionals and consumers are advised to report adverse events and quality problems
associated with the use of medical products through the approved national reporting channels,
including the nearest NAFDAC office, e-mail to pharmacovigilance@nafdac.gov.ng, the e-reporting
platform available on the NAFDAC website, and the Med Safety App.”

The Agency may issue and periodically update a standard reporting statement or template for use
across all regulated safety communications to ensure consistency.

4.9 Means and Channels of Safety Communication
4.9.1 A range of communication tools and channels may be used depending on the urgency of the
issue, the intended audience, the need for immediate action, the complexity of the message, and the
expected reach of the communication. More than one channel may be used where necessary to ensure
timely and effective dissemination. Communication channels may include:

Review Date: 15-07-2031

a. Direct Healthcare Professional Communications (DHPCs);

b. Lay-language public advisories or question-and-answer documents;

c. Press releases and press briefings;

d. NAFDAC website notices and MAH-controlled website updates;

e. Digital tools, including mobile applications, electronic alerts, social media or other approved web-
based platforms;

f. Bulletins, newsletters and circulars;

g. Responses to public enquiries and medical information requests;

h. Educational materials used as additional risk minimization measures;

i. Scientific or professional publications where relevant.

When multiple channels are used, the messages should be aligned and mutually consistent. The timing
of release across channels should be coordinated so that primary audiences, especially healthcare
professionals, receive actionable information at or before the time the issue is widely publicized.

4.9.2 Direct Healthcare Professional Communication (DHPC)
4.9.2.1 A Direct Healthcare Professional Communication (DHPC) is a targeted regulatory
communication used to deliver important safety information directly to individual healthcare
professionals where there is a need for immediate awareness, specific action, or a change in clinical or
dispensing practice in relation to a medicinal product.

4.9.2.2 A DHPC is not a response to an individual enquiry and should not be used as routine
promotional or educational material. It is a focused safety intervention intended to support patient
protection and regulatory implementation.

4.9.2.3 The preparation of a DHPC should involve cooperation between the MAH and the Agency.
The content, recipient groups, dissemination method, timing and any associated materials should be
agreed before dissemination. Where several MAHs market products containing the same active
substance or otherwise affected by the same safety issue, a single harmonized message should
normally be used unless the Agency directs otherwise.

4.9.2.4 Where appropriate, healthcare professional bodies, specialist societies or relevant programme
stakeholders may be consulted during the development of a DHPC to improve relevance and usability,
provided this does not delay urgent communication.

4.9.2.5 A DHPC may also form part of an additional risk minimization measure and may be
accompanied by other communication tools such as website notices, FAQs, patient advisories or
educational materials.

Review Date: 15-07-2031

4.9.2.6 A DHPC should be disseminated where there is a need to take immediate action or change
current practice in relation to a medicinal product, including but not limited to the following situations:

a. Suspension, withdrawal, cancellation or revocation of a Certificate of Registration or authorization
for safety reasons;

b. An important change to the use of a medicinal product due to restriction of an indication,
introduction of a new contraindication, change in recommended dose, or addition of clinically
significant monitoring requirements for safety reasons;

c. Restriction in availability, supply interruption, or discontinuation of a medicinal product where the
interruption may have detrimental effects on patient care and requires clinical substitution or
mitigation measures;

d. Identification of a new serious or clinically significant risk requiring immediate professional
awareness or action;

e. A clinically important increase in the frequency or severity of a known risk;

f. New recommendations for prevention, early recognition or management of adverse reactions,
medication errors, misuse or abuse;

g. Interim communication during the assessment of an important potential risk where immediate
vigilance or enhanced monitoring is needed pending final regulatory action.

The Agency may require a DHPC or may issue an equivalent regulatory communication whenever it
considers such action necessary for the continued safe and effective use of a medicinal product.

4.9.3 Lay-Language Documents and Public-Facing Communication
4.9.3.1 Communication materials intended for patients, caregivers and the general public should use
plain, clear and accessible language. Question-and-answer formats, summary bullet points,
infographics, visual aids and frequently asked questions may be used where they improve
understanding.

4.9.3.2 Lay-language communications should explain the safety concern, what is known, what actions
patients or caregivers should take, when medical attention should be sought, and where further
information can be obtained. They should also reflect the Agency’s recommendations and any relevant
risk minimization advice for patients and healthcare professionals.

4.9.3.3 Where a safety issue is likely to attract widespread public attention or may affect populations
with varying levels of literacy, consideration should be given to adapting or translating the
communication into Nigerian Pidgin and the major Nigerian languages, particularly for high-impact
public health communications. Such adaptation should preserve the scientific meaning and avoid
introducing ambiguity.

4.9.3.4 Where public-facing communication is issued by an MAH, it should remain aligned with the
current regulatory position and should not contradict, pre-empt or undermine the Agency’s assessment
or instructions.

4.9.4 Press Communication
4.9.4.1 Press communication includes press releases, press briefings, media statements and other
structured communications intended primarily for journalists and media organizations. Press
communication may be used where a safety issue is likely to attract public attention, requires wide
amplification, or may be misunderstood if left to informal or third-party interpretation.

4.9.4.2 The Agency may issue press communications directly and may publish them on official
platforms. This helps ensure that journalists and the public receive information that is consistent with
the current scientific and regulatory assessment.

4.9.4.3 MAHs may also prepare press statements where appropriate, subject to applicable regulatory
requirements. Such statements should accurately reflect the current regulatory position, clearly identify
any ongoing review where relevant, and avoid speculative, promotional or misleading content.

4.9.4.4 Where a DHPC is also being issued, healthcare professionals should ideally receive it before, or
at approximately the same time as, the press communication so that they are prepared to respond to
patient questions and implement any required action.

4.9.5 Websites and Controlled Digital Platforms
4.9.5.1 Websites are an important source of information for patients, healthcare professionals and the
general public actively seeking information on medicinal products. Important safety information
published on the Agency or MAH-controlled websites should be easy to locate, clearly dated,
understandable, and maintained in an up-to-date form.

4.9.5.2 Outdated information should be archived, clearly marked as superseded, or removed where
appropriate to avoid confusion. Where a communication is updated, the revised version should clearly
indicate that it replaces a previous version and, where necessary, summarise the changes made.

4.9.5.3 MAHs should ensure that safety information posted on websites under their control is
consistent with approved or directed regulatory communication and that version control is maintained
within the pharmacovigilance quality system.

4.9.6 Other Web-Based and Rapid Communication Tools
In addition to websites, safety information may be disseminated through other approved digital tools
and channels, including mobile applications, secure e-mail alerts, electronic circulars, professional
messaging systems, or other web-based platforms used by target audiences.

The Agency recognises the role of digital reporting and communication tools, including the Med
Safety App, which supports the reporting of suspected adverse reactions and may contribute to
improved accessibility of pharmacovigilance reporting by healthcare professionals, MAHs and
consumers.

When using rapid or digital communication channels, special attention should be paid to governance,
accuracy, version control, and consistency with official regulatory messages. Faster channels should
not compromise scientific accuracy, regulatory oversight or clarity of recommended actions.

4.9.7 Bulletins, Newsletters and Circulars
Bulletins, newsletters and circulars may be used to provide periodic updates on the safety and
effectiveness of medicinal products, communicate lessons from pharmacovigilance data, reinforce
reporting obligations, and highlight recent regulatory actions or product information changes.

These tools may be particularly useful for reaching broad professional audiences over time and for
supporting awareness-building in lower-urgency situations. However, they should not be used as the
sole communication channel where urgent action is required.

4.9.8 Responding to Enquiries from the Public
MAHs should have documented systems for responding to enquiries from patients, caregivers and
members of the public regarding medicinal products and associated safety concerns. Responses should
be accurate, consistent with the current public domain information and aligned with the latest
Agency’s recommendations.

Where a question relates to individual treatment advice, diagnosis or clinical decision-making, the
individual should be advised to consult a qualified healthcare professional. Enquiry responses should
not be used to provide promotional information under the guise of medical information.

4.9.9 Other Means of Communication
Additional communication tools may include scientific publications, publications of professional
bodies, training materials, programme guidance notes, institutional circulars, professional webinars or
other context-specific channels. Where such tools are used to communicate a safety issue, the content
should remain consistent with the approved or official regulatory position.

4.10 Safety Communication as Part of Risk Minimisation
4.10.1 In addition to product information, safety communication tools may be used as part of broader
risk minimisation activities. Depending on the nature of the risk, these may include educational
materials, controlled access information, patient alert cards, checklists, prescribing guides, dispensing
guides, monitoring tools or targeted training materials.

4.10.2 Where safety communication forms part of an additional risk minimisation measure, it should
be planned, documented and evaluated in a manner consistent with the overall risk management plan
and relevant pharmacovigilance obligations.

4.11 Evaluation of Effectiveness of Safety Communication
Safety communication is considered effective when the intended message is received, understood and
acted upon by the target audience in the way intended, and when the communication contributes to
safer medicine use or improved risk control.

Effectiveness evaluation may include, where appropriate:
Effective Date: 16-07-2026                                            Doc. Ref. No: PV-GDL-004-02
Review Date: 15-07-2031

a. Confirmation of successful dissemination to intended recipients;

b. Assessment of reach, accessibility or visibility of the communication;

c. Awareness, recall or understanding among recipients;

d. Changes in prescribing, dispensing, monitoring, reporting or patient behaviour;

e. Trends in adverse event reporting or medication error reporting after communication;

f. Feedback from healthcare institutions, professional bodies, patient groups or programme managers;

g. Assessment of whether the communication reduced confusion, misinformation or unintended
consequences.

A research-based or structured evaluation approach should be used where appropriate, especially for
high-impact or recurring safety issues.

4.12 Quality System Requirements
Safety communication should be governed by the pharmacovigilance quality system. The Agency and
MAHs should maintain written procedures that describe how safety communications are initiated,
reviewed, approved, disseminated, updated, archived and evaluated.

These procedures should define roles and responsibilities, escalation pathways for urgent
communications, approval requirements, document control rules, version control expectations,
dissemination records, archiving requirements, and arrangements for training relevant staff.

Safety communications should be traceable and inspection-ready. Records should include, as
applicable, the scientific rationale for communication, decision records, approval history, final versions
of materials, recipient groups, dates of dissemination, channels used, and any follow-up or
effectiveness evaluation outputs.

4.13 Quality Control, Review and Documentation
    A. Safety communications should be subject to appropriate quality control to ensure scientific
       accuracy, clarity, consistency, readability, legal compliance and non-promotional presentation.

   B. For this purpose, reviewed procedures with allocated responsibilities should be followed and
      documented. Depending on the type of communication, review may involve
      pharmacovigilance, medical, regulatory, legal, quality and communications functions, provided
      that such review does not delay urgent patient safety action.

   C. Where errors, omissions or inconsistencies are identified after dissemination, prompt corrective
      communication should be considered where necessary, and the incident should be managed
      within the quality system using appropriate deviation, investigation and corrective/preventive
      action processes.

4.14 Conclusion
This Chapter provides a strong and operationally useful framework for safety communication under
the NAFDAC GVP system. It improves clarity of language, strengthens regulatory expectations,
standardises roles, and embeds safety communication within the wider pharmacovigilance quality and
risk management architecture. Most importantly, it recognises that medicine safety information only
achieves public health value when it is communicated in a timely, coordinated, understandable and
actionable manner. With this revised formulation, the chapter is better positioned to support regulatory
consistency, stakeholder accountability, patient protection and effective risk minimisation in Nigeria.
