I.       INTRODUCTION

We, FDA, are providing you, blood establishments that collect blood and blood components,
including Source Plasma, with recommendations for testing blood and blood components for
hepatitis B virus (HBV) to reduce the risk of transfusion-transmitted HBV. The
recommendations contained in this guidance apply to the collection of Whole Blood and blood
components, including Source Plasma.

HBV is a relevant transfusion-transmitted infection (RTTI) (Title 21 of the Code of Federal
Regulations (CFR) 630.3(h)(1)(ii)) and, therefore, subject to the testing requirements in 21 CFR
610.40, the donor deferral requirements in 21 CFR 610.41 and the donor notification
requirements in 21 CFR 630.40.

Under 21 CFR 610.40(a)(1), an establishment that collects blood and blood components must
test each donation of human blood or blood components for transfusion or for use in
manufacturing a product, including donations intended as a component of, or used to
manufacture, a medical device, for evidence of infection due to HBV. In addition, under 21 CFR
610.40(b), establishments must use, in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, one or
more screening tests that FDA has licensed, approved or cleared as necessary to meet the testing
requirement and to reduce adequately and appropriately the risk of transmission of RTTIs. This
guidance provides recommendations for testing blood donations to reduce adequately and
appropriately the risk of HBV transmission under these regulations.

II.      BACKGROUND

      A. HBV and Blood Safety

HBV is a human pathogen of global significance that can cause acute and chronic hepatitis. HBV
infection is a blood borne infection and is transmitted from HBV-positive mothers to infants
(perinatal route), through sexual contact, close person-to-person contact (e.g., contact with open
cuts or sores) and through sharing of contaminated syringes and needles among people who
inject drugs (Refs. 1-2).

Worldwide, approximately 254 million people have chronic HBV infection, with the highest
prevalence observed in sub-Saharan Africa, the Western Pacific, and South-East Asia (Ref. 3).
In the U.S., based on the January 2017- March 2020 National Health and Nutrition Examination
Survey, an estimated 660,000 persons aged 6 years and older had HBV infection (Ref. 4). In
immunocompetent adults, most infections with HBV are acute and resolve spontaneously. Many
cases of acute HBV infection are subclinical. Adults with acute HBV infection only develop
icteric hepatitis in approximately 30-50% of cases and severe disease such as fulminant hepatitis
is rare (Ref. 5). Most chronic HBV infections in the United States are caused by infection
acquired before or at birth (perinatal transmission) and acute HBV infection in newborn infants
and children is often without symptoms. Following exposure, the risk of progressing from acute
to chronic infection is estimated to be 90% among infants born to HBV-infected mothers, 20% in
children under 6 years, and <5% in older children and adults (Ref. 1). Those infections that
progress to chronic HBV infection can remain asymptomatic for decades. As a result, HBV-
positive individuals may be unaware of their infection status. Chronic HBV infection can lead to

                           Contains Nonbinding Recommendations

progressive liver disease including fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma, resulting in
significant morbidity and mortality. There is no treatment that completely eliminates the virus
from the body. However, there are effective prophylactic vaccines that can protect against HBV
infection and antiviral therapies for management of chronic infection (Ref. 6).

The risk of posttransfusion hepatitis was recognized in the 1940s, but the etiology was unknown
for many years. In the 1960s, posttransfusion hepatitis was reported in up to 1.5% of transfusion
recipients (Ref. 7). HBsAg was identified to be associated with HBV infection in 1970, and
HBsAg assays became the first to screen blood donations for a viral infection. Subsequent
improvements in blood donor screening assays resulted in detection of early or acute viral
markers (HBsAg, HBV DNA) and chronic infection (anti-HBc) in plasma and serum. On
average, HBsAg becomes detectable 30 days after exposure (with a range of 6-60 days), while
HBV DNA can be detected 10-20 days earlier (Ref. 8). Anti-HBc appears after HBV DNA and
HBsAg and persists for life in most infected individuals (Ref. 9). With currently available
sensitive tests for markers of HBV infection, the risk of transfusion-transmission per donation in
the U.S. is currently estimated to be less than 1 in 2 million (Ref. 10).

   B. FDA Regulatory History

FDA first recommended testing for HBV in December 1987. In a memorandum addressed to all
registered blood establishments, FDA recommended testing each donation for HBsAg using an
FDA-licensed test and that an HBsAg-reactive unit should not be used for transfusion or further
manufacturing use. The December 1987 memo also recommended using an FDA-licensed
confirmatory (neutralization) test for HBsAg to evaluate donors whose donation tested
repeatedly reactive. FDA provided recommendations for evaluating the status of the donor,
including that the donor should be permanently deferred when the HBsAg confirmatory test is
positive by neutralization. If the HBsAg reactivity in the donor’s sample is not neutralizable
(i.e., not confirmed positive), the memo recommended testing for anti-HBc with an FDA-
approved test. If repeatedly reactive for anti-HBc, the donor is permanently deferred; if not
reactive, then the donor should be deferred for 8 weeks, and the subsequent donation should be
tested for HBsAg and anti-HBc to determine donor eligibility and donation suitability.

On February 20, 1990, FDA announced in the Federal Register that, effective March 31, 1991,
total anti-HBc test kits used for the purpose of screening blood donations would be regulated as
licensed biological products. In September 1991, in a memorandum to all registered blood
establishments, FDA recommended that all donations of blood and blood components intended
for transfusion be screened by an FDA-licensed anti-HBc test in accordance with the
manufacturer’s instructions. The memo recommended that a repeatedly reactive donation should
not be used for transfusion and that donors who tested repeatedly reactive for anti-HBc on more
than one occasion should be deferred. The memo provided recommendations for labeling and
quarantine and disposition of repeatedly reactive donations.

The September 1991 recommendations to test donations for anti-HBc did not apply to Source
Plasma because of the possibility that excluding plasma containing anti-HBc from pools for
further manufacturing into plasma derivatives might result in decreased safety of plasma
derivatives, because it would also reduce the content of antibody to hepatitis B surface antigen

(anti-HBs), which is protective against HBV infection in plasma therapeutics. The memo
clarified that recovered plasma donations may be shipped for further manufacture under a valid
short supply agreement regardless of the anti-HBc test results.

In a July 1996 memo to blood establishments, FDA provided recommendations for the
quarantine and disposition of blood, blood components, and Source Plasma from prior
collections from donors with repeatedly reactive screening tests for HBV. The July 1996 memo
recommended that blood and plasma establishments should identify and quarantine such
donations in their inventory within one week and notify consignees to quarantine components
that had been distributed. The memo provided recommendations for the disposition of units
placed in quarantine based on the result of further testing of the initially reactive donation.

FDA issued guidance in November 2007 that recommended minimum sensitivity standards for
HBsAg tests for the purpose of blood donation screening. The guidance recommended that
blood establishments use HBsAg assays that have a lower limit of detection capability of 0.5
nanograms (ng) HBsAg/mL or less. The guidance also provided test kit manufacturers with a
recommended performance standard when testing the CBER HBsAg Lot-Release Panel #12
under 21 CFR 610.44.

In May 2010, with the availability of FDA-licensed NAT for HBV DNA and improved
sensitivity of licensed total anti-HBc tests, FDA provided recommendations for a requalification
method for donors deferred because of falsely positive repeatedly reactive test results for anti-
HBc on more than one occasion. The guidance recommended that after an eight-week period, a
follow-up pre-donation sample from the donor should be tested for HBsAg, anti-HBc and HBV
DNA by NAT (with a sensitivity of <2 IU/mL at 95 % detection rate) using FDA-licensed tests.
Negative results on all three tests in the follow-up sample qualified the donor for reentry. The
May 2010 guidance also recommended continued indefinite deferral of the donor if the follow-
up sample tested repeatedly reactive on the HBsAg or anti-HBc test or reactive on the HBV
NAT.

FDA provided recommendations in the November 2011 guidance for the requalification of
certain deferred donors following a recent vaccination against HBV. Specifically, based on the
transient detection of HBsAg following HBV vaccination, the guidance recommended a
requalification method for reentry of donors with repeatedly reactive screening tests for HBsAg,
confirmed positive by neutralization, when the donor received vaccination against HBV within
28 days prior to collection of the donation that was confirmed positive for HBsAg and the donor
is not infected by HBV. The recommended requalification method varied depending on whether
the donor received the vaccination for protection from possible future exposure or if the
vaccination was given for the purpose of HBV prophylaxis following a specific incident of
potential exposure (e.g., needlestick, risk behavior).

In October 2012, FDA notified blood establishments in guidance that we consider the use of an
FDA-licensed NAT for HBV DNA to be necessary to reduce adequately and appropriately the
risk of transmission of HBV under 21 CFR 610.40(b). The October 2012 guidance
recommended that when testing for HBV DNA by NAT is implemented, blood establishments
should continue testing blood and blood components for transfusion for HBsAg and anti-HBc,

For donation screening, the October 2012 guidance recommended use of an FDA-licensed NAT

In May 2015, FDA issued a final rule titled, “Requirements for Blood and Blood Components
Intended for Transfusion or for Further Manufacturing Use” (the “Donor Eligibility” rule, 80 FR
29841). Among other things, the rulemaking defined RTTI as including HBV in 21 CFR
630.3(h)(1)(ii)) and codified that each donation of blood and blood components for transfusion
or for use in manufacturing a product must be tested for evidence of HBV (21 CFR
610.40(a)(1)). The 2015 final rule clarified that when a donation tests reactive on a screening
test for an RTTI, including HBV, you must perform further testing as described in 21 CFR
610.40(e); the donation is not suitable for release unless an exception applies (21 CFR
630.30(b)); and, the donor must be deferred (21 CFR 610.41) and notified of the reason for
deferral (21 CFR 630.40).

In July 2025, FDA issued draft guidance recommending that testing for HBsAg is not necessary
to reduce adequately and appropriately the risk of transmission of HBV under 21 CFR 610.40(b)
when donations of blood and blood components are tested for HBV DNA by NAT and for anti-
HBc using screening tests that FDA has licensed for such use, in accordance with the
manufacturer's instructions. The guidance explained that FDA’s recommendation was based on
epidemiologic trends in the incidence and prevalence of HBV in the U.S., and the extensive
experience over many years in screening blood donations with the three-test algorithm. Acute
HBV infection cases in the general U.S. population have consistently decreased with the
introduction in 1991 of universal hepatitis B vaccination of newborns and other prevention
efforts (Ref. 11). Concomitantly, the incidence and prevalence of HBV infection among blood
donors in the U.S. also decreased, and the risk for transfusion-transmission of HBV may
continue to decrease as donors who were immunized against HBV infection in childhood
represent an increasing proportion of donors in the U.S. (Ref. 12).

The recommendations in the 2025 draft guidance were supported by data from four publications
that reported on the extensive experience of three large blood establishments in screening
millions of donations for HBsAg, HBV DNA, and anti-HBc in the U.S., Germany, and the

Netherlands (Refs. 13-16). The totality of data showed that HBsAg testing does not identify
HBV infections that are missed by both HBV NAT and anti-HBc screening test.

The draft guidance did not change our recommendations with respect to HBsAg testing for
Source Plasma donations because there are no available data on HBV screening test results to
evaluate the effect of discontinuing HBsAg testing. Although data on blood donations is not
directly comparable to Source Plasma because the latter is collected from paid donors,
extrapolating from the available blood donation data does not support discontinuing HBsAg
testing when HBV NAT is performed in the absence of anti-HBc testing (Refs. 13-14). Because
Source Plasma donations are not tested for anti-HBc, the draft guidance continued to recommend
that both HBsAg and HBV NAT testing for Source Plasma donations are necessary to reduce
adequately and appropriately the risk of HBV transmission under 21 CFR 610.40(b).

FDA considered public comments on the July 2025 draft guidance and the available scientific
evidence and decided to finalize the recommendations without changes. Therefore, this guidance
finalizes the July 2025 draft guidance and recommends that blood establishments may
discontinue testing blood donations (other than Source Plasma) when donations are tested for
HBV DNA by NAT and for anti-HBc. Conforming changes to FDA’s recommendations for
donor requalification and labeling are also made in this guidance. Consistent with Good
Guidance Practices, FDA may modify this guidance, including recommendations for testing
Source Plasma donations, in the future based on scientific evidence as it becomes available.

C. Terminology

For the purpose of this guidance and for consistency with the regulations in part 21 CFR Part
610, we use the terms “reactive” and “nonreactive” for results of screening tests (HBsAg, anti-
HBc, and HBV NAT) performed under 21 CFR 610.40(a) and use the terms “positive” and
“negative” for results of further testing (HBsAg neutralization and HBV NAT ) performed under
21 CFR 610.40(e). The term “reactive” includes “repeatedly reactive” when the test requires
initial and repeat testing as part of a single screening procedure (i.e., for HBsAg and anti-HBC
tests). Blood establishments must perform HBV testing in accordance with the manufacturer’s
instructions (21 CFR 610.40(b) and 21 CFR 606.65(e)).

C. Product Management, Retrieval and Quarantine, Notification of Consignees

   1.      You may release donations that are nonreactive for HBV when tested in
           accordance with 21 CFR 610.40 provided all other donation suitability
           requirements are met (21 CFR 630.30).

   2.      You must not ship or use a donation that is reactive for HBV, unless an exception
           for shipment or use is applicable (21 CFR 610.40(h) and 21 CFR 630.30(b)(1)).

   3.      We recommend that you destroy or relabel blood and blood components that test
           reactive for HBV. The blood and blood components may be released for research
           or for further manufacture into non-injectable products or in vitro diagnostic
           reagents when no other suitable sources are available, when written approval by
           FDA is obtained and the blood and blood components are labeled appropriately as
           described below (see 21 CFR 610.40(h)(2)(ii).

           You must label the reactive unit as required under 21 CFR 606.121 and with the
           “BIOHAZARD” legend (21 CFR 610.40(h)(2)(ii)(B)). You must use the

           CFR 606.121(c)(12), 606.121(f), and 610.40(h)(2)(ii)), as applicable:

           a. “NOT FOR TRANSFUSION: Collected from a Donor Determined to be
              Reactive for [HBV DNA, Anti-HBc, and/or HBsAg]”

           b. “Caution: For Laboratory Research Only”

IV.      REPORTING CHANGES TO AN APPROVED APPLICATION

Licensed blood establishments must report changes to their approved biologics license
application in accordance with 21 CFR 601.12.

A. Licensed blood establishments that discontinue testing donations for HBsAg, make
   conforming changes to donor requalification, revise their circular of information, or make
   other changes to their standard operating procedures consistent with the recommendations in
   section III of this guidance, must report the change in an annual report under 21 CFR
   601.12(d), noting the date of implementation (see 21 CFR 601.12(a)(3)). Unlicensed blood
   establishments do not need to report those changes to FDA.

      We recognize that some blood establishments may continue testing for HBsAg when
      donations are tested for anti-HBc and HBV DNA by NAT. In this case, you must continue to
      follow your current standard operating procedures for testing donations for HBV (21 CFR
      606.100(b)) and comply with all applicable requirements, including requirements related to
      the management of reactive screening test results, including further testing and restriction on
      shipment and use (21 CFR 610.40), donor deferral and requalification (21 CFR 610.41),
      donor notification (21 CFR 630.40) and donation suitability (21 CFR 630.30). Because you
      are not making any changes to your approved application, you do not need to report to FDA
      under 21 CFR 601.12.

B. We consider implementation of an HBV testing strategy or other procedures other than as
   recommended in section III of this guidance to be a major change. Licensed blood
   establishments must submit a prior approval supplement under 21 CFR 601.12(b).
