35   II.     BACKGROUND
36
37   In 1970, the Poison Prevention Packaging Act (PPPA) was enacted to protect children (under 5
38   years of age) from unintentional exposure to certain household substances, including foods,
39   drugs, and cosmetics. 8 Under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act), a drug
40   that has packaging or labeling that is in violation of an applicable regulation issued pursuant to
41   section 3 or 4 of the PPPA is deemed to be misbranded. 9 FDA was responsible for enforcing the
42   PPPA until 1973, when jurisdiction was transferred to the U.S. Consumer Product Safety
43   Commission (CPSC). 10
44
45   CPSC’s regulations list “standards for special packaging” 11,12 (also referred to in this guidance
46   as child-resistant packaging (CRP)) for a wide range of household products, including most oral
47   prescription drugs and many nonprescription drug products. 13 There are different ways to make
48   packaging child-resistant, with the most common forms being a child-resistant closure (e.g., a
49   safety cap) and certain unit-of-use blister packaging (e.g., puncture-resistant and peel-push
50   blisters). However, not all container closures (i.e., packaging components that contain and
51   protect drug products), including unit-of-use packaging, are child-resistant. Furthermore, child-
52   resistant should not be equated to child-proof, because CRP is not designed to completely
53   eliminate the possibility of an accidental pediatric ingestion. It can only impede access to
54   harmful products.
55
56   CRP is regarded as an important public health safety tool for reducing the incidence of harmful
57   health outcomes related to unintentional ingestions. 14 Many oral liquid drug products are

75   III.      DISCUSSION OF FLOW RESTRICTORS
76
77   An example of a restricted delivery system is a flow restrictor, which can be added to the neck of
78   a bottle to restrict or control the flow of liquid. Flow restrictors can be added to a container at
79   the site of manufacture, or they can be co-packaged with the drug product and added to the
80   container at the time of use. Alternatively, the design of the container can be such that the flow
81   restrictor is molded and integrated into the bottle neck and cannot be separated from the bottle.
82   Bottles containing a flow restrictor may need a dosing device, such as an oral syringe, to extract
83   the drug product. Depending on the design and technology, flow restrictors can be of the open or
84   closed type.
85

87             volume at one time. A closed flow restrictor can be designed with a self-closing valve

 93        2. Open Flow Restrictors. An open flow restrictor allows for a continuous, controlled

 95           removal of the oral syringe and when the bottle is inverted it permits a slow flow of

 97
 98   The efficacy of flow restrictors has been demonstrated in the pediatric population. 18,19 Children
 99   age 3 to 4 years are typically able to empty the entire contents of bottles without flow restrictors
100   within 2 minutes; however, when a bottle was fitted with a flow restrictor, children of the same
101   age were prevented from completely emptying the bottle contents within a 6-minute period. 20 In
102   cases of unintentional liquid acetaminophen ingestions, the average individual dose was lower in
103   cases that involved bottles with flow restrictors. 21 In general, the incorporation of a flow
104   restrictor reduces the total volume of liquid children are able to extract from liquid medicine
105   bottles, which may reduce the risk of harm resulting from unintentional ingestions.

         Lovegrove et al. assessed the efficacy of flow restrictors in 110 children age 36 to 59 months in a block
      randomized trial with a convenience sample from five preschools. The authors noted that flow restrictors on
      uncapped (open) bottles decreased the ability of young children to remove liquid medicine from bottles when
      compared to uncapped or partially opened bottles without flow restrictors. In this randomized study, 3- and 4-year-
      old children were able to remove the entire contents of nearly all bottles without flow restrictors that were either
      open (96%; 25 out of 26 children) or incompletely closed (82%; 68 out of 83 children) within 2 minutes. However,
      none of the children emptied a bottle with a flow restrictor in less than 6 minutes, and only 6% (7 out of 110
      children) emptied a bottle with a flow restrictor within the full 10-minute testing period. Furthermore, the maximum
      amount of liquid the youngest children studied (age 36 to 41 months) were able to remove was less than 5 milliliters.
      Overall, the study findings showed that children removed less total liquid from bottles with flow restrictors when
      compared to open or incompletely closed control bottles.
      21
         Geller et al. conducted a 6-month poison center phone survey of 1,952 parents of children who had unintentional
      ingestions of liquid drug products that demonstrated the potential efficacy of flow restrictors in children younger
      than 12 years old. The survey identified 289 cases of single-ingredient unintentional acetaminophen ingestions
      among children of the 528 parents who completed the survey. The majority (88.1%) of these children were younger
      than 3 years old. The authors noted that the unintentional ingestions involving liquid acetaminophen with flow
      restrictors resulted in a lower estimated average dose, whereas the unintentional ingestions involving packaging
      without flow restrictors resulted in a 2.5 times larger risk of an estimated exposure greater than 150 milligrams
      (mg)/kilogram (kg), which is the threshold dose used by some poison centers for referral to a health care facility.
      The American Academy of Clinical Toxicology (ACCT) guidelines and POISINDEX recommend 200 mg/kg as the
      threshold dose for which referral to ED is suggested in cases of acetaminophen toxicity.

107   IV.      GENERAL RECOMMENDATIONS
108
109   Manufacturers should consider the use of a restricted delivery system, such as a flow restrictor,
110   as an additional measure to further reduce the risk of unintended ingestions of oral liquid drug
111   products.
112
113   As manufacturers seek to develop restricted delivery systems, they are strongly encouraged to
114   discuss with FDA any proposed restricted delivery system early in the development process. 22
115
116   Standardized test methodologies 23,24 for assessing restricted delivery systems for liquid
117   consumer products are currently being developed. We recommend that manufacturers consider
118   general testing methods and parameters that assess product functionality for labeled use and
119   assess performance necessary to prevent unintentional ingestions. Manufacturers should also
120   consider elements such as those discussed below when developing a restricted delivery system
121   for their oral liquid drug product.
122
123            A.       Restriction Effects 25
124
125   FDA recommends targeted restriction effects to limit the amount of an oral liquid drug product
126   that may be delivered over a period of time. The majority of unintended ingestion cases occur
127   within 5 minutes of access to drug products, 26 and an analysis of NEISS-CADES data reports
128   that 60% of unintentional OTC liquid medication ingestions involve children 2 years old and
129   younger. 27 Accordingly, the recommended targeted restriction effects of the restricted delivery
130   system should reduce the amount of liquid that flows from a bottle in a 5-minute period based on
131   the toxic dose of the drug product to a 2-year-old child. 28,29

141   The use of a restricted delivery system that limits access to a single-unit volume at one time,
142   such as a closed flow restrictor, is warranted for drug products with a narrow therapeutic index 32
143   because there is a small difference between therapeutic and toxic doses. This type of restricted
144   delivery system should also be considered for oral liquid drug products with significant toxicities
145   at doses that are close to the therapeutic dose. For drug-drug combinations, manufacturers
146   should consider the most toxic agent in the combination as well as the combined toxicity of all
147   product ingredients in determining the desired restriction effect.
148
149   Viscosity of the liquid is another important consideration because it will determine the rate at
150   which a liquid flows and influence the performance of restricted delivery systems. Restricted
151   delivery systems should be suitable for use with a liquid with the viscosity of the particular
152   formulation so that typical volumes can be reasonably dispensed by the target
153   consumer/patient/caregiver populations. Products with high viscosity may be difficult for a
154   parent or caregiver to extract from some restricted delivery system designs. Conversely,
155   products with low viscosity, which flow freely, may benefit from a more restrictive packaging
156   design.

170   The following considerations apply specifically to flow restrictors:
171
172       •   Different types of flow restrictors (e.g., open or closed) should be considered and the
173           optimal flow restrictor should be used.
174
175       •   The flow restrictor should not leak when used.
176
177       •   Performance testing should include testing for the maximum number of doses available in
178           the liquid drug container multiplied by a factor of 1.5. For example, a 100 milliliter (mL)
179           bottle that is intended to deliver 5 mL doses (contains 20 total 5 mL doses), should be
180           tested 30 times (20 doses available x 1.5 safety margin = 30 performance tests). These
181           performance tests should be conducted at all specified orientations or when not specified,
182           all possible container orientations as defined by the product label. 34 Compatibility of the
183           dosing device (e.g., a calibrated and labeled oral syringe) with the restricted delivery
184           system should be carefully considered and evaluated during the design process.
185
186   When designing or selecting an appropriate restricted delivery system, manufacturers should
187   balance the degree of restrictiveness with the need to ensure ease of use. For example, the
188   restricted delivery system should not be overly burdensome in ways that discourage product use
189   or that encourage improper use. In addition, it is vital that the restricted delivery system not be
190   easily removed or pushed through the neck of the bottle, to avoid contaminating the drug product
191   or presenting a choking hazard to children.
