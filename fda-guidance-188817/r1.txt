14   I.        INTRODUCTION
15
16   This guidance is intended to assist sponsors in the overall development program for drug and
17   biological products 2 for the treatment of malaria, caused by clinically relevant Plasmodium
18   species (e.g., P. falciparum, P. vivax, P. malariae, P. ovale, and P. knowlesi as well as some
19   zoonotic species, e.g., P. simium and P. cynomolgi). Most of the experience in drug
20   development for the treatment of malaria has been gained from P. falciparum trials. This
21   guidance is intended to serve as a focus for continued discussions on the design of malaria
22   treatment trials among the Agency, pharmaceutical sponsors, the academic community, and the
23   public. 3 Some of the trial design issues were discussed at the FDA public workshop on Clinical
24   Trial Design Considerations for Malaria Drug Development held on June 30, 2016. 4
25
26   This guidance does not address drug development pertaining to prophylaxis of malaria.
27   Furthermore, this guidance does not address other aspects of drug development such as
28   pharmacology/toxicology, clinical pharmacology, or manufacturing. The general issues of
29   statistical analysis or clinical trial design are addressed in the ICH guidances for industry E9
30   Statistical Principles for Clinical Trials (September 1998), E9(R1) Statistical Principles for
31   Clinical Trials: Addendum: Estimands and Sensitivity Analysis in Clinical Trials (May 2021),
32   and E10 Choice of Control Group and Related Issues in Clinical Trials (May 2001), 5
33   respectively.

42   II.       BACKGROUND
43
44   Malaria is a parasitic disease primarily transmitted by Anopheles species mosquitoes. Rarely, it
45   can also be transmitted by transfusion of blood or blood components. Malaria is caused by
46   several Plasmodium species and is endemic in most tropical countries. Clinical manifestations,
47   including the severity of malaria, are dependent on the infecting species and host factors.
48
49   Following the inoculation of Plasmodium sporozoites through the bite(s) of infected mosquitoes,
50   the sporozoites migrate to the liver and invade hepatocytes (hepatic or exoerythrocytic phase of
51   the life cycle). Merozoites are released from hepatocytes into the circulation and invade and
52   multiply within red blood cells. These red blood cells can rupture, releasing merozoites that
53   invade other red blood cells (erythrocytic phase of the life cycle). Some merozoites mature into
54   male and female gametocytes within the red blood cells. Asexual forms of the erythrocytic stage
55   parasites are responsible for the clinical manifestations of the disease.
56
57   P. vivax and P. ovale form a dormant stage in the liver, known as hypnozoites, that can cause
58   relapse when released into the circulation weeks or years later; P. falciparum, P. malariae, and
59   P. knowlesi do not form hypnozoites.
60
61   The biology of Plasmodium species has specific implications for antimalarial drug development.
62   Treatment is directed at the eradication of the erythrocytic and/or exoerythrocytic stages of
63   infection depending on the target Plasmodium species.
64
65   The terminology used for assessing clinical and parasitological responses includes the following:
66
67         •   Clinical cure is directed at eradication of the parasites in addition to adequate clinical
68             response (i.e., absence of clinical symptom(s) present at baseline).
69
70         •   Radical cure eliminates both erythrocytic and exoerythrocytic stages of infection,
71             including the hypnozoites.
72
73         •   Recrudescence is recurrence of the existing parasitemia due to the survival of the
74             erythrocytic stage parasites of Plasmodium species.
75
76         •   Relapse is recurrence of the original parasitemia attributable to the Plasmodium parasites
77             that have a hypnozoite stage, that is, P. vivax or P. ovale.

 87   III.       DEVELOPMENT CONSIDERATIONS
 88
 89              A.     General Drug Development Considerations
 90
 91   Considerations for early phase drug development and assessments of efficacy and safety are
 92   outlined as follows:
 93
 94              1.     Nonclinical Development Considerations
 95
 96          •   The activity of an antimalarial drug and its metabolites should be measured using
 97              appropriate in vitro and animal models of Plasmodium species. Testing should be
 98              performed against the erythrocytic and exoerythrocytic stages of the laboratory strains
 99              and clinical isolates of P. falciparum and other Plasmodium species.
100
101          •   Considerations when choosing an appropriate nonclinical model and experimental design
102              should include selecting appropriate Plasmodium species relevant to human infection, the
103              similarity of the course of infection and disease in animals and humans, as well as the
104              ability to obtain reproducible parasitemia. Similarly, measurement of activity should be
105              based on relevant endpoints and biomarkers (e.g., survival of the animal, reduction in
106              parasitemia, effect on erythrocytic and exoerythrocytic stages, parasite clearance time,
107              and recrudescence or relapse).
108
109          •   The ability of Plasmodium strains to develop reduced drug sensitivity/resistance when
110              subjected to drug pressure should be examined in appropriate in vitro and/or in vivo
111              models, and efforts should be made to identify the potential resistance markers.
112              Furthermore, the potential for cross-resistance with antimalarial drugs in the same class
113              or in other classes should be evaluated. Investigating the underlying mechanism(s) of
114              resistance would be informative if reduced drug sensitivity is demonstrated. Attempts
115              should be made to evaluate the significance of any changes in phenotype (e.g., in vitro
116              sensitivity to the drug) or genotype observed by correlating such changes with outcome.
117
118              2.     Early Phase Clinical Development Considerations
119
120          •   Controlled human malaria infection (CHMI) model studies, usually conducted in malaria-
121              naïve healthy participants from nonendemic regions, may aid in providing preliminary
122              evidence of efficacy for investigational drugs and for selecting doses and a regimen based
123              on pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic analysis. Additionally, CHMI studies may
124              provide clinical evidence of the contribution of each drug when investigational drugs are

125           used in combination. CHMI studies should be performed with well-characterized strains
126           of the parasites (e.g., P. falciparum or P. vivax). The endpoints may be based on
127           assessment of parasitemia measured by microscopic evidence of parasites on blood
128           smears and/or a biomarker (e.g., P. falciparum 18S rRNA/DNA 6) in addition to clinical
129           signs and symptoms of malaria. Clinical protocols for CHMI studies in nonendemic and
130           endemic regions should be discussed with the Agency.
131
132       •   Exposure-response relationships should be explored during early phases of drug
133           development to aid in selection of optimal dosing strategies for evaluations in the later
134           trials. Evaluation of the relationship between drug concentration and parasite clearance
135           by integrating the pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic information from in vitro and in
136           vivo drug evaluations is recommended to develop dosing strategies that avoid the
137           emergence of resistance to the investigational drug. During early phases of clinical
138           development, a simplified analysis relating exposure and proportion of participants with
139           clinical failure can be used to support evidence of effectiveness and justify dose selection.
140           Additional analyses of the exposure-safety relationship using similar approaches should
141           be performed to assist in evaluating the balance between effectiveness and toxicity of
142           different dosage regimens. Physiologically-based pharmacokinetic modeling integrated
143           with pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic information from in vitro and in vivo
144           evaluations can be useful to define dosing strategies for phase 3 development.
145           Evaluations of specific patient populations with different intrinsic factors (e.g., age, body
146           weight, and hepatic or renal impairment) in phase 3 efficacy trials can provide additional
147           information about exposure-response relationships.
148
149           3.       Efficacy Considerations
150
151       •   Drugs that are effective against the erythrocytic stages of P. falciparum may be effective
152           against the erythrocytic stages of other less prevalent Plasmodium species. 7 Sponsors
153           who seek an indication for more than one Plasmodium species (e.g., P. vivax in addition
154           to P. falciparum) should demonstrate the efficacy and safety of the test drug in a subset
155           of participants with monoinfection with the relevant Plasmodium species (e.g., P. vivax
156           or P. ovale).
157
158       •   If the investigational product contains multiple drugs, or the investigational drug will be
159           given in combination with other antimalarial drugs, the contribution of each of the
160           component drugs to the combination should be demonstrated to address the treatment
161           contribution of each component. 8 Potential approaches include historical evidence of the

177          B.      Uncomplicated Malaria
178
179   Considerations for trial design and evaluation of efficacy in participants with uncomplicated
180   malaria are outlined below:
181
182          1.      Trial Design
183
184      •   Use of an active control regimen containing FDA-approved antimalarial drugs is strongly
185          recommended. Non-FDA-approved comparator products may be appropriate if they
186          represent the local standard of care. If a sponsor proposes use of non-FDA-approved
187          comparator products in clinical studies intended to support approval of a marketing
188          application for their proposed drug, they should consult the review division on the
189          scientific acceptability of the proposal. When evaluating the current standard of care,
190          FDA considers recommendations by authoritative scientific bodies (e.g., the World
191          Health Organization, Infectious Diseases Society of America, Centers for Disease
192          Control and Prevention) based on clinical evidence and other reliable information that
193          reflects current clinical practice.
194
195      •   In trials, sponsors should minimize risk of reinfection by monitoring participants in a
196          hospitalized setting and/or ensuring the use of insecticide-treated nets.
197
198      •   The optimal duration of follow-up depends on the Plasmodium species. FDA recognizes
199          that in rare cases recrudescent or relapse infection may occur more than 28 days after
200          initial therapy. Inclusion of a late follow-up visit at 42 days or more after initiation of
201          therapy should be considered, particularly when antimalarial drugs with prolonged half-
202          lives are being studied.
203
204          2.      Trial Population
205
206   Efficacy trials should be conducted in different geographical regions to address variations in
207   transmission rates, to assess the sensitivity of isolates/strains to existing antimalarial therapy, and

208   to reflect differences in population host factors. Host factors include immune status (e.g., those
209   living in endemic areas for many years may experience low levels of parasitemia with no ill
210   effects), blood type (e.g., Duffy negative blood types are mostly resistant to infection/disease
211   with P. vivax), pregnancy, and age (e.g., pregnant participants and infants have a higher risk of
212   developing complicated malaria). Attempts should be made to study both immune and
213   nonimmune participants.
214
215       •   Some relevant inclusion criteria include the following:
216
217           — Identification of the Plasmodium species on peripheral blood smears; parasitemia
218             should be limited to values between 1000/µL and <250,000/µL (0.02 percent and <5
219             percent). 9 Proposals to study parasitemia outside of this range should be discussed
220             with the Agency before protocol submission (see section III.C).
221
222           — Participants with mixed Plasmodium infections can be included in P. falciparum
223             treatment studies; however, participants with infection due to more than one
224             Plasmodium species should be evaluated in prespecified subgroup analyses.
225
226           — Fever (e.g., oral or tympanic temperature greater than or equal to 38ºC) should be
227             documented at study entry in a participant who reports fever.
228
229           — At least two of the following symptoms or signs of malaria should be present:
230             shivering/chills, malaise, headache, and loss of appetite in adults or irritability,
231             lethargy, and anorexia in children; or malaria-related laboratory abnormalities (e.g.,
232             decreased hemoglobin, increased serum creatinine).
233
234       •   Some relevant exclusion criteria include the following:
235
236           — Participants with severe or complicated malaria (which is discussed in more detail in
237             section III.C)
238
239           — Participants with prior antimalarial therapy for the current episode unless the new
240             drug is under development for subjects failing treatment with other drugs
241
242           — Participants with concurrent febrile illnesses (e.g., typhoid fever)
243
244           — Participants who require use of antibacterial drugs with antimalarial activity (e.g.,
245             sulfonamides, tetracyclines, and macrolides) for other reasons
246
247       •   Pregnant participants are at a greater risk of symptomatic malaria and poor birth
248           outcomes. When available data from adult clinical trials indicate that the investigational
249           drug should be studied in pregnant participants, adequate reproductive and developmental

262            3.       Clinical and Parasitological Outcomes
263
264   Treatment outcomes can be classified as early treatment failure, late clinical failure, late
265   parasitological failure, or adequate clinical and parasitological response that are mostly
266   consistent with the World Health Organization criteria. 14
267
268        •   Early treatment failure (any of the following)
269
270            – Development of severe malaria (which is discussed in more detail in section III(C))
271              on day 1, 2, or 3 of treatment in the presence of parasitemia
272
273            – Parasitemia on day 2 greater than day 0 (i.e., the day of the baseline visit) irrespective
274              of axillary temperature
275
276            – Parasitemia on day 3 with axillary temperature greater than or equal to 37.5ºC
277
278            – Parasitemia on day 3 greater than or equal to 25 percent of the count on day 0
279

304       4.     Efficacy Endpoints
305
306   •   Primary endpoint:
307
308       Clinical Cure: Clinical cure is based on adequate clinical (i.e., absence of clinical
309       symptom(s) present at baseline) and parasitological responses; parasitological response is
310       based on blood smear findings (i.e., uncorrected cure rates) and varies depending on the
311       Plasmodium species.
312
313       — In trials of drugs that target the erythrocytic stage parasites of Plasmodium
314         species, the primary efficacy endpoint of clinical cure is typically defined as the
315         absence of parasitemia on day 28 in participants who did not previously meet any of
316         the criteria for early treatment failure, late clinical failure, or late parasitological
317         failure. Therefore, recurrence of parasitemia after initial clearance or persistence of
318         parasitemia during the 28-day period would be considered a failure. Duplicate blood
319         smears should be examined frequently, for example, every 6 to 12 hours until
320         parasitemia has been eradicated; eradication is defined as two successive parasite-
321         negative smears. If parasitological eradication has occurred, subsequent malaria
322         smears should be examined at least once a week until the end of the study on day 28.
323
324       — In trials of drugs that target the liver stage parasites of Plasmodium species with
325         a hypnozoite stage, such as P. vivax and P. ovale, clinical cure is defined as the

326          absence of parasitemia on blood smears (i.e., uncorrected or unadjusted cure rates),
327          clinical signs and symptoms, and laboratory abnormalities without recurrence after
328          completion of treatment for an interval consistent with the natural history of the local
329          parasite strains (e.g., 6 to 12 months for radical cure). As recurrence patterns vary
330          geographically with the strain of P. vivax and P. ovale, documentation of the malaria
331          local relapse patterns should be provided and justification for the selected recurrence
332          monitoring period should be included in the protocol submission.
333
334       If test(s) other than blood smears are used for assessing parasitemia, then details of the
335       method(s) to be used should be discussed with the Agency.
336
337       Rescue therapy with alternative antimalarial therapy or loss to follow-up should be
338       considered as lack of clinical cure.
339
340   •   Secondary endpoints:
341
342       — Parasite clearance time
343       — Fever clearance time
344       — Corrected/adjusted cure rates
345
346             The cure rates adjusted for new infections based on genotypic and phenotypic
347              markers should be considered as secondary or exploratory endpoints. Attempts
348              should be made to characterize the genotype(s) of parasite isolates, collected at
349              baseline (pretreatment) and at the time of recurrent parasitemia, to differentiate
350              recrudescence/relapse due to the same genotype (homologous) of the blood stage
351              parasites from a new infection with a different genotype (heterologous). The
352              specimens should be stored appropriately and analyzed at the same time using
353              appropriate methods.
354
355             Differences in Plasmodium parasite genotyping methods can affect estimates of
356              corrected cure rates in clinical trials across different epidemiological settings. It
357              is recommended that the performance of the genotyping assay(s) be assessed
358              across different geographic regions with a range of transmission intensities (e.g.,
359              low, moderate, and high), multiplicity of infection (e.g., variation in the
360              prevalence and number of genetically distinct dominant and minority/low-density
361              parasite clones/strains) and allelic frequencies; such information will be useful in
362              understanding the epidemiological settings and the diversity of genotyping
363              markers in the study population. The validity of the genotype results depends on
364              systematic analysis of the test performance including but not limited to sensitivity,
365              specificity, and reproducibility, as well as effective quality control assessments.
366              The details of the methods used, the performance characteristics of the assays, and
367              quality control parameters implemented in the laboratory where testing of clinical
368              specimens will be conducted should be well documented. The performance of the
369              laboratory should be in accordance with Clinical Laboratory Improvement
370              Amendments. The data elements to be collected should be discussed with the
371              Agency.

378          C.       Severe or Complicated, Including Cerebral Malaria
379
380   Considerations for trial design and evaluation of efficacy in participants with complicated
381   malaria are outlined below:
382
383          1.       Trial Design
384
385   Trial design aspects for severe complicated malaria are similar to those for uncomplicated
386   malaria (see section III.B).
387
388          2.       Trial Population
389
390   Some relevant inclusion and exclusion criteria for adults and/or pediatric participants with severe
391   or complicated malaria include the following:
392
393      •   Inclusion criteria:
394
395          — Participants with hyperparasitemia based on one of the following criteria:
396
397                  Parasitemia of greater than or equal to 10 percent (i.e., 500,000/µL)
398
399                  Parasitemia of greater than or equal to 5 percent (i.e., greater than or equal to

404                   coagulation, jaundice, and/or severe anemia.
405
406               Inclusion of participants with parasitemia of greater than or equal to 5 percent,
407               without any of the major complications listed above, should be discussed with the
408               Agency.
409
410      •   Exclusion criteria:
411
412          — Participants with uncomplicated malaria
413
414   The inclusion and exclusion criteria or other design aspects for special populations such as
415   pediatric participants with cerebral malaria or pregnant participants should be discussed with the
416   Agency.

446              D.       Statistical Considerations
447
448          •   Justification of a noninferiority margin will be essential for all trials assessing efficacy
449              using a noninferiority design. 15 Although effective antimalarial regimens that would be
450              appropriate active controls are generally thought to have large effects over a hypothetical
451              placebo, the noninferiority margin justification may depend on the active control arm, the
452              possible combination regimen to be evaluated in the investigational treatment arm, and
453              the planned population and parasite species of interest.
454
455          •   Sponsors should consider the following definitions of analysis populations:
456
457              — Intent-to-treat population: All randomized participants. If participants are
458                randomized before parasitological confirmation, they should be included in the intent-
459                to-treat population regardless of subsequent confirmation or nonconfirmation of
460                malaria. A secondary or supplementary analysis of the primary endpoint should be
461                conducted in the intent-to-treat population.

463            — Modified intent-to-treat population: All randomized participants with a
464              microbiologically confirmed Plasmodium species at baseline. For both noninferiority
465              and superiority trials the primary efficacy assessment is typically conducted on a
466              modified intent-to-treat analysis population. In noninferiority trials the modified
467              intent-to-treat population should exclude participants if the control is not effective
468              against the baseline pathogen(s). In superiority trials the modified intent-to-treat
469              population may or may not exclude participants based on the activity of the control
470              against the baseline pathogen(s). Participants should not be excluded from this
471              population based on events that occur after randomization (e.g., lost to follow-up).
472
473            — Safety population: All participants who received at least one dose of the study
474              treatment during the trial.
475
476        •   New infections should be considered treatment failures in the primary efficacy analyses
477            for the following reasons: Firstly, from a patient perspective, any new infection is a
478            failure. Secondly, there is a possibility that the new Plasmodium strain was present in
479            low density at baseline. Lastly, a new infection requires treatment, thus confounding an
480            assessment of relapse/recrudescence. Additional analyses separating failures by
481            relapse/recrudescence and new infection should be conducted.
482
483        •   To improve the precision of treatment effect estimation and inference, sponsors may
484            consider adjusting for prespecified prognostic baseline covariates (e.g., age under 5 years,
485            pregnancy, nonimmune traveler status) in the primary efficacy analysis and propose
486            methods of covariate adjustment. 16
487
488        •   The trial should aim to minimize missing data. The protocol should distinguish between
489            discontinuation from the study drug and withdrawal from study assessments. Trial
490            participants may choose to discontinue treatment during the trial for various reasons, such
491            as experiencing adverse events or perceived lack of efficacy. Unless the participant
492            withdraws consent, sponsors should encourage participants who discontinue therapy to
493            remain in the study and to continue follow-up for key safety and efficacy assessments.
494
495        •   Participants who are lost to follow-up or withdraw consent will have missing data for
496            outcomes occurring after that event. For a primary analysis, these participants should be
497            imputed as failures/non-cures; however, additional analyses should be proposed and
498            conducted to assess the overall impact of missing data on the study results. The study
499            protocol should address how missing laboratory samples or other missing information
500            should be handled in the analysis.
