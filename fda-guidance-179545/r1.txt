15   I.       INTRODUCTION
16
17   This guidance addresses key aspects of drug delivery performance information for devices, 1 and
18   combination products 2, 3 that include device constituent parts, 4 intended for delivery of a human
19   drug, including a biological product 5, 6 (herein referred to as drug delivery devices). The
20   guidance describes FDA’s recommendations related to information that is essential for
21   establishing and assessing drug delivery performance and focuses on recommendations for the
22   information and data to submit in investigational, marketing, and post-market change
23   applications. 7 This guidance also provides examples of such information for specific types of
24   devices.
25
26   Device drug-delivery performance information is intended to demonstrate that the device drug-
27   delivery function consistently performs as intended. FDA is providing recommendations for
28   development and organization of this information and these data to improve the consistency of
29   this information in applications and to facilitate transparency and predictability in development
30   and regulatory review. Ultimately, these recommendations are intended to facilitate and
31   streamline development and lifecycle management of drug delivery devices.

54   II.      SCOPE
55
56   The focus of this guidance is the information and data developed and submitted to FDA
57   regarding drug-delivery performance for devices regulated by CDRH and CBER and device
58   constituent parts of CDER-led and CBER-led combination products 10 intended for delivery of a
59   human drug, including a biological product. 11 Essential drug delivery device performance
60   characteristics (EDDCs) are the key aspects of device drug-delivery that warrant development of
61   and submission to FDA of performance information and data to ensure delivery of the intended
62   drug dose to the intended delivery site. EDDCs demonstrate device drug-delivery performance
63   at the system level that is dependent on the device design (see section IV for more information).
64   Drug delivery includes successful product preparation 12 and the initiation, progression, and
65   completion of dose delivery.

71   III.       BACKGROUND
72
73   Designing the drug delivery device to achieve its intended performance is necessary for
74   marketing authorization and is part of the design and development process in the QMSR. 13 For
75   purposes of this guidance, the EDDCs warrant design verification, design validation, control, and
76   maintenance 14 to ensure that the device (1) functions as designed for its intended use, and (2)
77   maintains its intended function, safety, and effectiveness throughout its lifecycle. To ensure the
78   quality of the drug delivery function throughout the product lifecycle, data and information on
79   EDDCs are developed and submitted for review to FDA as appropriate. 15, 16 For purposes of this
80   guidance, in the context of an investigational or marketing application, the terminology used
81   broadly relates to the submitted data and information and their associated scientific or technical
82   justification (e.g., design verification and validation, control, and maintenance). Terminology
83   used in this guidance is not intended to constrain how an applicant establishes their quality
84   management system.
85
86   EDDC identification, control, and maintenance processes can also facilitate development by:
87
88          •   Informing the determination of which data to submit in an investigational or marketing
89              application to demonstrate the drug delivery performance;
90
91          •   Ensuring the appropriate device design attributes and manufacturing process steps are
92              evaluated during lifecycle changes; and
93
94          •   Providing a basis for comparing device drug-delivery performance for bridging or
95              leveraging data across products. 17

130   IV.       IDENTIFYING ESSENTIAL DRUG DELIVERY DEVICE PERFORMANCE
131             CHARACTERISTICS
132
133   This section provides further description of EDDCs and a product-specific example of what is
134   and is not an EDDC. The appendices in this guidance provide examples of potential EDDCs for
135   different product types. 25
136
137   EDDCs are based on product type (e.g., syringe, autoinjector, infusion pump) and product
138   specifics. These characteristics warrant design verification, design validation, control, and
139   maintenance, as appropriate, to demonstrate the drug delivery function performs as intended (i.e.,
140   delivery of the intended drug dose to the intended delivery site) throughout the product
141   lifecycle. 26 Generally, EDDCs have specifications, including acceptance criteria that must be
142   met to ensure that the product performs as intended. These specifications with acceptance
143   criteria are generally included in the submission. 27 EDDCs are high-level characteristics that
144   incorporate lower-level characteristics or product features. If the specification(s) for an EDDC is
145   met, it should reflect that the specification(s) for the lower-level characteristic(s) related to that
146   EDDC are also met. For EDDCs, the lower-level characteristics or product features alone do not
147   ensure the intended drug delivery, and these data are generally not included in the submission for
148   purposes of ensuring the EDDC performance. 28 In addition, although the design considers the
149   user needs, the EDDCs are dependent on the functional design of the device (device design) and
150   not on the user actions. The following illustrates what an EDDC is and is not.
151
152         •   What is an EDDC? It is a drug delivery aspect that:
153             o Is a high-level (i.e., system level) function that incorporates lower-level (i.e.,
154               component level) aspects, and
155             o Depends on the functional device design.
156
157         •   What is not an EDDC?
158             o A component level aspect that is encompassed by a system level EDDC is not an
159               EDDC.
160             o A user dependent function is not an EDDC.
161
162   For example, for a prefilled syringe (PFS) that includes a needle and silicone oil as a lubricant:
163      • Glide force is an example of an EDDC.

186   V.       VERIFYING AND VALIDATING EDDC SPECIFICATIONS
187
188   While design and development verification 31 and validation 32 activities are intended to address
189   all device functions, the following recommendations are specific to EDDCs. Appropriate
190   verification activities for EDDCs depend on the conditions (e.g., environmental conditions) to
191   which the product will be exposed during production, shipping, storage, and preparation and the
192   conditions associated with use. Examples of conditions that may impact performance include,
193   but are not limited to, temperature, pressure, humidity, vibration and shock, and physical
194   orientation. Also, during storage or shipping, a product may be exposed to more than one
195   variation of a condition or sequence of conditions (e.g., ground by truck to air, ground by truck
196   to boat, ground by rail to air to ground by truck). In addition to storage and shipping stressors,
197   there are stressors associated with the use environments (e.g., health care facility, school, home,
198   first response environment).

219              A.       Design and Development Verification for EDDCs
220
221   Prior to initiation of any clinical studies (or any in vivo bioequivalence studies, as applicable) or
222   commercial distribution, applicants verify the performance of the product as appropriate for the
223   development stage. How applicants conduct design verification testing is dependent on device
224   design, intended use, and applicable regulations, standards, and guidances. Design verification
225   generally includes preconditioning and subsequent verification testing to confirm that the device
226   drug-delivery function is maintained in accordance with the instructions for use. The following
227   sections provide considerations when developing a design verification approach. If an applicant
228   intends to use an alternative design verification approach, we recommend providing an
229   explanation of and rationale for the alternative approach and requesting Agency feedback in a
230   formal meeting or communication with FDA (see section VIII).
231
232              1.       Preconditioning
233
234   During the product’s lifecycle, the product can be exposed to multiple stressors prior to each use
235   that may influence performance of the device drug-delivery function. Preconditioning is a
236   method to simulate exposing the product to stressors prior to testing to which the product will
237   likely be exposed (including worst case scenarios) during storage, shipping, and before each use
238   (e.g., cleaning, reprocessing, storage (see section V.A.1.a), or repeat use, as applicable).
239   Applicants should identify preconditions applicable to the specific product, and verification
240   testing should assess the ability of the product to withstand those stressors. Applicants should
241   identify preconditions based on the risk of the product design, research and development
242   characterization testing, intended use, how the product will move from the manufacturer of the

430   VI.   CONTROL STRATEGIES FOR EDDCS
431
432   A control strategy is used to ensure that each lot of the final finished product is manufactured to
433   consistently meet its EDDC specification. For a given EDDC, an appropriate control strategy
434   might consist of one or more types of control steps at different stages of the manufacturing
435   process. Some control steps are performed earlier in the manufacturing process (e.g., upstream
436   controls such as in-process controls, control of process parameters, control of incoming
437   materials, and purchasing information). Other control steps are performed at the end of the
438   manufacturing process (e.g., a downstream control such as lot release testing).
439
440   The control strategy should be based on product and process understanding in combination with
441   risk management activities. 49 Product and process understanding can be drawn from sources
442   such as development of the device design and manufacturing process, structural analysis, design
443   and development verification testing, and prior knowledge from manufacture of similar devices.
444   Risk assessments can include consideration of factors such as the intended user (e.g., healthcare
445   provider, patient) and the context of use as well as the complexity of the EDDC (e.g.,
446   interdependencies at the component level) and the complexity and robustness of the
447   manufacturing process. The result of the risk assessment is used to determine the need for risk
448   controls; this information is further used to inform the type and extent of controls appropriate for
449   each EDDC. The amount of information regarding the control strategy to include in an
450   application should similarly be commensurate with the risks and associated impact.
451
452   Appropriate controls should be identified for each EDDC, considering the relevant features and
453   manufacturing process steps, and the documentation used to support these controls should be
454   identified. The control strategy can consist of one or more controls, implemented upstream,
455   downstream, or both, as previously stated. When developing an upstream control strategy for an
456   EDDC, it is important to consider the subsequent manufacturing process steps that can influence
457   the EDDC and provide a justification describing why the proposed upstream control is adequate
458   to ensure that the EDDC of the final finished product will meet its specification (i.e., after these
459   process steps occur). For certain EDDCs, purchasing information or an incoming
460   test/examination to confirm that the incoming product meets required specifications may be
461   sufficient (e.g., for the length of a syringe and needle that cannot change over the expiration
462   dating period). For other EDDCs, a singular downstream control (e.g., release testing) or a
463   combination of upstream and downstream controls may be appropriate.
464
465   The description of the control strategy should include information to support why the control or
466   combination of controls is considered to be adequate to ensure the EDDC will meet its
467   specification(s). In the context of the manufacturing process, this information is submitted as
468   part of the marketing application, submitted upon request, or available during facility inspection,
469   depending on application type (see section VII.B). Depending on the EDDC and its proposed
470   control(s), the supporting information might consist of risk analysis, engineering drawings,
471   development work, supporting process knowledge, in-process data, batch analysis (i.e., testing
472   the final finished product against the specification), or EDDC evaluation conducted as part of

732
733   During product development we strongly encourage applicants to begin developing and
734   characterizing the EDDCs that will need to be controlled in the marketed product and to begin to
735   develop a device control strategy for the product the applicant plans to market. We recommend
736   applicants submit the proposed EDDCs and control strategy for Agency feedback. The EDDCs
737   and control strategy could be discussed at multiple types of formal meetings or in other
738   communications with FDA, depending on application type. These meetings should be used
739   consistently with their intended purpose. Also, as appropriate for the type of submission and the
740   combination product lead center, the meeting request information may vary. 81
741
742   When seeking feedback on proposed EDDCs, the meeting background package should provide a
743   clear description of the product and its drug delivery function. This may include figures,
744   instructions for use, description of the intended user population, and context of use. The meeting
745   background package should provide specific EDDC questions and may request the participation
746   of an FDA office or staff with the appropriate expertise. If there are questions regarding the
747   control of EDDCs, the control strategy proposal should be provided. These meetings would
748   include discussion of the proposed control strategy and the proposed approach and timing of
749   EDDC-related verification and validation activities (e.g., type of studies and completion in
750   relation to pivotal clinical studies and marketing submissions). At a meeting type appropriate for
751   the product, we recommend applicants request feedback on the identified EDDCs (and

753   included in the marketing application. In addition, sponsors may request feedback on testing for
754   other related characteristics during EDDC testing.

830   APPENDIX D – EXAMPLE OF A CONTROL STRATEGY FOR THE EDDC OF
831   EXTENDED NEEDLE LENGTH FOR AN AUTOINJECTOR
832
833   The following is an example control strategy for the extended needle length for an autoinjector
834   assembled with the prefilled syringe (PFS) at the final finished combination product 121
835   manufacturing facility. The applicant is ultimately responsible for the control of the essential
836   drug delivery device performance characteristic (EDDC). This example shows how the
837   applicant can leverage information regarding the design and manufacturing from suppliers and
838   purchasing agreements (e.g., certificate of analysis), which are part of the purchasing information
839   (ISO 13485, subclause 7.4.2), 122 to adequately control the EDDC upstream.
840
841   In this example, supplier A is the syringe and needle manufacturer, supplier B is the autoinjector
842   subassembly manufacturer, and the applicant is the manufacturer of the final finished
843   combination product. The applicant performs the following manufacturing steps, which include:
844
845         (1) Manufacturing the primary container (PFS) – filling and stoppering the syringes from
846             supplier A with drug,
847         (2) Manufacturing the autoinjector – Inserting and securing the PFSs into autoinjector
848             subassemblies from supplier B, and
849         (3) Final packaging and labeling of autoinjector.
850
851   As shown in the table below:
852      • The length of the syringe and needle are not subject to change after filling or assembly
853         into the autoinjector, or over the autoinjector shelf life. Therefore, control of the length
854         of the syringe and needle is effective at supplier A (the syringe and needle manufacturer).
855
856         •   The needle cover retraction distance is also a dimensional function that is not impacted
857             by aging and subsequent assembly steps at the final manufacturing stage. Therefore,
858             control of the needle cover retraction distance is effective at supplier B.
859
860         •   The compatibility of the length of the syringe and needle with the needle cover retraction
861             distance is demonstrated by the applicant’s engineering analysis. This analysis validates
862             the extended needle length specification.
863
864   The applicant determines that the final extended needle length for the autoinjector is dependent
865   on the process steps to assemble the syringe into the subassemblies. Therefore, the applicant
866   provides a process risk assessment to support which steps impact this EDDC, the applicant’s in-
867   process work instructions relating to this process step, and process validation data to show this
868   process step is controlled and yields autoinjectors with adequate extended needle lengths.
869   See sections VII.B and VII.C for control strategy information to include in applications.
