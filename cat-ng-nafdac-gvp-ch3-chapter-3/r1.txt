CHAPTER 3



3.0 RISK MANAGEMENT SYSTEM

3.1 INTRODUCTION

Prior to marketing authorisation, the safety profile of a medicinal product is established
primarily from clinical trial data, which are limited by relatively small, selected study
populations, controlled conditions use and short durations of exposure as well as follow-up.
Therefore, some adverse reactions, particularly those that are rare, delayed or occur in specific
populations may only become evident after the product is used in routine clinical practice.

A medicinal product is authorised when its overall benefit–risk balance is considered
favourable for the approved indication(s) and target population based on the available evidence
at the time of authorisation. However, not all identified or potential risks are fully known at
this stage. Additional safety information may emerge during post-authorisation use thus
requiring continuous monitoring and evaluation of the medicinal benefit–risk profile.

Risk Management Plan (RMP) provides a structured approach to identifying, characterising,
preventing or minimising important risks while generating additional safety information where
needed. It supports the proactive management of a medicinal product's safety throughout its
lifecycle and helps ensure that its benefits continue to outweigh its risks.

3.2 PURPOSE

The purpose of this guideline is to provide Marketing Authorisation Holders (MAHs) with
guidance for establishing and maintaining an effective Risk Management System (RMS). It
also aids with preparing and implementing a Risk ManagePlan that will ensure the continuous
identification, characterisation, monitoring, prevention and minimisation of risks associated
with medicinal products thereby maintaining a positive benefit–risk balance throughout the
product lifecycle.

3.3 SCOPE

This guideline applies to all Marketing Authorisation Holders responsible for medicinal
products authorised by the National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control
(NAFDAC). It outlines the requirements for the development, submission, implementation,
evaluation and updating of the Risk Management Plans (RMPs) throughout the lifecycle of a
medicinal product.

The provisions of this guideline are consistent with the NAFDAC Good Pharmacovigilance
Practice (GVP) Regulations (2021) and reflect internationally recognised best practices

3.4      DEFINITIONS

Additional Pharmacovigilance Activities: Activities undertaken beyond routine

Additional Risk Minimisation Measures: Measures implemented in addition to routine

Adverse Drug Reaction (ADR): A response to a medicinal product that is noxious and

chemical properties.

Benefit–Risk Balance: An evaluation of the positive therapeutic effects of a medicinal
product in relation to its risks to individual patients and public health.

Benefit–Risk Evaluation: The ongoing assessment of the benefits and risks of a medicinal
product using available evidence to support regulatory decision-making.

3.5 Risk Management Framework

The Risk Management Framework provides a structured approach for the continuous
identification, assessment, management, communication and monitoring of risks associated
with medicinal products throughout its lifecycle. It supports the systematic implementation of
pharmacovigilance activities and risk minimisation measures to ensure that the benefit–risk
balance of a medicinal product remains favourable during routine clinical use

Planning the necessary pharmacovigilance activities to characterize the safety profile of the
medicinal product will be improved if it is based on specific issues identified from pre- or post-
authorization data and from pharmacological principles. However, the purpose of risk
identification and characterization is to allow for risk minimization or mitigation wherever
possible. Therefore, risk management has three stages which are inter-related and reiterative:

        a.       Safety profile characterisation: Characterization of the safety profile of the
                 medicinal product including what is known and not known
Effective Date: 16-07-2026                                           Doc. Ref. No: PV-GDL-003-02
Review Date: 15-07-2031

        b.       Pharmacovigilance planning: Planning of pharmacovigilance activities to
                 characterize risks and identify new risks and increase the knowledge in general
                 about the safety profile of the medicinal product

        c.       Risk minimization and effectiveness evaluation planning and implementation
        of risk minimization, mitigation and assessment of the effectiveness of these activities.
        (See Annex for Components of RMP)

The overall aim of risk management is to ensure that the benefits of a particular medicinal
product (or a series of medicinal products) exceed the risks by the greatest achievable margin
for the individual patient and for the target population as a whole.

3.6 MAH Responsibilities for Risk Management
3.6.1 In relation to the risk management of medicinal products, the MAH shall,

        a. Maintain an appropriate risk management system throughout the lifecycle of the
        medicinal product.

        b. Continuously monitor and critically evaluate the safety profile of the medicinal
        product including emerging safety information, new or changing risks and changes to
        the benefit-risk balance.

        c. Identify, characterise and evaluate identified and potential risks, as well as areas
        where important information is missing.

        d. Promptly notify NAFDAC of significant new safety information and submit updated
        RMP where such information results in, or may result in a significant change to the
        product's safety profile, risk management system or benefit-risk balance.

        e. Implement appropriate pharmacovigilance and risk minimisation activities that are
        proportionate to the identified risks and areas of missing information.

        f. Ensure that product information and risk communication materials remain accurate,
        current and consistent with the latest available safety information and promptly update
        and communicate such information where necessary.

        g. Evaluate the effectiveness of risk minimisation measures and submit the results to
        NAFDAC as required.

3.6.2 Preparation and maintenance of an RMP shall involve appropriately qualified and
experienced personnel from relevant scientific and technical disciplines as appropriate to the
nature and safety profile of the medicinal product. The expertise may include
pharmacovigilance,      clinical   pharmacology,        toxicology,       pharmacoepidemiology,
epidemiology, clinical medicine, regulatory affairs, biostatistics, risk communication and other
relevant disciplines.

3.6.3 Preparation of an RMP may involve personnel from different departments within the
MAH or appropriately qualified external experts or service providers. Where RMP activities
Effective Date: 16-07-2026                                         Doc. Ref. No: PV-GDL-003-02
Review Date: 15-07-2031

are outsourced, the MAH shall retain overall responsibility for the completeness, accuracy,
scientific integrity and regulatory compliance of the RMP and associated risk management
activities.

3.6.4 The RMP shall be prepared, reviewed and maintained under the oversight of appropriately
qualified and trained personnel with relevant pharmacovigilance and risk management
expertise. The MAH shall maintain documented evidence of the review and approval of the
RMP by the designated responsible person(s).

3.6.5 The MAH shall ensure the RMP is kept current throughout the lifecycle of the medicinal
product and shall submit revisions to NAFDAC when required by emerging safety information,
changes in the product's benefit-risk profile, completion of important pharmacovigilance or
risk minimisation activities, evaluation of the risk minimisation activities, regulatory
requirements or other circumstances that may materially affect the risk management system.

3.6.6 The MAH shall ensure that all commitments and activities described in the approved
RMP are implemented within the agreed timelines and that adequate records are maintained to
demonstrate implementation and effectiveness to NAFDAC upon request.

3.6.7 The MAH shall ensure that risk management activities adequately address the Nigerian
context, including local safety information, patterns of medicinal product use, healthcare
practices, identified or potential local safety concerns, implementation of risk minimisation
measures and evaluation of their effectiveness in Nigeria.



3.7. Structure of the risk management plan

The Risk Management Plan (RMP) is a live, dynamic and stand-alone document that must be
updated throughout the life cycle of the medicinal product. It should be reviewed regularly and
revised to reflect new safety information including updates arising from periodic safety update
reports/periodic benefit-risk evaluation reports (PSURs/PBRERs) and signal management
activities.

The RMP consists of seven elements:

            1. Product(s) Overview which provides basic information on the medicinal
               product(s), therapeutic area and target population.

            2. Safety Specification summarizes identified, potential and missing safety
               concerns.

            3. Pharmacovigilance Plan describes routine and additional pharmacovigilance
               activities to monitor and characterize risks.

            4. Plans for Post-Authorization Efficacy Studies outlines studies to further
               evaluate the product’s effectiveness in routine clinical practice.

            5. Risk Minimization Measures contain details of routine and additional measures
               to prevent or reduce risks including evaluation of their effectiveness.

            6. Summary of the Risk Management Plan provides a concise overview of key
               risks, activities and measures for quick reference.

            7. Annexes should contain supporting documentation, study protocols and
               additional data referenced in the RMP

3.8.2. Safety specification

The purpose of the Safety Specification is to provide a comprehensive synopsis of the safety
profile of the medicinal product(s) including the current knowledge, identified risks, potential
risks and other uncertainties relevant to the benefit–risk balance of the product(s). It should be
a summary of the important identified risks, potential risks and missing information of a
medicinal product. Missing information is defined as gaps in knowledge about a medicinal
product related to safety or use in particular patient populations which could be clinically
significant (see RMP Annex I). It should also address the populations potentially at risk (where
the product is likely to be used i.e. both labelled and off-labelled use) and outstanding safety
questions which warrant further investigation to refine understanding of the benefit-risk
balance during the post-authorization period. In the RMP, the safety specification will form the
basis of the pharmacovigilance plan and the risk minimization plan. The safety specification
consists of eight elements:

              i. Epidemiology of the indication(s) and target population(s)

              ii. Non-clinical part of the safety specification

            iii. Clinical trial exposure

            iv. Populations not studied in clinical trials

              v. Post-authorization experience

            vi. Additional requirements for the safety specification by the Agency

            vii. Identified and potential risks

          viii. Summary of the safety concerns
Effective Date: 16-07-2026                                             Doc. Ref. No: PV-GDL-003-02
Review Date: 15-07-2031

The elements of the safety specification included are only a guide. Elements which might be
incorporated but not limited to include:

            a)    Quality aspects if relevant in relation to the safety and efficacy of the product

            b)     The disposal of the product where it might pose a particular risk because of
                 remaining active substance (e.g. patches)

            c)    Innovative pharmaceutical forms or

            d)   Use a medical device.



3.8.2.1 Epidemiology of the indications and target population

The epidemiology of the indication(s) should be discussed. This discussion should include
incidence, prevalence, mortality as well as relevant co-morbidity and whenever possible be
stratified by age, sex, racial and/or ethnic origin. Differences in the epidemiology in the
different regions should be discussed where feasible (because the epidemiology of the
indication(s) may vary across regions) but the emphasis should be on the epidemiology in
Nigeria of the proposed indication. Information should be provided on the important co-
morbidities in the target population. For example: if a medicinal product is intended for treating
prostate cancer, the target population is likely to be men over the age of 50 years. Men over the
age of 50 are also at risk of myocardial infarction. To identify whether a medicinal product
might be increasing the risk of myocardial infarction, it is important to know how many cases
would be expected amongst prostate cancer patients (ideally) or men in the same age group not
taking the medicinal product. Estimation of the risk in the target population as compared with
the same age/sex group in the general population may be particularly important if the disease
itself increases the risk of a particular adverse event.

The RMP should include a statement of the intended purpose and impact of the product e.g.
whether it is intended to prevent disease, to prevent particular serious outcomes due to a
condition or to reduce progression of a chronic disease.

3.8.2.2. Non-clinical part of the safety specification

This should present a summary of the important non-clinical safety findings, for example:

   Toxicity (key issues identified from e.g. repeat-dose toxicity, reproductive/developmental
    toxicity, nephrotoxicity, hepatotoxicity, genotoxicity, carcinogenicity)

   General pharmacology (e.g. cardiovascular including QT interval prolongation as well as
    nervous system)

   Drug interactions

   Other toxicity-related information or data.
Effective Date: 16-07-2026                                           Doc. Ref. No: PV-GDL-003-02
Review Date: 15-07-2031

What constitutes an important safety finding will depend upon the medicinal product, the target
population and experience with other similar compounds or therapies in the same class.
Normally significant areas of toxicity (by target organ system) and the relevance of the findings
to the use in humans should be discussed. Quality aspects if relevant to safety (e.g. important
information on the active substance or its impurities, e.g. genotoxic impurities) should be
discussed. If a product is intended for use in women of childbearing age, data on the
reproductive/developmental toxicity should be explicitly mentioned and the implications for
use in this population discussed. Where the non-clinical safety finding could constitute an
important risk to the target population, it should be included as a safety concern. Special
populations depending upon the indication and target population, consideration should be given
to whether specific non-clinical data needs exist.




3.8.2.3. Clinical trial exposure

In order to assess the limitations of the human safety database, data on the patients studied in
clinical trials should be provided. This data should be provided in the most appropriate format
like tables/graphs. The size of the study population should be detailed using both numbers of
patients and where appropriate patient time (patient-years, patient-months) exposed to the
medicinal product.

This should be stratified for relevant categories and also by the type of trial (randomized
blinded trial population only and all clinical trial populations.) Stratifications would normally
include:

    a.    Age and gender

    b.   Indication

    c.   Dose

    d.   Racial origin

Duration of exposure should be provided either graphically by plotting numbers of patients
against time or in tabular format. The exposure of special populations (pregnant women, breast-
feeding women, renal impairment, hepatic impairment, cardiac impairment, sub-populations
with relevant genetic polymorphisms, immuno-compromised) should be provided as
appropriate. The degree of renal, hepatic or cardiac impairment should be specified as well as
the genetic polymorphism. These categories are only suggestions and tables/graphs should be
tailored to the product. For example, indication may not be a relevant stratification for a
medicinal product where only one indication has been studied and route of administration,
number of courses/immunizations or repeat administrations may be important categories to be
added.
Effective Date: 16-07-2026                                            Doc. Ref. No: PV-GDL-003-02
Review Date: 15-07-2031

When presenting age data, categories should be chosen which are relevant to the target
population. Broad artificial divisions which are not clinically relevant, such as <65 and >65
should be avoided. Pediatric data should be divided by categories; similarly, the data on elderly
patients should be considered for stratification into categories such as 65-74, 75-84 and
85+.The age strata should reflect the target population. For teratogenic drugs, stratification into
age categories relating to childbearing potential might be appropriate for the female population.

Data should not be presented by individual trials but should be pooled. Totals should be
provided for each table/graph as appropriate. Where patients have been enrolled in more than
one trial (e.g. open label extension study following a trial) they should only be included once
in the age/sex/ethnic origin tables. Where differences in the total numbers of patients arise
between tables, the tables should be annotated to reflect the reasons for discrepancy.

When the RMP is being submitted with an application for a new indication, a new
pharmaceutical form or route, the clinical trial data specific to the application should be
presented separately at the start of the chapter as well as being included in the summary tables
(as described above) representing pooled data across all indications.



3.8.2.4 Populations not studied in clinical trials

Limitations of the clinical trials should be presented in terms of the relevance of inclusion and
exclusion criteria in relation to the target population. This is particularly important when
exclusion criteria are not proposed as contraindications for the drug. Lists of

3.8.2.6.3 Potential for misuse, abuse or use for illegal purposes

The MAH should assess the potential for the medicinal product to be intentionally misused for

product formulation, pack size restrictions or controlled distribution to reduce the risk of such
misuse.

3.8.2.6.4 Potential for medication errors

For the purpose of the RMP, medication errors are unintended errors in the prescribing,
dispensing, preparation or administration of a medicinal product by healthcare professionals,
patients or caregivers. Medication errors may result in patient harm and should be assessed as
part of the Safety Specification. They fall broadly into 4 categories:

1.      Wrong medication: Administration, dispensing or prescribing of a medicinal product
        different from the one intended or prescribed for the patient. This may occur due to
        confusion between drug names, packaging or prescribing mistakes.

2.      Wrong dose (including strength, form, concentration, amount): Administration of a
        dose that differs from the prescribed or intended dose including errors in strength,
        dosage form, concentration or the amount of medicine given (e.g. giving too much or
        too little of a medicine).

3.      Wrong route of administration: Administration of a medicinal product by a route
        different from the one intended or authorized (e.g. giving a medicine intravenously
        instead of orally).

4.      Wrong patient: Administration of a medicinal product to a patient for whom the
        medicine was not prescribed or intended.

MAH should consider routinely the likelihood of medication errors. In particular, they should
assess prior to marketing the common sources of medication errors. If a product has potential
for serious harm when administered by an incorrect route, consideration should be given as to
how such administration can be avoided. This is particularly important when it is common
practice to administer the product at the same time as other medicinal products given by the
hazardous route. In this situation, medication errors should be included as a safety concern.

The need for visual (or physical) differentiation between strengths of the same medicinal
product and between other medicinal products commonly administered or taken at the same
time should be discussed. In addition, if there are other products containing the same active
substance on the market with formulations which are not proven to be bioequivalent, measures
to avoid medication error should be discussed and appropriate risk minimisation activities
proposed.

When a medicinal product is likely to be used by a visually impaired population, special
consideration should be given to the potential for medication error. Where appropriate,
medication error should be included as a safety concern and appropriate risk minimisation
measures proposed to address the possibility of medication error due to visual impairment.
Consideration should be given to the prevention of accidental, ingestion or other unintended
use by children.

Medication errors identified during product development including clinical trials should be
discussed and information on the errors, their potential cause(s) and possible remedies given.

If the formulation or strength of a product is being changed, medication error should be

relation to changes to the presentation, pack size, route of administration or release
characteristics of the medicinal product.

If the product is to be administered with a medicinal device (integrated or not), consideration
should be given to any safety concerns which could represent a risk to the patient (medicinal
device malfunction).




3.8.2.6.5 Potential for off-label use

The potential for off-label use should be discussed. Off-label use relates to situations where the
medicinal product is intentionally used for a medicinal purpose not in accordance with the
authorised product information. This is particularly relevant where a medicinal product has an
indication restricted to a subset of the population within a disease area or there are situations
where the medicinal product must not be given for safety reasons. The potential for use in other
disease areas should also be considered where this is likely. The MAH should consider data
comparing actual use with the authorised use of the medicinal product in other markets.

Where the medicinal product has been marketed, the MAH should provide cumulative data on

When deciding which measure to use for exposure data, it is important to consider the way a
medicinal product is used. Exposure data based on the number of kilograms of medicinal

medicinal products used chronically, the appropriate measure may be patient years of use.
However, when use is typically limited and utilisation is determined by pack size (e.g. a course
of antibiotics), a simple count of packs sold may be more appropriate.

additional stratification of the data such as by age group or approved indication. Where the
medicinal product is used for different indications with different dosing regimens or other
relevant characteristics, the MAH should, where possible, provide exposure data accordingly.
A more accurate breakdown of drug exposure based on market research should be provided
where possible. If a drug utilisation study has been performed, the MAH should provide a
detailed breakdown of patient exposure based on market research or drug utilisation studies.
Such data should reflect the use of the medicinal product in routine clinical practice and support
the assessment of its safety profile.

reference should be made in this section as to which risks and populations are affected.

3.8.3. Pharmacovigilance plan

The purpose of the pharmacovigilance plan is to describe how the MAH will further identify,
characterise and monitor the risks outlined in the safety specification. Routine
pharmacovigilance activities apply by default to all medicinal products however, additional
pharmacovigilance activities should be implemented where routine activities are insufficient to
adequately address specific safety concerns. Safety concerns and the corresponding
pharmacovigilance activities should be clearly linked and traceable within the RMP safety
concern activity grid. The Pharmacovigilance plan provides a structured plan for;

1.      The identification of new safety concerns

2.      Further characterisation of known safety concerns including elucidation of risk factors,
        increased frequency of safety concern, change in pattern of a known safety concern

3.      The investigation of whether a potential safety concern is real or not

4.      How missing information will be sought.

5.      It does NOT include actions intended to reduce, prevent or mitigate risks

The pharmacovigilance plan should be based on the safety concerns summarised in section
3.8.2 of the safety specification. Early discussions between the Agency and the MAH are
recommended to identify which additional pharmacovigilance activities are needed. It is
important to note that only a proportion of risks are likely to be foreseeable therefore, signal
detection which is part of routine pharmacovigilance will be an important element in
identifying new risks for all products.

Pharmacovigilance activities can be divided into routine pharmacovigilance activities and
additional pharmacovigilance activities. For each safety concern, the MAH should list their
planned pharmacovigilance activities for that concern. Pharmacovigilance plans should be
proportionate to the risks of the product. If routine pharmacovigilance is considered sufficient
for post authorisation safety monitoring, without the need for additional actions (e.g. safety
studies) “routine pharmacovigilance” should be entered against the safety concern.

3.8.3.1. Routine pharmacovigilance activities

Routine pharmacovigilance is the set of activities required to fulfil the legal requirements for
Pharmacovigilance Regulations of the Agency. The Pharmacovigilance System Master File
(Chapter 2 confirm) contains details of the system and processes each MAH has in place to
achieve this.

In certain situations, the Agency may make recommendations for specific activities related to
the collection, collation, assessment and reporting of spontaneous reports of adverse reactions
which differ from the normal requirements for routine pharmacovigilance (Chapter 1 confirm).

3.8.3.2. Specific adverse reaction follow-up questionnaires

If the MAH uses or is requested by the Agency to use specific questionnaires to collect
additional information on adverse reactions of special interest, copies of the questionnaires
Effective Date: 16-07-2026                                           Doc. Ref. No: PV-GDL-003-02
Review Date: 15-07-2031

should be included in the Annex of the RMP. The use of such questionnaires for the follow-up
of suspected adverse reactions is considered a routine pharmacovigilance activity. Where
possible, MAHs are encouraged to use standardised questionnaires to promote consistency and
reduce the reporting burden on healthcare professionals.



3.8.3.3. Additional pharmacovigilance activities

Additional pharmacovigilance activities may include non-clinical studies, clinical trials or non-
interventional studies undertaken to further characterise or investigate a safety concern.
Depending on the nature of the safety concern, its level of characterisation and the feasibility
of further investigation, additional pharmacovigilance activities may or may not be required.
The MAH should consider additional pharmacovigilance activities where routine
pharmacovigilance is insufficient to adequately assess a safety concern. This may include
situations where long-term safety data are limited, pre-clinical findings require further
evaluation or the background incidence of an event in the target population makes it difficult
to determine whether the medicinal product increases the risk. If there is uncertainty regarding
the need for additional pharmacovigilance activities, the MAH should consult the Agency.

The objectives of additional pharmacovigilance activities should be tailored to the safety
concern being addressed. Important identified and potential risks may include further
characterising the risk, estimating its frequency, identifying risk factors, evaluating the effect
of dose or duration of exposure or assessing causality. For missing information, the objective
may be to investigate a potential risk or provide evidence to support its absence.

Studies included in the Pharmacovigilance Plan should address the safety concerns identified
in the Safety Specification or evaluate the effectiveness of risk minimisation measures. The
MAH should include all relevant post-authorisation safety studies initiated, managed or funded
by the MAH whether conducted voluntarily or in response to a requirement from the Agency.
Additional pharmacovigilance studies included in the Pharmacovigilance Plan such as
pharmacoepidemiology, pharmacokinetic, clinical or non-clinical studies should be designed
and conducted in accordance with applicable good scientific and regulatory practices. The
MAH remains responsible for the scientific quality and validity of the study protocols including
those discussed with the Agency.

3.8.3.4 Studies to measure the effectiveness of risk minimisation measures

Studies undertaken to evaluate the effectiveness of risk minimisation measures including PASS
shall be described in the Pharmacovigilance Plan and cross-referenced in the Risk
Minimisation Plan for the relevant safety concern(s).

3.8.3.6 Registries

A registry is an organised system that uses observational methods to collect uniform data on
specified outcomes in a population defined by a particular disease, condition or exposure. A
registry can be used as a data source within which studies can be performed. Entry in a registry
is generally defined either by diagnosis of a disease (disease registry) or prescription of a drug
(exposure registry).

3.8.4 Plans for Post-Authorisation Efficacy Studies
The benefit-risk assessment of a medicinal product at the time of marketing authorisation is
based on available clinical and other relevant evidence. Efficacy is expected to remain
reasonably consistent following authorisation, additional evidence may be required where there
is uncertainty regarding the maintenance, magnitude, duration or generalisability of the
treatment benefit in routine clinical practice. Post-authorisation efficacy studies are not
routinely required for all medicinal products. However, NAFDAC may require or the MAH
may propose such studies where they are necessary to address an identified uncertainty
regarding efficacy or to further evaluate the benefits of a medicinal product. Such studies may
be particularly relevant where:

        a. the duration of available efficacy data is limited in relation to the expected duration
        of treatment.
        b. long-term maintenance of efficacy requires further evaluation.
        c. efficacy may vary substantially between different patient populations or clinical
        settings.
        d. there are important uncertainties regarding the generalisability of clinical trial results
        to routine clinical practice in Nigeria.
        e. the product is intended for a population for which clinical evidence is limited,
        including certain paediatric populations.
        f. the medicinal product is an advanced therapy medicinal product or another product
        for which long-term follow-up of therapeutic benefit is clinically relevant.
        g. new evidence raises a material uncertainty regarding the established benefit of the
        medicinal product.
Where a post-authorisation efficacy study is required or proposed as part of the RMP, the MAH
shall describe the objective, rationale, study design, population, endpoints, data sources,
timelines and expected outcomes and explain how the results will contribute to the evaluation
of the product's benefit-risk balance.

        c. the key clinical studies supporting the authorised indication.

        d. the relevant study populations, treatment regimens and duration of follow-up.

        e. the principal efficacy endpoints and the key results.

3.8.5. Risk minimisation measures

Based on the Safety Specification, the MAH should determine the appropriate risk
minimisation measures for each safety concern. The risk minimisation plan should provide
details of the risk minimisation measures which will be taken to reduce the risks associated
Effective Date: 16-07-2026                                           Doc. Ref. No: PV-GDL-003-02
Review Date: 15-07-2031

with individual safety concerns. It is not possible to provide precise guidance on which risk
minimisation activity should be used in a given situation as each safety concern needs to be
considered on a case-by-case basis and will depend upon the severity of the risk, the healthcare
setting, the indication, the pharmaceutical form and the target population. A safety concern
may be addressed using more than one risk minimisation measure.

For active substances where there are individual products with substantially different
indications or target populations, it may be appropriate to have a risk minimisation plan specific
to each product. Example, when multiple risk minimisation plans could be considered include:

        i. An active substance available as both prescription-only and non-prescription
        medicinal products.
        ii Medicinal products with significant safety risks that are used across different medical
        specialties may require additional educational materials tailored to the needs of the
        intended healthcare professionals, particularly where specialist safety knowledge
        cannot be assumed. For example, a medicinal product associated with a significant risk
        of QT prolongation may not require educational materials when used exclusively by
        cardiologists but may require such materials when prescribed by healthcare
        professionals in non-specialist settings, where awareness of QT prolongation and drug
        interactions may be limited.
Active substances with significant safety risks that vary across different target populations may
require population-specific risk minimisation measures.

Risk minimisation activities may consist of routine risk minimisation (e.g. measures associated
with locally authorised product labelling) or additional risk minimisation activities (e.g. Direct
Healthcare Professional Communications/educational materials/controlled distribution
systems). All risk minimisation measures should have a clearly identifiable objective and
should be reviewed at regular intervals and their effectiveness assessed and feedback provided
to the Agency.

3.8.5.1. Routine risk minimisation

Routine risk minimisation activities are those which apply to every medicinal product. These
relate to:

        a)    The summary of product characteristics (SmPC)

        b)   The labelling

        c)    The package leaflet

        d)   The pack size(s)

        e)    The legal status of the product

3.8.5.1.1. Summary of product characteristics (SmPC) and package leaflet (PL)

The SmPC and PL are important tools for risk minimisation as they constitute a controlled and
standardised format for informing healthcare practitioners and patients about the medicinal

Review Date: 15-07-2031

product. The design of the packaging as well as the formulation itself may play an important
role in preventing medication error.

3.8.5.1.2 Pack size

Pack size is an aspect of product presentation that may contribute to the safe and appropriate
use of a medicinal product and may be considered as a routine or additional risk minimisation
measure where justified by the product's safety profile.

The MAH shall consider if the proposed pack size and number of dosage units may influence;

        a. the potential for overdose or accidental ingestion.

        b. prolonged use without appropriate clinical review or monitoring.

        c. accumulation or inappropriate storage of the medicinal product.

        d. access to medicines with potential for misuse, abuse or diversion

        e. the safe use of the medicinal product by patients, caregivers and healthcare
        professionals.

Where appropriate, limiting the number of dosage units per pack may encourage more regular
clinical review, monitoring or reassessment of treatment especially for medicinal products
requiring periodic laboratory or clinical monitoring.

For medicinal products associated with a significant risk of overdose, misuse, abuse, diversion
and accidental ingestion or other safety concerns that may be mitigated through control of the
quantity supplied, the MAH should consider whether smaller or appropriately restricted pack
sizes would reduce the risk. If pack size is proposed as a risk minimisation measure, the MAH
shall provide a clear scientific and public health justification for the proposed pack size and
describe how its effectiveness will be monitored.

If more than one pack size is authorised or proposed, the MAH shall justify the range of pack
sizes in relation to the authorised indications, dosing regimens, treatment duration and
identified or potential risks.

NAFDAC may require modification or restriction of pack sizes where this is considered
necessary to minimise an identified or potential risk and is proportionate to the level of risk.

3.8.5.1.3 Legal status

The legal status and conditions under which a medicinal product is supplied, prescribed,
dispensed, administered or used may constitute an important risk minimisation measure where
restrictions are necessary to reduce identified or potential risks associated with its use or
misuse.

When a Certificate of Registration is granted, NAFDAC may specify conditions or restrictions
relating to the supply, prescribing, dispensing, administration or use of a medicinal product,

where justified by the product's safety profile and in accordance with applicable regulatory
requirements. Some of the conditions may include restrictions on:

a. the availability of the whether the medicinal product on prescription or without prescription.
b. the healthcare professional authorised to prescribe or administer the medicinal product.
c. the healthcare setting in which the medicinal product may be prescribed, dispensed or
administered like a specified healthcare facility.
d. requirements for specialist supervision or involvement of appropriately qualified healthcare
professionals
e. conditions relating to patient monitoring, laboratory testing or clinical assessment before or
during treatment
f. other conditions necessary to ensure the safe and appropriate use of the medicinal product.
For medicinal products available only on prescription, additional restrictions may be applied if
warranted by the nature and severity of the identified or potential risks. Such restrictions may
include requirements for specialist prescribing, administration in specified healthcare settings
or other controlled conditions of supply and use. Where the legal status or conditions of supply
and use are proposed as an additional risk minimisation measure, the MAH shall provide a
clear scientific and public health justification, describe how the measure will be implemented
and where appropriate, propose measures to monitor its implementation and effectiveness.

The MAH shall ensure the product information, packaging, labelling and relevant risk
communication materials are consistent with the approved legal status and conditions of supply
and use. If available evidence indicates that the existing legal status or conditions of supply and
use are insufficient to adequately manage an identified or potential risk, NAFDAC may require
additional or modified restrictions, proportionate to the level of risk and taking into account the
availability of alternative risk minimisation measures.

3.8.5.1.4 Restricted medical prescription

A medicinal product may be subjected to restricted medical prescription so there is control over
who may initiate or prescribe treatment, the healthcare setting in which the product is supplied
or administered or the level of specialist supervision required is necessary to minimise
identified or potential risks. To determine if a medicinal product should be subjected to
restricted medical prescription, the following factors should be considered;

1.      Hospital or specialised treatment: The medicinal product has pharmaceutical or clinical
        characteristics or presents safety or public health considerations that require treatment
        to be initiated and/or administered in a hospital or other appropriately equipped
        healthcare facility.
2.      Specialised diagnosis: The medicinal product is intended for the treatment of conditions
        that require diagnosis in a hospital or healthcare institution with adequate diagnostic
        facilities, even where subsequent administration or follow-up may be undertaken in
        another appropriate setting.
3.      Serious adverse reactions requiring specialist supervision: The medicinal product is
        intended for outpatient treatment but may cause serious or potentially life-threatening
        adverse reactions, such treatment should be initiated or prescribed by an appropriately
        qualified specialist and require specific clinical supervision or monitoring during
        treatment.

Where restricted medical prescription is identified as a risk minimisation measure, the MAH
shall describe in the RMP:

        a. the safety concern(s) necessitating the restriction.

        b. the proposed conditions of prescribing, dispensing, administration or monitoring.

        c. the healthcare professionals and/or healthcare settings to which the restriction
        applies.

        d. the measures required to support implementation of the restriction

        e. the measures for monitoring and evaluating the implementation and effectiveness of
        the restriction.

The level of restriction should be proportionate to the identified or potential risk and should be
reviewed when new safety information becomes available.

3.8.5.1.5. Special medical prescription

A medicinal product may be subject to special medical prescription where its characteristics,
composition or potential for abuse, dependence, misuse or diversion require additional controls
over its prescribing, supply, dispensing or use. To determine whether a medicinal product
should be subject to special medical prescription, the following factors should be considered:

1.      Narcotic or psychotropic substances: The medicinal product contains a quantity not
        otherwise exempted under applicable legislation, a substance classified as a narcotic or
        psychotropic substance under applicable national requirements.
2.      Potential for abuse, dependence or illegal use: The medicinal product, if incorrectly
        or inappropriately used, may present a substantial risk of abuse, dependence, addiction,
        diversion or misuse for illegal purposes.
3.      Precautionary classification: The medicinal product contains a substance which,
        because of its novel properties, pharmacological characteristics or emerging safety
        information, may warrant special medical prescription as a precautionary measure due
        to a potential for abuse, dependence, misuse or diversion.

Where special medical prescription is considered necessary as part of risk management, the
MAH shall provide appropriate justification and describe the relevant conditions for
prescribing, dispensing, supply, administration as well as monitoring of use. Likewise, the
MAH shall also consider if additional risk minimisation measures are necessary to support the
safe use of the medicinal product. These measures may include controlled distribution,
prescriber or dispenser education, patient information, treatment agreements, prescription
controls, monitoring of use or other measures proportionate to the identified or potential risk.

The need for special medical prescription and associated risk minimisation measures should be
reviewed when significant new safety information becomes available or when there is a change
in the benefit-risk profile or pattern of use of the medicinal product. It is important to note
where routine risk minimisation measures are insufficient to adequately manage an identified
or potential risk, additional risk minimisation measures may be required.”

3.8.5.2 Additional risk minimisation activities

Additional risk minimisation measures (aRMMs) are risk minimisation measures that are
required beyond routine risk minimisation activities to address specific identified or potential
risks or important missing information associated with a medicinal product. aRMMs should be
proposed if routine risk minimisation measures are insufficient to adequately manage the
relevant safety concern and where the additional measures are considered necessary to support
the safe and effective use of the medicinal product. The measures must be proportionate to the
risk, evidence-based, feasible and capable of being implemented and evaluated within the
Nigerian healthcare setting. Where aRMM is proposed, the MAH shall:

        a. clearly identify the safety concern or missing information that necessitates the
        measure.

        b. provide a clear scientific and public health justification for the proposed measure.

        c. describe the objectives, target population, intended users and implementation
        arrangements in sufficient detail.

        d. ensure that the measure is developed, reviewed and implemented by appropriately
        qualified personnel.

        e. describe how implementation and where appropriate, effectiveness will be monitored
        and evaluated.

        f. specify the criteria and circumstances under which the measure may be modified,
        strengthened or discontinued.

3.8.5.2.1 Risk Minimisation Communication Materials
aRMMs involve communication materials intended to reinforce or clarify information
contained in the approved Summary of Product Characteristics (SmPC), package leaflet or
other approved product information. Hence, Risk minimisation communication materials
should;

        a. be clearly focused on the specific risk minimisation objective and provide accurate,
        clear as well as consistent safety information.

        c. be targeted to the relevant healthcare professionals, patients, caregivers or other
        intended recipients.

        d. be consistent with the approved product information and other regulatory
        requirements.

        e. be clearly distinguishable from promotional or marketing materials.

Risk minimisation communication materials must not contain promotional claims or content
that could encourage inappropriate prescribing, dispensing or use of the medicinal product.

3.8.5.2.2 Engagement with NAFDAC
The MAH should engage with NAFDAC as early as practicable where aRMMs are likely to be
required especially if their implementation may need to be adapted to the Nigerian healthcare
system, regulatory framework, healthcare delivery arrangements or available monitoring
infrastructure. The MAH should discuss with NAFDAC:

        a. the proposed risk minimisation objectives and target population

        b. the suitability and feasibility of the proposed intervention within Nigeria

        c. the content and format of educational or communication materials

        d. implementation arrangements and responsibilities

        e. the proposed approach for monitoring implementation and evaluating effectiveness.

Early engagement does not replace the formal regulatory review and approval processes
applicable to the RMP or associated risk minimisation materials.

3.8.5.2.3 Engagement with Healthcare Professionals and Patients
If aRMMs are directed towards healthcare professionals, patients or caregivers, the MAH
should where feasible and appropriate seek input from relevant stakeholders during the
development of the measures. This engagement may help ensure that the proposed materials
and interventions are understandable, relevant, practical and appropriate for the intended users
and healthcare setting. Stakeholder input should be appropriately documented and the MAH
should consider and address relevant feedback when finalising the proposed aRMMs.

3.8.5.3. Educational material

Educational material should be non-promotional. It is recommended that communication
experts, patients and healthcare professionals are consulted on the design and wording of
educational material and that it is piloted before release for use.

The final version of the educational material will need to be approved by the Agency who will
check and ensure that the material contains the key elements in an appropriate design and
format and is not promotional.

For public health reasons, where multiple MAHs have medicinal products containing the same
active substance, the Agency may require educational materials to have, as far as practicable,
a similar layout, content, colour scheme and format to minimise the risk of patient confusion.
This requirement may also apply to other patient-facing materials like patient alert cards and
patient monitoring cards. Accordingly, MAHs are strongly encouraged to avoid the use of
company logos, trademarks or other proprietary or patented elements in educational materials
where these may hinder standardisation or create confusion among patients and healthcare
professionals.

3.8.5.5. Evaluation of the Effectiveness of Risk Minimisation Activities
The effectiveness of RMMs should be evaluated throughout the lifecycle of a medicinal
product to determine if the intended objectives are being achieved, whether the burden of
adverse reactions is being reduced and if the overall benefit-risk balance remains favourable.

The evaluation should be proportionate to the identified safety concern and the nature of the
risk minimisation measure. Where applicable, the MAH should use appropriate process,
intermediate and outcome indicators supported by relevant data sources to assess the
effectiveness of the measures.

        a) Process indicators: These assess whether the risk minimisation measure has been
        implemented as planned. Examples include the distribution and availability of
        educational materials, number and proportion of targeted healthcare professionals
        reached or trained and implementation of required monitoring or prescribing
        procedures.

        b) Intermediate indicators: These assess changes in knowledge, awareness,
        understanding, behaviour among the target population including healthcare
        professionals and patients. Examples include healthcare professional knowledge of the
        identified risk, adherence to recommended monitoring requirements, appropriate
        prescribing or dispensing practices and patient understanding of relevant precautions.

        c) Outcome indicators: These assess if the identified risk has been reduced following
        implementation of the risk minimisation measures. Examples include changes in the
Effective Date: 16-07-2026                                            Doc. Ref. No: PV-GDL-003-02
Review Date: 15-07-2031

        incidence, frequency, severity or reporting rate of the relevant adverse reactions or other
        safety outcomes.

The MAH should define the objectives, indicators, targets, data sources, methods of assessment
and frequency of evaluation for each additional risk minimisation activity. The methodology
should be scientifically justified and sufficiently sensitive to detect meaningful changes in the
effectiveness of the measure. Likewise, if the RMP is updated, the risk minimisation plan
should include an evaluation of the impact of routine and/or additional risk minimisation
activities. Results of studies, surveys, audits, utilisation studies or other formal assessments
conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of risk minimisation activities should be submitted to
the Agency when available.

The MAH should critically assess the results and provide an explanation of factors that may
have contributed to the success, partial effectiveness or failure of the risk minimisation
activities. Where the available evidence indicates that a risk minimisation strategy is
ineffective, insufficient or imposes an excessive or undue burden on patients, healthcare
professionals or the healthcare system, the MAH should propose appropriate corrective or
alternative measures. Any important identified or potential safety concern for which risk
minimisation is required, the MAH should state if the existing measures remain adequate and
whether additional, modified or alternative risk minimisation activities are necessary.

If risk minimisation measures are demonstrated to be insufficient to adequately control a safety
concern and maintain a favourable benefit-risk balance, the Agency may require further
regulatory action. Such action may include strengthening or modifying risk minimisation
measures, restricting the use of the medicinal product to appropriately selected patients or
healthcare settings, suspending or revoking the marketing authorisation or even withdrawal of
the product from the Nigerian market.

3.8.5.6. Gap analysis

If the safety data for a specific product is obtained from a different region, the MAH should
include a gap analysis (population, practice, genetics, comorbidity, co‑medication) of the safety
specifications, pharmacovigilance plan and risk minimization activities in the risk management
plan. The gap analysis should discuss the differences between RMP developed for Nigeria and
that of the other region, highlighting and justifying any gaps or missing information. Where
appropriate, the evaluation should take into account the Nigerian healthcare context and any
factors that may influence the implementation or effectiveness of the risk minimisation
activities. These may include availability and accessibility of healthcare services, prescribing
and dispensing practices, healthcare professional coverage, patient access and understanding
of educational materials, availability of required laboratory or monitoring facilities and other
relevant healthcare-system factors. MAHs should provide data that demonstrate the
implementation and effectiveness of the measures within Nigeria.

3.8.6. Summary of activities in the risk management plan by medicinal product

summary of the RMP will be evaluated during the assessment of the RMP.

This is a scientific summary written for unprofessional reader to fulfil the requirements in the
regulation. In situations where the RMP covers more than one product, a separate section

RMP should be put into context with a very concise and focussed description of the benefits of
the medicinal product. Technical terms, scientific abbreviations or acronyms should be avoided
or explained in full if deemed necessary.

The summary of the sections, should contain the following information:

        a. Overview of disease epidemiology

        b. Summary of treatment benefits

        c. Unknowns relating to treatment benefits

        d. Summary of safety concerns:

                  i. Important identified risks

                 iii. Missing information: Summary of risk minimisation activities by safety
                 concern

                 iv. Planned post authorisation development plan;

                 v. Studies which are a condition of the Certificate of Registration

The summaries in the Risk Management Plan (RMP) need to be concise, targeted and aligned

3.8.7.1. Principles for assessment of risk management plans

The principle points which need to be considered when preparing or reviewing a risk
management plan for a medicinal product are.

3.8.7.2. Safety specification

a.    Have all appropriate parts of the safety specification been included?

b.    Have all appropriate data been reviewed when compiling the safety specification, i.e. are
     there important (outstanding) issues from other sections of the dossier which have not been
     discussed in the safety specification?

c.     If parts of the target population have not been studied, have appropriate safety concerns
     in relation to potential risks and missing information been included?

d.      What are the limitations of the safety database and what reassurance does it provide
     regarding the safety profile of the medicinal product?

e.    Are there specific risks in addition to those addressed under ICH-E2E, e.g. off-label use,
     misuse and abuse, transmission of infectious disease, medication error, etc.?

f.    Does the safety specification provide a true reflection of the safety concerns (i.e. important
     identified risks, important potential risks and missing information) with the product?

g.    If a generic or hybrid application, have all safety concerns from the reference medicinal
     product been included in the safety specification?

h.     Does its place in the therapeutic armamentarium as described concur with the intended
     indication and current medicinal practice?

3.8.7.8. Quality systems and record management

Several qualified experts may contribute to the preparation of the Risk Management Plan
(RMP), the Marketing Authorisation Holder retains the ultimate responsibility for the quality,
accuracy, completeness and scientific integrity of the RMP. The Qualified Person responsible
for Pharmacovigilance in Nigeria (QPPV) should be fully aware of the content of the RMP and
have sufficient authority and oversight to ensure that it accurately reflects the safety profile of
the medicinal product and the pharmacovigilance and risk minimisation activities being
implemented in Nigeria.

The Marketing Authorisation Holder (MAH) is responsible for ensuring that the RMP is kept
up to date whenever new safety information or other relevant information becomes available.
The MAH should apply the quality principles set out throughout the preparation,
implementation, review and maintenance of the RMP.

The MAH should maintain appropriate records of all RMP submissions to the Agency
including the dates of submission and a clear description of significant changes made between
successive versions. The submitted RMPs, supporting documentation, and records relating to
the information contained in the RMP should be retained and made available to the Agency
upon request and may be subject to regulatory inspection or audit.
