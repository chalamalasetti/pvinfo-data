15   I.       INTRODUCTION
16
17   This guidance assists sponsors in the design and analysis of studies that assess the influence of
18   impaired hepatic function, herein referred to as hepatic impairment (HI), on the
19   pharmacokinetics and, where appropriate, the pharmacodynamics of a drug. 2,3
20
21   This guidance provides recommendations on the following topics:
22
23        •   When studies to assess the influence of HI on the pharmacokinetics or
24            pharmacodynamics of a drug should be conducted and when they may not be warranted
25
26        •   Design and conduct of studies to characterize the effects of HI on the pharmacokinetics
27            of a drug, including the population pharmacokinetic (popPK) approach
28
29        •   Data analysis and interpretation of these study results, as well as the evaluation of
30            relationships between measures of hepatic function and pharmacokinetic and/or
31            pharmacodynamic parameters

40   II.     BACKGROUND
41
42   The liver is a major organ involved in the elimination of drugs through metabolism and/or biliary
43   excretion of unchanged drugs or their metabolites. Liver disease can change these metabolic and
44   excretory pathways by impacting drug metabolizing enzymes and transporters. 4,5 In addition, the
45   absorption and distribution of drugs can also be affected by liver disease. The presence and
46   magnitude of portal hypertension in patients with cirrhosis can further affect a drug’s
47   pharmacokinetics to varying degrees because of alterations in hepatic arterial and portal blood
48   flow as well as intra- and extra-hepatic shunting. Portal hypertension can also affect systemic
49   circulation, leading to changes in the volume of distribution of a drug. In patients with advanced
50   cirrhosis, protein binding can be affected due to associated hypoalbuminemia. Transporter
51   function can also be affected in patients with liver disease. In summary, liver disease can lead to
52   changes in a drug’s pharmacokinetics, to a degree necessitating different recommendations for
53   use of the drug (e.g., a different recommended dosage in patients with HI).
54
55   Although therapeutic peptides and proteins are not generally considered to be substrates of drug
56   transporters or drug metabolizing enzymes of the cytochrome P450 family or, their
57   pharmacokinetics can be altered by other mechanisms in patients with HI. Altered
58   pharmacokinetics of monoclonal antibodies and antibody-drug conjugates has been reported in
59   patients with HI and should be considered during drug development. 6
60
61   Liver disease can also alter the pharmacodynamic effects of a drug, potentially affecting safety
62   (e.g., increased sedation if patients with hepatic encephalopathy are treated with
63   benzodiazepines; impaired synthesis of coagulation factors in patients with cirrhosis may
64   increase the bleeding risk of direct-acting oral anticoagulants). Liver disease can also alter
65   kidney function (e.g., hepatorenal syndrome), potentially leading to changes in the
66   pharmacokinetics of a drug and its metabolites even when the liver is not primarily responsible
67   for their elimination. The specific impact of various phenotypes of liver disease (e.g.,
68   hepatocellular vs. cholestatic, acute vs. chronic) on hepatic function is still not completely

 72   Patients with HI have often been excluded from clinical trials, even when they are part of the

 79   pharmacokinetics and, where appropriate, on the pharmacodynamics of a drug during drug
 80   development to help determine if risk mitigation is needed with the use of the drug in patients
 81   with HI (e.g., a modified dosage, increased monitoring of adverse events).

 84   III.       DECIDING WHETHER TO CONDUCT A DEDICATED PHARMACOKINETIC
 85              STUDY IN PATIENTS WITH HEPATIC IMPAIRMENT
 86
 87              A.       When a Dedicated Pharmacokinetic Study Should be Conducted
 88
 89   A pharmacokinetic study in subjects with HI is recommended if any of the following conditions
 90   are met:
 91
 92          •   Hepatic metabolism and/or excretion accounts for >30 percent of the elimination of
 93              absorbed parent drug or active metabolites. 9
 94
 95          •   Minimal drug concentration changes may lead to clinically significant differences in
 96              safety or effectiveness.
 97
 98          •   The elimination pathways of the drug are not adequately characterized.
 99
100          •   The drug is intended to treat patients with liver disease.
101
102          •   The drug is likely to be used in patients with HI.

104             B.     When a Dedicated Pharmacokinetic Study May Not Be Warranted
105
106   If HI is not likely to alter the pharmacokinetics of a drug sufficiently to necessitate a different
107   recommended dosage in patients with HI, a dedicated pharmacokinetic HI study is generally not
108   warranted, unless clinical concerns suggest otherwise. The following drug properties could
109   support this conclusion when the drug is:
110
111         •   Metabolized and/or excreted by the liver to a small extent (< 30 percent of the absorbed
112             drug in humans) and minimal drug concentration changes do not lead to clinically
113             significant differences in safety or effectiveness.
114
115         •   Excreted entirely via the renal route with no involvement of the liver.
116
117         •   Intended only for single administration.
118
119         •   Locally acting with limited or no systemic exposure.
120
121         •   Gaseous or volatile, and the drug and its active metabolites are primarily eliminated via
122             the lungs.
123
124             C.     Timing of the Hepatic Impairment Study
125
126   The effect of HI should be assessed early in drug development to adequately guide trial
127   eligibility and identify a recommended dosage for patients with HI.
128
129   In the case of orphan drugs, where the population with the disease is fewer than 200,000,
130   sponsors should reach out to the appropriate FDA review division early during drug development
131   to discuss the timing and characterization of the pharmacokinetics of a drug in subjects with HI.
132
133   IV.       STUDY DESIGN CONSIDERATIONS
134
135   The primary purpose of the dedicated HI study is to characterize the impact of HI on the
136   pharmacokinetics of the drug and determine whether the extent of the changes in the
137   pharmacokinetics, and where appropriate the pharmacodynamics, of the drug warrants different
138   conditions of use in patients with HI (e.g., avoiding use, modified recommended dosage,
139   increased monitoring for adverse reactions).
140
141   This section describes the considerations for the design of a dedicated HI study.
142
143             A.     Assessment of Hepatic Function
144
145   FDA recommends using the Child-Pugh (CP) classification to categorize the degree of HI in
146   subjects with cirrhosis due to chronic liver disease. In subjects with cancer, sponsors can use the
147   National Cancer Institute (NCI) Organ Dysfunction Working Group criteria (NCI Criteria) to
148   categorize the degree of HI, except for subjects with hepatobiliary cancers. For subjects with
149   hepatobiliary cancers in which cirrhosis may be the key risk factor, FDA recommends using the
                                                        4
                                       Contains Nonbinding Recommendations

150   CP classification unless known safety considerations preclude use of the drug in subjects with
151   advanced cirrhosis. FDA does not recommend the use of the NCI Criteria to assess hepatic
152   function for non-cancer patients.
153
154   Other approaches may be considered to identify patients with HI for a given indication (See
155   IV.A. 3. Other Assessments of Hepatic Function). FDA recommends that sponsors consult with
156   the appropriate FDA review division if they plan to use a hepatic classification system other than
157   CP in subjects without cancer or the NCI Criteria in subjects with cancer (except hepatobiliary
158   cancers).

160           1.       Child-Pugh Classification
161
162   The CP 10 classification system, originally developed for patients with cirrhosis undergoing
163   portocaval shunt surgery, is based on points contributed by five components, as shown in Table
164   1. HI is classified as mild, moderate, or severe, which corresponds to CP scores of 5-6 (CP group
165   A), 7-9 (CP group B), and 10-15 (CP group C), respectively. Despite some limitations (e.g.,
166   subjective assessment of ascites), the CP classification continues to be a useful marker of liver
167   function and is generally accepted to predict changes in pharmacokinetics for the purposes of
168   drug development.

198           3.       Other Assessments of Hepatic Function
199
200   Other scoring systems using laboratory tests to assess the severity of liver disease exist, e.g., the
201   Model of End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score and the Albumin-Bilirubin (ALBI) score.
202   However, these scoring systems have not been sufficiently characterized with respect to their
203   ability to predict changes in pharmacokinetics to support their use in lieu of the CP score. When
204   using the CP classification, MELD scores can be useful for further stratification of disease
205   severity within CP groups B and C. Generally, when alternative scores are used, the CP
206   classification should also be assessed, and results should be reported for all classification
207   methods. FDA encourages assessment of other relevant markers to explore correlation with
208   pharmacokinetic change.
209
210   Exogenous marker or probe drug tests may be used as exploratory tools for assessing hepatic
211   drug clearance and possibly support an alternative dosage in patients with HI (compared to those
212   with normal hepatic function) in conjunction with use of appropriate hepatic classification.

216           1.       Study Subjects
217
218   The sponsor should conduct a study in subjects with mild, moderate, and severe HI, as well as a
219   control group without HI. In certain cases, a study design that only investigates the effect of

220   severe HI could be a reasonable approach to begin characterization of the effect of HI on the
221   pharmacokinetics of a drug. In that case, if a significant effect is observed in subjects with severe
222   HI, FDA recommends that the other HI subpopulations be studied. The sponsor should collect
223   and report the etiology/etiologies of the underlying liver disease or other hepatic conditions that
224   contributed to cirrhosis.
225
226   Laboratory parameters and clinical status should be stable prior to study enrollment. Sometimes,
227   the repeat laboratory tests or ascites and encephalopathy scores can change substantially (e.g., >
228   30 percent change) prior to drug administration. In this case, laboratory test and ascites or
229   encephalopathy assessment should be repeated (e.g., after one week) to allow the subject to be
230   properly classified. If the severity of HI for a subject changed, the subject should be included in
231   the severity group identified closest to pharmacokinetic sampling.
232
233   Sponsors should also consider that different etiologies of underlying liver diseases could affect a
234   drug’s pharmacokinetics or pharmacodynamics differently (e.g., cholestatic liver diseases may
235   have a different impact on pharmacokinetics and/or pharmacodynamics compared to
236   hepatocellular diseases that may be related to biliary excretion for drugs that undergo
237   enterohepatic circulation).
238
239   For drugs under development for a liver disease, it could be important to enroll subjects with
240   cirrhosis due to the target liver disease. Additionally, some drugs intended for the treatment of
241   cancer should, due to safety considerations, only be administered to subjects with cancer who
242   also have HI.
243
244   Study subjects should be excluded if they are experiencing acute liver disease (e.g., acute
245   alcohol-associated hepatitis, acute viral hepatitis) or acute-on-chronic liver disease.
246
247   To the extent possible, subjects with normal hepatic function (i.e., the control group) and
248   subjects with HI should have similar characteristics that are expected to affect the
249   pharmacokinetics of the drug (e.g., age, sex, race or ethnicity, weight, phenotypes for
250   polymorphic enzymes or transporters, if applicable). If subjects in the study use concomitant
251   drugs, a careful assessment of their influence on pharmacokinetics or pharmacodynamics of the
252   investigational drug should be made at the time of study planning.
253
254   For drugs whose pharmacokinetics is known to be affected by a polymorphic enzyme or
255   transporter (e.g., CYP2D6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19; OATP1B1, BCRP, OCT1), the sponsor should
256   determine the genotype of subjects during the screening phase.
257
258          2.      Sample Size
259
260   The number of study subjects enrolled in each group should be sufficient to ensure precise
261   estimation of the relevant pharmacokinetic parameters. Justification should be provided for the
262   selected sample size. For example, one approach could be to prospectively target a 95 percent
263   confidence interval within 60 to 140 percent of the point estimate of the geometric mean of the

267           3.       Drug Administration
268
269   A single-dose design is generally sufficient to describe the pharmacokinetics of the drug and its
270   active metabolites when both exhibit dose-proportional and time-independent pharmacokinetics
271   at anticipated therapeutic concentrations. A multiple-dose design, preferably at steady-state,
272   should be considered when the drug or an active metabolite demonstrates non-dose-proportional
273   or time-dependent pharmacokinetics.
274
275   Although the anticipated therapeutic dosage is generally recommended as the study dosage, a
276   lower dosage may be appropriate in subjects with HI when dose-limiting toxicity is anticipated at
277   higher drug concentrations. The rationale for the dosage selection should be included in the study
278   protocol and study report.
279
280   If multiple routes of administration are proposed for a drug, the study should use the route of
281   administration that is expected to result in the highest systemic exposure.
282
283           4.       Sample Collection and Analysis
284
285   The concentrations of the parent drug and/or active metabolites should be evaluated in the
286   appropriate matrix.
287
288   The sampling frequency and duration should be adequate to determine the relevant
289   pharmacokinetic parameters of the parent drug and its active metabolites, in study subjects with
290   HI.
291
292   For drugs that are highly extracted by the liver (extraction ratio >0.7) and are extensively bound
293   to plasma proteins (fraction unbound <10 percent), the sponsor should determine the unbound
294   fraction at trough and maximum plasma concentrations, as well as other concentration time
295   points, when possible. The sponsor is encouraged to follow this recommendation for drugs with
296   low hepatic extraction (extraction ratio <0.3) and extensive plasma protein binding to evaluate
297   whether considerations based on total or unbound (free) drug concentrations are more
298   appropriate. A rationale for not measuring unbound drug concentrations should be included in
299   the study protocol and study report. Relevant pharmacokinetic parameters should be reported in
300   terms of both unbound and total concentrations of drug, where applicable, in the study report.
301
302   For drugs not extensively bound to plasma proteins, the sponsor should retain and archive a
303   sufficient quantity of the samples to allow for future drug binding studies to evaluate unexpected
304   observations.

309          C.      Population Pharmacokinetic Approach
310
311   If subjects with varying degrees of HI are adequately represented in clinical trials (see section
312   IV.A. Assessment of Hepatic Function), and there are sufficient drug concentration data to
313   describe the pharmacokinetics of the drug, popPK analyses of data from clinical trials could be
314   sufficient to characterize the impact of HI on drug exposure for the population represented in the
315   trials. If subjects with HI cannot be enrolled in sufficient numbers, a dedicated HI
316   pharmacokinetic study may be conducted to support appropriate dosage for the HI population not
317   represented in clinical trials.
318
319   To allow for the successful use of a popPK approach or exposure-response analysis, the clinical
320   trial design should retain some of the critical components of the dedicated HI studies described
321   in sections IV.A and B of this guidance. FDA recommends the following:
322
323      •   Including adequate numbers of subjects within each HI group
324
325      •   Determining hepatic function using the appropriate classification system for the target
326          population
327
328      •   Maintaining accurate records of dosing and sample collection times
329
330      •   Obtaining adequate numbers of samples per subject
331
332      •   Measuring unbound drug concentrations when appropriate
333
334      •   Measuring active metabolite levels, where applicable, in addition to levels of the parent
335          drug
336
337      •   Using the same method to classify hepatic function when data are pooled across studies
338
339      •   Conducting exposure-response analyses, when available, accounting for HI as an
340          independent predictor of response
341
342   For additional information on the application of popPK approaches, refer to the guidance for
343   industry Population Pharmacokinetics (February 2022).

345          D.      Considerations for Pharmacodynamic Assessments
346
347   Whenever appropriate, pharmacodynamic assessments should be included in HI studies. Such
348   assessments are important in situations where HI could result in pharmacodynamic changes that
349   are independent of pharmacokinetic changes (e.g., oral anticoagulants). In such situations, the
350   pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic data could be critical to determine if a different dosage

                                                       9
                                   Contains Nonbinding Recommendations
                                           Draft — Not for Implementation
351   in patients with HI is needed. The selection of the pharmacodynamic endpoints should be
352   discussed with the relevant FDA review division.
353
354   Pharmacodynamic assessments can also be useful in studies designed to assess the impact of HI,
355   especially if concentration-response data are not available, or if there are concerns that HI could
356   alter the pharmacodynamic response. In situations where the relationship between
357   pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics is unaltered, the pharmacokinetic measurements
358   would be sufficient to characterize the impact of HI. However, when pharmacodynamics is
359   studied, the pharmacodynamic endpoint can be chosen in one of two ways:
360
361        •   The pharmacodynamic endpoint can be based on the pharmacological characteristics of
362            the drug and/or its active metabolite(s).
363
364        •   For drugs that are likely to affect integral components of hepatic function assessments,
365            for example serum bilirubin or INR, one or more of these biomarkers can be studied.

368   V.       DATA ANALYSIS
369
370            A.     Estimating Parameters and Conveying Results
371
372   Plasma concentration data (and urine concentration data, if collected) should be analyzed to
373   estimate measures or parameters describing the pharmacokinetics of the drug and/or its active
374   metabolites, for example:
375
376        •   Area under the plasma concentration-time curve (AUC)
377        •   Peak concentration (Cmax)
378        •   Apparent clearance (CL/F)
379        •   Renal and nonrenal clearance (CLR and CLNR)
380        •   Apparent volume of distribution (Vdz or Vdss)
381        •   Terminal half-life (t1/2)
382        •   Time to peak concentration (Tmax)
383        •   Fraction unbound (fu)
384
385   Pharmacokinetic parameters should be expressed in terms of unbound concentrations, where
386   assessed, and total concentrations. Non-compartmental and/or compartmental modeling
387   approaches to parameter estimates can be employed. The sponsor should provide the geometric
388   and arithmetic means and measures of variability for pharmacokinetic parameters, the point
389   estimate for the ratio of the means of the impaired and control groups for total and unbound
390   AUC and Cmax, and the associated 90 percent confidence interval.
391
392            B.     Relationship Between Measures of Hepatic Function and Pharmacokinetics
393

395   components of the relevant hepatic classification systems using linear and nonlinear models. A

398   parameters should be considered to help identify relevant predictors of changes in
399   pharmacokinetics and aid in refining dosage recommendations; however, some analyses may
400   rely on categorical variables (e.g., CP Scores). The sponsor should calculate, and report estimates
401   of the parameters of the chosen model as well as measures of their precision (e.g., standard errors
402   or confidence intervals).
403
404             C.     Development of Dosage Recommendations
405
406   Dosage recommendations in patients with HI should be based on the overall understanding of the
407   relationship between hepatic function, drug exposure, and the exposure-response relationships
408   for efficacy and safety. For drugs where small changes in exposure do not lead to clinically
409   significant differences in the safety and effectiveness of the drug, the recommended dosage of
410   the drug in patients with HI may be the same as in patients with normal hepatic function. When
411   there is a need for a different recommended dosage in patients with HI compared to those with
412   normal hepatic function, the dosage recommendations should be based on exposure-matching to
413   a reference group with an acceptable benefit-risk profile, often the normal hepatic function
414   group.
415
416   There are a number of approaches to dosage selection for patients with HI. For example,
417   pharmacokinetic simulations that project systemic exposures that fall within the 5th and 95th
418   percentiles of those achieved in the reference group can be used. Another approach is to establish
419   no-effect boundaries, which represent an interval within which a change in systemic exposure is
420   deemed not significant enough to warrant clinical action (see also section V.C.1 in the
421   International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) guidance for industry Drug Interaction Studies
422   (August 2024)).
