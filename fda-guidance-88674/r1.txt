15       I. INTRODUCTION
16
17   This draft guidance describes the Food and Drug Administration’s (FDA’s or Agency’s) current
18   thinking on recommended practices for drug manufacturers (firms) and their representatives to
19   follow if they choose to distribute to health care professionals 2 or health care entities 3 scientific
20   or medical journal articles that discuss new risk information for approved prescription drugs 4,5,6
21   marketed in the United States. The recommendations in this draft guidance are intended to
22   address issues specific to the distribution of new information about risks associated with a drug
23   that further characterizes risks identified in the approved labeling.
24
25   The recommendations in this draft guidance are intended to apply to distribution of new risk
26   information (as that term is further explained in section II of this document) for drugs intended
27   for human and animal use. Throughout this draft guidance, the Agency provides references to
28   regulations and guidances specific to drugs intended for human use. Unless otherwise indicated,
29   the Agency generally takes a similar approach when addressing the issues raised in this draft
30   guidance as they pertain to animal drugs.
31

33   responsibilities. Instead, guidances describe the Agency’s current thinking on a topic and should

38       II. BACKGROUND
39
40   In February 2014, FDA issued a draft guidance titled Distributing Scientific and Medical
41   Publications on Unapproved New Uses—Recommended Practices (Draft Revised Reprints
42   Guidance). 7 That draft guidance revised an earlier guidance from 2009 to clarify the Agency’s
43   position on manufacturer dissemination of scientific or medical publications that include
44   information on unapproved new uses of the manufacturer’s products. The Draft Revised Reprints
45   Guidance was issued to enable the public to provide comments. Stakeholders have raised
46   questions regarding the Agency’s position on manufacturer dissemination of new scientific or
47   medical information about safety information contained in the labeling for approved drugs.
48   Because this concerns dissemination of new risk information related to approved uses of a drug,
49   this issue is distinct from the dissemination of information on unapproved new uses of approved
50   drugs.
51
52   In response to those questions, the Agency is issuing this draft guidance to clarify and solicit
53   public comments on the Agency’s position on manufacturer dissemination of new risk
54   information regarding lawfully marketed drugs for approved uses to health care professionals or
55   health care entities. FDA recognizes that the safety profile of a drug evolves throughout its
56   lifecycle as the extent of exposure to the product increases and that it can be helpful for health
57   care practitioners to receive significant new risk information about an approved product in a
58   timely manner. FDA anticipates that the earliest distribution of new risk information will
59   generally involve distribution of recently published studies, as opposed to textbooks or clinical
60   practice guidelines. Accordingly, FDA is providing guidance for firms that choose to distribute
61   new risk information in the form of a reprint or digital copy of a published study.
62
63   FDA believes that recommendations specific to the distribution of new risk information about
64   approved prescription drugs and biological products are needed for two reasons. First, in general
65   there are differences in the purpose, nature, and reliability of the evidence used to determine the
66   effectiveness of a drug (e.g., to support a new intended use) and the evidence that is the basis for
67   the product’s risk assessment (see section III.A). Therefore, FDA believes guidance is needed to
68   address the spectrum of data sources that could be appropriate for distribution to provide new
69   risk information. Second, new risk information may contradict or otherwise deviate from the risk
70   information in the approved labeling, which may cause confusion or otherwise contribute to
71   patient harm. If the new information is unreliable or presented without the appropriate context, it
72   could influence prescribing decisions or patient monitoring in a way that could harm patients.
73   For example, postmarket data concerning an adverse reaction identified in the approved labeling
74   may suggest that the adverse reaction is less severe or occurs at lower frequency than indicated
75   in the approved labeling, or may call into question the basis for concluding that there is a causal
76   relationship between the reaction and the drug. That information could, for example, lead to use

 83   For purposes of this draft guidance, the term new risk information refers to information that
 84   becomes available after a drug is marketed that rebuts or mitigates information about a risk
 85   already identified in the approved labeling 8 or otherwise refines risk information in the approved
 86   labeling in a way that does not indicate greater seriousness of the risk. New risk information
 87   could, for example, include data indicating that the severity or rate of occurrence of an event is
 88   lower than described in the approved labeling or call into question a causal relationship between
 89   a drug and an event in the approved labeling. New risk information could also supplement risk
 90   information in approved labeling derived from a general population with information about risks
 91   in subpopulations of interest (e.g., data that show that the risk of an event in patients with hepatic
 92   disease is similar to the risk in a general population). 9
 93
 94   New risk information does not include, and this guidance is not intended to apply to, information
 95   about a newly identified risk (not previously included in the approved labeling) or new
 96   information that indicates that a risk already identified in approved labeling is more serious than
 97   is reflected in that labeling.
 98
 99   Nothing in this draft guidance is intended to change a firm’s existing obligations under the
100   Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act), the Public Health Service Act (PHS Act)
101   and implementing regulations to update its approved labeling, to accurately reflect what is
102   known about the safety profile of the drug, to ensure that the labeling is not false or misleading,
103   or for other reasons. 10

109   As reflected in the statutory new drug and new animal drug approval standards in sections 505
110   and 512 of the FD&C Act and the standard for licensure under section 351 of the PHS Act, the
111   evidence that is the basis for the risk assessment of a drug differs from the evidence that is the
112   basis for an effectiveness determination. The effectiveness determination is generally intended
113   to evaluate a drug’s positive effect based on adequate and well-controlled trials designed to
114   evaluate a specific efficacy variable (or a discrete number of efficacy variables) for the purpose
115   of distinguishing the effect of the drug from other influences. 11 In contrast, FDA’s premarket
116   risk assessment is intended to identify and characterize the nature, frequency, and severity of the
117   usually broad spectrum of adverse events and other risks associated with the use of a product
118   based on a range of data sources. For human drugs, the supporting data are generally not derived
119   from studies designed to test a specific safety hypothesis. The assessment is based largely on
120   observations of adverse events from studies intended to assess effectiveness and includes all
121   patients exposed to the investigational drug during its development. For animal drugs, there are
122   generally data from safety studies in the target animal, in addition to observations of adverse
123   events from studies intended to assess effectiveness. 12 For both human and animal drugs,
124   relevant data are weighted and integrated, based on the nature and reliability of the data sources,
125   into a coherent assessment of what adverse reactions are reasonably associated with the use of a
126   product and, to the extent possible, their frequency and severity. 13
127
128   Risk information is presented in various sections of the approved labeling. The ADVERSE
129   REACTIONS section is the repository for all adverse reactions associated with a drug.
130   Depending on the seriousness or other important clinical implications of an adverse reaction, it
131   may also be discussed in greater detail in other labeling sections, in particular the WARNINGS
132   AND PRECAUTIONS, CONTRAINDICATIONS, and BOXED WARNING sections. 14 For
133   example, the ADVERSE REACTIONS section of approved human drug labeling is explicitly
134   required to “describe the overall adverse reaction profile of the drug based on the entire safety
135   database.” 15 The threshold for inclusion of an adverse event 16 in approved human drug labeling
136   is a determination that “there is some basis to believe there is a causal relationship between the
137   drug and the occurrence of the adverse event.” 17 Whether there is some basis to believe there is
138   a causal relationship is a matter of judgment based on factors such as: (1) the frequency of
139   reporting of the event, (2) whether the adverse event rate for a drug exceeds the placebo rate, (3)
140   the extent of dose-response, (4) the extent to which the adverse event is consistent with the
141   pharmacology of the drug, (5) the timing of the event relative to the time of drug exposure, (6)

145   As these factors suggest, the strength of the evidence of a causal relationship between a drug and
146   an adverse event (and, where applicable, the assessment of rate of occurrence) may vary across
147   different drugs and diseases, and across different adverse reactions for the same drug, based on
148   the types of data sources available in the premarket safety database, the size of the premarket
149   population exposed to the drug, the pharmacology of the drug, and the nature of the event.
150   Placebo-controlled and dose-response studies, if available, will generally be more informative
151   than active-control and single-arm studies, and a larger premarket population will generally be
152   more informative than a smaller one. Also, certain types of events are more strongly suggestive
153   of a causal relationship than others. For example, a single occurrence, or a small number of
154   occurrences, of a hypersensitivity reaction to a drug in which the patient was rechallenged with
155   the drug and reacted, or an event that very rarely occurs spontaneously in a population not
156   exposed to a drug (e.g., agranulocytosis, Stevens-Johnson Syndrome), can provide strong
157   evidence of a causal relationship. However, a limited number of occurrences of a serious event
158   that would be expected to occur in the study population independent of exposure to a drug (e.g.,
159   cardiovascular events in an elderly study population) provide more ambiguous evidence of a
160   causal relationship. Therefore, a premarket risk assessment, although a thorough and rigorous
161   assessment of the available safety data from the entire population exposed to a drug prior to
162   marketing, may be limited in its ability to fully characterize a drug’s safety profile by the nature
163   of the supporting data and the size of the population exposed.

165           B. Postmarket Risk Assessment
166
167   Because of the inherent limitations of the premarket risk assessment, postmarketing
168   pharmacovigilance activities—both active (new controlled and epidemiologic studies) and
169   passive (spontaneous reports)—may be critically important to developing and refining the safety
170   profile of a drug. For example, new adverse reactions may be identified through postmarket
171   reports of adverse events, particularly rare serious events that occur at a frequency too low to
172   have been identified and characterized premarketing. Also, new data and information may be
173   developed to further characterize previously identified adverse events. The types of data that can
174   contribute to further developing the safety profile of a drug after marketing include data from
175   controlled trials intended to evaluate a specific safety endpoint, controlled and uncontrolled trials
176   evaluating efficacy (e.g., new uses), pooled analyses of new and existing risk information from
177   controlled trials, epidemiologic studies evaluating a particular safety endpoint or safety
178   generally, registries, and analyses of postmarketing reports of adverse events obtained through
179   active (e.g., Sentinel) or passive surveillance processes. In some cases, additional new studies—
180   controlled trials or epidemiologic studies—may be postmarketing requirements or commitments
181   made at the time of approval or thereafter (e.g., a cardiovascular outcome study is often required
182   for a new drug to treat type 2 diabetes). 19

184          IV. CRITERIA FOR DETERMINING WHETHER NEW RISK INFORMATION IS
185              APPROPRIATE FOR DISTRIBUTION TO HEALTH CARE PROFESSIONALS
186              AND HEALTH CARE ENTITIES
187
188   FDA recognizes that the safety profile of a drug evolves throughout its lifecycle as the extent of
189   exposure to the product increases and that it can be helpful for health care practitioners to receive
190   significant new risk information about an approved product in a timely manner. Firms may
191   distribute to healthcare entities and healthcare professionals new risk information under
192   appropriate circumstances, even if such data are not consistent with the risk information
193   currently in approved labeling. Before distributing new risk information that suggests that an
194   adverse reaction or other risk currently in approved labeling is not causally related to a drug or is
195   less consequential than is reflected in the labeling, or that otherwise refines the characterization
196   of an adverse reaction identified in approved labeling, firms should carefully consider the
197   reliability and persuasiveness of the data. As discussed in section III.A, there are cases in which
198   even one occurrence of an adverse event can provide strong evidence that the drug caused the
199   event. In contrast, where existing evidence provides some basis to believe that there is a causal
200   relationship between an event and a drug, 20 the evidence offered to overcome that prior showing
201   should generally be from a study or analysis in a population large enough to detect a meaningful
202   difference in the rate of occurrence of an event in patients who are exposed to the drug versus
203   those who are not.
204
205   FDA does not intend to object to the distribution of new risk information that rebuts, mitigates,
206   or refines risk information in the approved labeling, and is distributed by a firm in the form of a
207   reprint or digital copy of a published study, if the study or analysis and the manner of distribution
208   meet the principles set out below. Distribution of information that is not consistent with this
209   guidance may render the labeling of a drug false or misleading under section 502(a) of the
210   FD&C Act.
211
212   DATA SOURCE
213
214          •   The study or analysis should meet accepted design and other methodologic standards for
215              the type of study or analysis (e.g., provides a clear description of the hypothesis tested,
216              acknowledges and accounts for potential bias and multiplicity) and should be sufficiently
217              well-designed and informative to merit consideration in assessing the implications of a
218              risk.
219
220          •   To rebut a prior determination (reflected in the approved labeling) that there is some basis
221              to believe there is causal relationship between the drug and the occurrence of an adverse
222              event, or to otherwise mitigate a described risk, the study or analysis should also be at
223              least as persuasive as the data sources that underlie the existing risk assessment of
224              causality, severity, and/or incidence of the adverse reaction as reflected in approved
225              labeling (e.g., data from a new controlled trial designed to estimate the relative risk of the
226              event, a pharmacoepidemiologic study that is capable of reliably estimating the relative
