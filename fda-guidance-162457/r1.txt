I.      INTRODUCTION

This guidance is intended to assist applicants that submit abbreviated new drug applications
(ANDAs) for topical dermatologic corticosteroid products of all potencies, 2 hereinafter referred
to as topical corticosteroids. This guidance describes recommendations for in vivo studies to
demonstrate the bioequivalence (BE) of topical corticosteroids.

This guidance replaces the guidance for industry Topical Dermatologic Corticosteroids: In Vivo
Bioequivalence that was issued in June 1995. This guidance is being issued to provide clarity for
potential ANDA applicants on the appropriate pilot and pivotal studies and other
recommendations for pharmacodynamic approaches to assess the BE of topical corticosteroids.

This guidance provides recommendations for the study design, method qualification, data
analysis, and data reporting for the pilot dose duration vasoconstrictor response study
(hereinafter also referred to as pilot vasoconstrictor study) and pivotal vasoconstrictor BE study
(hereinafter also referred to as pivotal BE study) used to demonstrate BE of topical
corticosteroids. This guidance also discusses considerations and approaches for estimating key
study parameters (e.g., dose duration corresponding to half the maximal vasoconstrictor response
(ED50) and sample size for the pivotal vasoconstrictor BE study).

In general, FDA’s guidance documents do not establish legally enforceable responsibilities.
Instead, guidances describe the Agency’s current thinking on a topic and should be viewed only
as recommendations, unless specific regulatory or statutory requirements are cited. The use of
the word should in Agency guidances means that something is suggested or recommended,
but not required.

II.      BACKGROUND

The Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act) generally requires an ANDA to contain,
among other things, information to show that the proposed generic drug product (test product) is
bioequivalent to its reference listed drug (RLD). 3 BE “is the absence of a significant difference
in the rate and extent to which the active ingredient or active moiety in pharmaceutical
equivalents or pharmaceutical alternatives becomes available at the site of drug action when
administered at the same molar dose under similar conditions in an appropriately designed
study.” 4

This guidance describes an in vivo pharmacodynamic approach to demonstrate the BE of topical
corticosteroids. Topical corticosteroids are known to cause vasoconstriction of the dermal
vasculature that produces the pharmacodynamic effect of skin blanching. The magnitude of
blanching (change in skin color) depends upon the potency of the topical corticosteroid, and it
increases relative to the amount of the topical corticosteroid permeating into the skin, when study
parameters are suitably controlled. Thus, the pharmacodynamic vasoconstrictor response can be
a surrogate measure of the rate and extent to which a topical corticosteroid becomes available at
the site of action in the skin.

A pilot vasoconstrictor study is routinely performed to define appropriate parameters for a
pivotal vasoconstrictor BE study used to support a demonstration of BE between a test topical
corticosteroid and its reference standard (RS), which ordinarily is the RLD. 5 Therefore, this
guidance recommends that ANDA applicants that propose to use an in vivo pharmacodynamic
approach to demonstrate BE between a test topical corticosteroid and its RS conduct two in vivo
vasoconstrictor studies: (1) a pilot dose duration vasoconstrictor response study, using the RS;
and (2) a pivotal vasoconstrictor BE study, comparing the test topical corticosteroid and RS. The
proposed methodology, including the study design, model selection, and model optimization for
the pilot dose duration vasoconstrictor response study, and the statistical method for the pivotal
vasoconstrictor BE study are discussed in more detail in subsequent sections of this guidance.

The purpose of the pilot dose duration vasoconstrictor response study is to determine the dose
duration-response relationship of the topical corticosteroid to be studied in the pivotal

III.     PHARMACODYNAMIC VASOCONSTRICTOR STUDIES

         A.       Vasoconstrictor Method Qualification

The chromameter is the apparatus most commonly used to measure the pharmacodynamic skin
blanching (small vessel vasoconstriction) response induced following the application of topical
corticosteroids. Prior to collecting data for vasoconstrictor studies, the chromameter should be
calibrated and qualified for its intended use. In addition, the repeatability and ruggedness 10 of
chromameter measurements by different operators should be qualified. These qualifications
should be completed before the start of a study. If studies have multiple groups, method
qualifications should be performed, at a minimum, before the start date of the first group.

         1.       Chromameter Qualification

Chromameter qualification is conducted with calibrated chromameters to support a
demonstration of the ruggedness of the chromameter measurements across multiple chromameter
units. Multiple chromameter units can be set up to measure the vasoconstrictor response in both
the pilot dose duration vasoconstrictor response study and the pivotal vasoconstrictor BE study.
All chromameters used in these pilot and pivotal vasoconstrictor studies should be reported with
their specific identification numbers and qualified to ensure consistent performance in study data
collection. Chromameter qualification should be performed on all chromameters planned to be
used in pilot and pivotal vasoconstrictor studies using one operator and one subject and with at
least four readings each at one designated skin site. Intrachromameter variability is calculated as
the variability within multiple readings at one skin site by one operator using one chromameter.
Interchromameter variability is calculated as the variability in readings between different
chromameters, with the mean value of multiple readings from each chromameter at one skin site
by one operator. The chromameter qualification should be repeated with at least four study
subjects, using at least four skin sites in each study subject to demonstrate the reproducibility of
the chromameter measurements. The study subjects could differ from those enrolled in the pilot
and pivotal vasoconstrictor studies. To determine procedure consistency between and within
chromameters, the variability (percent coefficient of variation (CV)) for the intrachromameter
and the interchromameter measurements should be not more than 15 percent in each and every
subject.

         2.       Operator Qualification

Operator qualification is conducted to support a demonstration of the ruggedness of the
chromameter measurements across multiple operators. The operators who conduct pilot and
pivotal vasoconstrictor studies should be reported with their specific identification numbers or
names and qualified to ensure that each one is operating the chromameters and measuring the
skin response consistently. Operator qualification should be performed by multiple operators
using one chromameter on one subject with at least four readings each at one designated skin

       B.      Dose Duration-Response Model

The conditions under which the pivotal vasoconstrictor BE study is performed should be
optimized to assure that the test topical corticosteroid and RS are compared in the sensitive
(steep) portion of the response curve, where the vasoconstrictor response would be sensitive and
discriminating to differences in the bioavailability of the corticosteroid between the test topical
corticosteroid and RS. Development of a dose duration-response relationship for a topical
corticosteroid relies on consistent administration of a predetermined dose of the drug product to
the skin. Development of a dose duration-response relationship for a topical corticosteroid will
identify the sensitive dose duration-response region to support pivotal vasoconstrictor BE study
design. In principle, the time course of the response should be measured until it trends toward
baseline to ensure that at each dose duration, the maximal pharmacodynamic response is
observed.

To identify the sensitive and discriminating region of the dose duration-response curve for the
pharmacodynamic skin blanching effect, it is useful to (1) produce conditions that are expected
to deliver increasing amounts of a corticosteroid drug into the skin (a practical way to modulate
the amount of drug (corticosteroid) delivered into the skin is to dose the fixed amount of topical
corticosteroid on the skin for progressively increasing dose durations) and (2) measure the
resulting skin blanching effect caused by dermal vasoconstriction.

response of topical corticosteroids, which describes the measure of effect (E) in terms of a

The in vivo vasoconstrictor response (detected as skin blanching) generally approaches a
maximum when the dermal vasculature is not able to vasoconstrict further. At relatively high
strengths for highly potent topical corticosteroids, there may be a diminishing change in the
vasoconstrictor response to increases in dose duration (flattening the response curve at the upper
end). Conversely, at relatively low strengths for low potency topical corticosteroids, it may be
challenging to elicit a vasoconstrictor response despite increases in dose duration (flattening the
response curve at the lower end). Therefore, a prospective applicant should design the pilot
vasoconstrictor study to cover a full dose duration-response curve appropriately according to the
potency of topical corticosteroids, and, hence, improve the dose duration-response model.

        C.       Study Design

        1.       Pilot Dose Duration Vasoconstrictor Response Study

This pilot dose duration vasoconstrictor response study should be based on the RS only, with
randomization of dose duration skin sites. Refer to Appendix II for a detailed example.

Untreated control sites on each arm should be used to enable correction of active drug skin sites
for color changes during the study unrelated to drug exposure. Because the vehicle
corresponding to the RS is not generally available, untreated control sites refer to untreated areas
of skin, not to areas of skin to which vehicle has been applied.

Dose durations (e.g., from 0.25 to 6.0 hours) should be designed properly to explore the dose
duration-response relationship and to determine the appropriate dose duration for the pivotal

vasoconstrictor BE study. Caution is recommended with respect to determining appropriate dose
duration, as observations from pilot studies data suggest that the ED50 (the dose duration to be
used in the pivotal vasoconstrictor BE study) decreases with increasing topical corticosteroid
potency. Evaluation of dose duration response requires dose duration data at some time (i.e., D1)

terms of area under the effect curve (AUEC) by readings of a chromameter at the end of each
dose duration after the removal of residual topical corticosteroid. A minimum of 12 subjects is
recommended.

           2.       Pivotal Vasoconstrictor BE Study

This pharmacodynamic BE study uses replicates of single-dose duration of test topical
corticosteroid and RS based on the population ED50 identified in the pilot vasoconstrictor study.
Also, the replicates of each of the dose durations (D1 and D2) of the RS should be included in the
pivotal vasoconstrictor BE study.

For a BE analysis, selection of an individual subject is based upon an acceptable ratio of mean
reference AUEC at D2 over mean reference AUEC at D1 for each subject. The minimum value
of the ratio should be 1.25 and both mean AUEC values at D1 and D2 should be negative, 13 if a
simple Emax model is proposed. However, other values for the ratio can be used with justification
depending on the selected dose duration-response model. Each individual subject who meets this
dose duration-response criterion (under conditions when both mean AUEC at D1 and D2 values
are negative) is defined as a detector (i.e., evaluable subject).

It is the applicant’s responsibility to design an adequately powered pivotal vasoconstrictor BE
study. FDA recommends that applicants enroll a sufficient number of subjects to yield a number
of detectors sufficient to power the study. When determining the sample size of enrolled
subjects, dropouts and estimated required number of detectors should be taken into
consideration. Based on observations from studies submitted in ANDAs, 40 or more detectors
are generally used for the pivotal vasoconstrictor BE study. The sample size determination for
the pivotal vasoconstrictor BE study should be prespecified in the protocol and justified.
Sufficient subjects should be recruited, randomized with respect to dose duration skin site, and
dosed at the beginning of the study to ensure that the desired number of detectors will be
available for analysis. All detectors should be included in the analysis.

       E.     Subject Exclusion Criteria

Applicants should consider the following for subject exclusion:

       Clinically significant hypertension or circulatory disease.

       Smoking within 1 week of study.

       Caffeine intake greater than 500 milligrams per day prior to or during the study. Coffee,
       tea, and energy drinks should all be considered as important caffeine sources.

       Clinically significant history of alcoholism or substance abuse disorder.

       Use of topical dermatologic drug therapy (either as therapy or participation in the clinical
       study) on ventral forearms within 1 month prior to the study.

       Adverse reactions to topical or systemic corticosteroids.

       Any current or past medical condition, including active dermatitis or any other
       dermatologic condition, which might significantly affect the pharmacodynamic response
       to the administered drug.

       Shaving needed on ventral forearms to ensure consistent dosing on the skin surface.

       Use of any vasoactive (constrictor or dilator) medication (prescription or nonprescription)
       that could modulate blood flow. Examples of such drugs include nitroglycerin,
       antihypertensives, antihistamines, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, aspirin, and
       nonprescription cough/cold drug products containing antihistamines and/or either
       phenylpropanolamine or phentolamine.

       Any obvious difference in skin color between arms.

       A history of hypopigmentation as a side effect of topical application of potent
       corticosteroids, particularly in subjects with skin of color.

       F.      Subject Screening for Response

In this guidance, a responder is defined as a subject who during screening shows a skin
blanching vasoconstriction response to a single-dose duration of the corresponding RS under the
same occlusive or nonocclusive conditions used in the pilot and pivotal vasoconstrictor studies.
Quantification of skin blanching in the pilot and pivotal vasoconstrictor studies by a
chromameter is considered to be the most satisfactory response measurement. However,
responder status may be based on visual readings with the discrete multiple-unit scale (0 to 3 or 0
to 4). A dose duration of 4 hours or 6 hours is suggested, with skin blanching assessment
following drug product removal (e.g., 2 hours after drug product removal), but dose duration
could vary based on potency of the drug product. A responder shows a visual reading of at least
one unit.

Inclusion of nonresponders reduces the ability of a study to detect true differences between the
test topical corticosteroid and RS, should they exist. Therefore, for both the pilot vasoconstrictor
study and the pivotal vasoconstrictor BE study, only responders should be included for the
enrollment.

To conserve skin sites on the ventral forearm for use in the dose duration-response study or BE
study, responder status may be based on studies conducted at sites other than the forearm (e.g.,
upper arm).

Criteria for identification of responders, including dose duration, magnitude of response, and
skin site tested, should be pre-specified in the study protocol and documented in the study report.

       G.      Occlusion Versus Nonocclusion

When use of occlusion is allowed in the label of the specific RS, the pilot vasoconstrictor study
and pivotal vasoconstrictor BE study may be conducted using a nonabsorbent occlusive film.
Occlusion may be appropriate for lower-potency drug products in the vasoconstrictor study. If
occlusion is used for the pilot vasoconstrictor study, it should also be used for the pivotal
vasoconstrictor BE study.

       H.      Methods of Application and Removal

Staggered application with synchronized removal (i.e., the topical corticosteroid is applied to
skin sites at different times and removed at the same time) could be utilized in the pilot and
pivotal vasoconstrictor studies (see Appendix I).

Applicants should consider the following for pharmacodynamic modeling for the pilot dose
