38   II.      BACKGROUND
39
40   Section 351(k) of the PHS Act provides an abbreviated licensure pathway for biological products
41   shown to be biosimilar to, or interchangeable with, an FDA-licensed reference product. 6 Section
42   351(k) of the PHS Act, as amended by the Biologics Price Competition and Innovation Act of
43   2009, sets forth the requirements for an application for a proposed biosimilar product and an
44   application or a supplement for a proposed interchangeable product. 7
45
46   Section 351(i)(2) of the PHS Act defines biosimilarity to mean “that the biological product is
47   highly similar to the reference product notwithstanding minor differences in clinically inactive
48   components” and that “there are no clinically meaningful differences between the biological
49   product and the reference product in terms of the safety, purity, and potency of the product.”
50
51   To meet the standard for interchangeability, an applicant must provide sufficient information to
52   demonstrate biosimilarity and also to demonstrate that the biological product can be expected to
53   produce the same clinical result as the reference product in any given patient and, if the
54   biological product is administered more than once to an individual, the risk in terms of safety or
55   diminished efficacy of alternating or switching between the use of the biological product and the
56   reference product is not greater than the risk of using the reference product without such

62
63   This guidance provides an overview of scientific considerations for developing presentations for
64   proposed biosimilar and interchangeable biosimilar products. This guidance focuses on
65   therapeutic protein biological products and provides an overview of important scientific
66   considerations in demonstrating biosimilarity and interchangeability of a proposed therapeutic
67   protein biological product in a particular presentation with a reference product in a particular
68   presentation.

71   IV.        GENERAL CONSIDERATIONS FOR EVALUATION OF PRESENTATIONS
72              FOR PROPOSED BIOSIMILAR AND INTERCHANGEABLE PRODUCTS
73
74   When developing a product for licensure under section 351(k) of the PHS Act, it is important
75   that sponsors carefully consider the presentation of the proposed product relative to the
76   presentation of the reference product. The proposed presentation cannot result in a condition of
77   use that has not been previously approved for the reference product or a dosage form, strength, or
78   route of administration that is not the same as the reference product. 10
79
80   The data and information needed to support licensure of a biosimilar or interchangeable
81   biosimilar product may be influenced by the proposed product’s presentation. This guidance
82   provides a framework for sponsors to determine the types of data and information related to a
83   proposed presentation that might be necessary to support licensure as a biosimilar or
84   interchangeable product. The considerations described in this guidance are intended to provide
85   clarity and support flexibility where appropriate and adequately justified.

133            A.       General Framework for Recommended Analyses
134
135   This guidance recommends a stepwise, systematic approach for sponsors to use in identifying
136   and analyzing differences in the user interface between a proposed biosimilar or interchangeable
137   biosimilar combination product and the user interface of its reference product:
138
139        (1) First, sponsors should use the comparative analyses to identify all differences between
140            the user interface of the proposed biosimilar or interchangeable biosimilar combination
141            product and the user interface of the reference product.
142
143        (2) If the sponsor identifies any differences, the sponsor should next carefully consider
144            whether any difference may impact a design feature that a user would rely on to perform
145            a combination product critical task. Combination product critical tasks are those user
146            tasks that if performed incorrectly, or not performed at all, would or could cause harm to
147            the patient or user, where harm is defined to include compromised medical care. 16 For
148            each task that may be impacted by a difference in the user interface, the applicant should
149            evaluate the risk associated with use errors that may occur for those tasks. A sponsor
150            may, for example, find a use-related risk analysis 17 to be a helpful tool for identifying
151            combination product critical tasks that may be impacted by design differences and the use
152            errors that may occur.
153
154        (3) As a final step, sponsors should classify each identified difference as a minor design
155            difference if the difference does not impact a design feature that a user would rely on to
156            perform a combination product critical task or as an other design difference if the
157            difference may impact a design feature that a user would rely on to perform a critical task
158            (see section B.3 Classify Identified Differences, below).
159
160   The discussion below further describes these three steps and considerations on when other design
161   differences are identified.

163            B.       Recommended Comparative Analyses To Identify Design Differences and
164                     Determine Whether They May Impact a Combination Product Critical Task
165
166   FDA recommends that sponsors use three types of comparative analyses to determine whether
167   there are differences between the user interface design of the proposed biosimilar or
168   interchangeable biosimilar combination product and its reference product. As noted above, to
169   ensure differences between the user interfaces are adequately assessed, FDA recommends that
170   sponsors conduct these analyses throughout the development process of the user interface of the
171   final finished combination product. The comparative analyses that are submitted to FDA should
172   reflect the proposed final finished combination product user interface. 18
173
174            1.       Conduct Comparative Analyses To Identify Design Differences
175
176   The following three types of analyses are recommended to compare the user interface of the
177   proposed biosimilar or interchangeable biosimilar combination product with the user interface of
178   its reference product:
179
180        (1) Physical comparison of the device constituent part: FDA recommends that the sponsor of
181            the proposed biosimilar or interchangeable biosimilar combination product acquire the
182            reference product and examine the physical features of the device constituent part for the
183            proposed combination product and compare them with the reference product (e.g., visual,
184            auditory, and tactile examination). 19 Sponsors should evaluate differences in the internal
185            mechanics between a proposed biosimilar or interchangeable biosimilar combination
186            product and its reference product to the extent they may impact a critical task associated
187            with the use of the product (e.g., differences in force necessary to push a button to inject a
188            product).
189
190        (2) Comparative task analysis: FDA recommends that sponsors conduct a comparative task
191            analysis between the proposed biosimilar or interchangeable biosimilar combination
192            product and its reference product. To conduct a comparative task analysis, sponsors
193            should systematically dissect the use process for both the proposed combination product
194            and the reference product and analyze and compare the sequential and simultaneous
195            physical and cognitive activities for end users interacting with both products.
196
197        (3) Labeling comparison: FDA recommends a side-by-side, line-by-line comparison of the
198            proposed labeling, including on-device labeling, carton labeling, information in the full
199            prescribing information related to use of the device, the instructions for use, and

203            2.       Determine Whether Any Identified Difference May Impact a Design Feature That

205
206   For each of the three types of comparative analyses, the following outcomes are possible:
207
208        (1) No design differences: When no differences are identified between the user interface of
209            the proposed biosimilar or interchangeable biosimilar combination product and the user
210            interface of its reference product, it is likely that additional information and/or data about
211            the user interface will not be necessary to support approval of the proposed combination
212            product.
213
214        (2) Differences in design: If differences are identified between the design of the user
215            interface of a proposed biosimilar or interchangeable biosimilar combination product and
216            the design of the user interface of its reference product, the sponsor should evaluate
217            whether those design differences may impact a design feature on which a user would rely
218            to perform a critical task. As part of this evaluation, the sponsor should carefully
219            evaluate the risk for use-related errors associated with each difference identified in the
220            design of the user interface between the proposed biosimilar or interchangeable
221            biosimilar combination product and its reference product and consider whether the
222            proposed labeling mitigates any potential risk. The sponsor should consider the
223            following 20:
224
225            •    The errors that users might commit or the tasks they might fail to perform
226
227            •    The potential clinical consequences of use errors, considering the impact of the
228                 identified difference(s) in the context of the overall risk profile for the product

253          C.      Considerations When Other Design Differences Are Identified
254
255   Differences between the user interface of the proposed biosimilar or interchangeable biosimilar
256   combination product and the user interface of its reference product may be acceptable provided
257   that the design, labeling, and task differences are analyzed appropriately and that, when
258   warranted, additional data and information beyond the comparative analyses are provided to
259   demonstrate that the user interface differences do not preclude a showing that the proposed
260   combination product meets the standards for biosimilarity or interchangeability, as applicable.
261
262   Early communication with FDA through the IND may be particularly helpful if the sponsor
263   identifies a difference between the design of the user interface of the proposed biosimilar or
264   interchangeable biosimilar combination product as compared with its reference product that may
265   impact a design feature on which a user would rely to perform a critical task (i.e., an other design
266   difference) and the sponsor would like FDA’s feedback on whether additional information and/or
267   data are warranted. For example, the sponsor should contact FDA if it determines that a critical
268   task was modified, removed from, or added to the use process for the proposed biosimilar or
269   interchangeable biosimilar combination product as compared with its reference product.

343   VII.    CONSIDERATIONS FOR APPLICANTS PROPOSING A PRESENTATION FOR
344           WHICH ITS REFERENCE PRODUCT IS NOT MARKETED
345
346   If an applicant intends to develop and seek licensure of a proposed combination product in a
347   different presentation than its reference product, it is likely that there will be differences between
348   the user interface of the applicant’s proposed combination product and its reference product.
349
350           A.       Biosimilar Products
351
352   Some design differences in the presentation of the proposed biosimilar combination product
353   compared with its reference product may be acceptable, as long as the proposed combination
354   product meets the standards for biosimilarity, including that such differences would not result in
355   a condition of use that has not been previously approved for its reference product or a dosage
356   form, strength, or route of administration that is not the same as that of its reference product. It
357   may be possible, for example, for an applicant to obtain licensure of a proposed biosimilar
358   combination product with a prefilled syringe or an auto-injector presentation, even if the
359   presentation of its reference product is a vial, provided that the proposed biosimilar combination

         For example, a sponsor may develop a proposed combination product’s pen injector with an ergonomic design
      feature that orients the hand such that the resulting angle of injection is 90°, which differs from the 45° angle of
      injection for its reference product. Based on the comparative analyses, a sponsor should conclude that there is a
      difference that may impact a design feature on which a user would rely to perform a critical task and should
      characterize it as an other design difference. However, the sponsor’s existing in vitro testing, when compared with
