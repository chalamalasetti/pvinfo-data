14   I.         INTRODUCTION
15
16   The purposes of eligibility criteria for cancer clinical trials are to select the intended patient
17   population and reduce potential risks to trial subjects. However, eligibility criteria are sometimes
18   more restrictive than necessary, and expanding eligibility criteria to be more representative is one
19   trial design consideration that may improve the applicability of clinical trial data to U.S.
20   populations. This guidance is one in a series of guidances that provide recommendations
21   regarding eligibility criteria for clinical trials of investigational drugs 2 regulated by CDER and
22   CBER for the treatment of cancer, although many of the concepts may apply more broadly to
23   other clinical areas. 3 Specifically, this guidance includes recommendations for selecting
24   appropriate laboratory values as trial eligibility criteria to avoid unjustified exclusions of trial
25   subjects. This guidance intends to assist interested parties, including sponsors, clinical
26   investigators, and institutional review boards (IRBs), who are responsible for the development
27   and/or oversight of clinical trials.
28
29   A clinical trial’s eligibility criteria (inclusion and exclusion) are essential components of the trial,
30   defining the characteristics of the study population. 4 Because there is variability in
31   investigational drugs and trial objectives, eligibility criteria should be developed taking into
32   consideration the mechanism of action of the drug, the targeted disease or patient population, the

57   II.     BACKGROUND
58
59   Laboratory value-based eligibility criteria are one of the most common categories of eligibility
60   criteria in clinical trials. Laboratory values within a range demonstrating that organs are
61   functioning above a minimum acceptable level are often required for drugs that pose toxicity
62   risks to specific organ systems or are metabolized and cleared by that organ system(s). However,
63   clinical trial eligibility criteria often include specific laboratory values that may exclude patients,
64   even when these considerations may not apply.
65
66   Despite the importance of eligibility criteria to protect trial subjects from treatment-related risks
67   when applied appropriately, there is potential for unintended consequences if laboratory value-
68   based eligibility criteria are overly restrictive. Strict renal and hepatic function requirements have
69   been documented as one of the most common reasons for excluding potential subjects from

 70   clinical trials. 7 In oncology, many patients are older adults – a population in which some degree
 71   of organ dysfunction is common. However, such organ function status does not universally result
 72   in clinical consequences. Laboratory value abnormalities may also represent manifestations of
 73   the underlying malignancy or comorbidities that do not otherwise preclude safe participation in a
 74   clinical trial. Additionally, laboratory values may vary among healthy individuals across
 75   different age groups (e.g., pediatric patients), and racial and ethnic populations. For example, the
 76   Duffy null phenotype has a high prevalence in African Americans and is associated with lower
 77   absolute neutrophil counts but not with greater risk of infection. 8 Laboratory-value criteria that
 78   do not take these differences into account can hamper efforts to enroll a representative
 79   population. 9,10 Furthermore, overly restrictive laboratory-value criteria may result in exclusion of
 80   patients from trials of potentially beneficial treatments.
 81
 82   Analysis of industry-sponsored trials in oncology over time shows little variation in laboratory
 83   value-based eligibility criteria, suggesting that these criteria may have been carried forward from
 84   early-phase to late-phase trials despite the accumulation of clinical experience – during trials or
 85   after approval – that should be considered in formulating the criteria in late-phase trials. 11,12
 86
 87   One potential concern that sponsors may have regarding broadening eligibility criteria is that
 88   laboratory-related adverse events during the course of a trial may occur more frequently and/or
 89   with higher severity if the enrolled population has baseline laboratory-related abnormalities. This
 90   possibility does not outweigh the importance of enrolling subjects representative of the
 91   population that would receive the drug if approved. Importantly, this reality highlights the need
 92   for randomized trials. Randomized controlled trials are designed to distinguish the effects of a
 93   drug from other influences, which greatly facilitates the characterization of the drug’s safety in
 94   addition to demonstration of its effectiveness. Excluding patients with abnormal baseline
 95   laboratory values in randomized trials without an evidence-based safety concern has little benefit
 96   to a drug development program as the between-arm differences in a randomized trial provide
 97   more interpretable data on the drug’s adverse effects than other safety comparisons.

102   Laboratory values should be used as exclusionary criteria only when clearly necessary to
103   mitigate potential safety concerns. Among medical therapies, substantial differences in
104   metabolism, excretion, and toxicity profiles render broad recommendations across all trials
105   challenging. Restrictions based upon renal, hepatic, or bone marrow function should reflect
106   specific, well-reasoned concerns regarding drug exposure that might result in toxicity, or organ
107   susceptibility to toxicity, based upon available data (e.g., pre-clinical data, known safety profiles
108   of other relevant products). 13 Furthermore, as investigational drugs advance from early phase to
109   late phase development, laboratory eligibility criteria should be adjusted based on additional
110   available clinical data (e.g., pharmacokinetic, safety, exposure-response data from early-phase
111   trials, renal/hepatic impairment studies, drug-drug interaction studies, etc.). When developing
112   eligibility criteria for later phase studies, appropriate efforts to broaden eligibility criteria should
113   be implemented, removing or loosening any criteria no longer justified by specific concerns.
114   Acknowledging that safety is of utmost concern, protocols should be written with an aim to
115   enroll subjects who reflect those expected to use the drug, if approved.

117            A.      Scientific Justification for Laboratory Tests as Exclusion Criteria
118
119        •   Laboratory value-based eligibility criteria should be customized to the drug(s)
120            under investigation. Laboratory value requirements should be established based on the
121            investigational drug’s mechanism of action, pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics
122            (PK/PD), and anticipated toxicities. For instance, if an investigational drug does not
123            undergo hepatic metabolism and is not expected to cause hepatic toxicity for the intended
124            patient population, hepatic entry criteria should be sufficiently broad to avoid
125            unnecessary exclusions of patients (e.g., only excluding patients with elevations in ALT
126            or bilirubin that are multiple-fold above upper limit of normal (ULN)). Wherever data are
127            available from similar agents in a therapeutic class, previous experience should be used
128            as a guide. In some instances (e.g., programmed cell death receptor-1/programmed cell
129            death-ligand 1 checkpoint inhibitors), pharmacology and toxicity profiles are similar
130            across agents, facilitating use of comparable laboratory-related eligibility criteria, as long
131            as they are otherwise well-justified. In other instances (e.g., ALK inhibitors), each
132            individual drug may have different requirements depending on its individual PK/PD
133            profile. Importantly, laboratory value-related restrictions from earlier clinical trials
134            should not be carried forward automatically but should be modified to reflect the
135            experiences of subjects in earlier trials and in post-market use, as applicable.
136
137        •   Laboratory-based eligibility criteria should be only as restrictive as necessary to
138            mitigate the clinical risk(s) of concern. As an example, in clinical trials of drugs that
139            may prolong the QTc interval, low levels of electrolytes such as potassium, calcium, and
140            magnesium may increase the risk of cardiac arrhythmias. A common response to this
141            concern is to require levels of these electrolytes to be within normal limits. This results in
142            unnecessary exclusion of patients whose electrolyte levels may be slightly above the
143            normal range, even though there is no increased risk of QTc prolongation. In these cases,
144            precise protocol writing (e.g., requirements for laboratory tests to be above the lower
145            limit of normal rather than within normal limits) with an understanding of the intent of

155                B.     Accounting for Potential Expected Variations in Laboratory Values
156
157          •     The impact on trial eligibility, enrollment, and generalizability should be assessed
158                when selecting laboratory-based eligibility criteria. Consider the target population of a
159                study. For a trial that seeks to evaluate the safety and efficacy of a drug in renal or
160                urothelial cancer, allowing patients with lower levels of baseline renal function would
161                facilitate generation of data that is more representative of the broader patient population
162                who would be candidates for this treatment outside of clinical trials. For a trial that seeks
163                to evaluate safety and efficacy in patients with highly advanced and aggressive cancer
164                including those with hepatic metastases, allowing greater degrees of hepatic dysfunction
165                while ensuring appropriate safety measures to protect subjects, would facilitate drug
166                development in a patient population with high unmet need. In cases such as these, early
167                studies investigating alternative dosing regimens in subjects with organ impairment may
168                be beneficial. 14 Similarly, the hematologic laboratory criteria should be carefully
169                considered and broadened where appropriate in trials evaluating products intended for
170                patients with hematologic malignancies.
171
172          •     Demographic differences in laboratory test results, and their implication across
173                populations, should be understood when eligibility criteria are written. Given natural
174                variations in laboratory values among people that may be associated with race and
175                ethnicity, those criteria that are included should be sufficiently broad to allow for these
176                natural variations. 15,16 For example, as discussed above, sponsors may consider
177                broadening the laboratory inclusion and exclusion criteria for absolute neutrophil count in
178                patients with the Duffy null phenotype.
179
180          •     Laboratory abnormalities occur frequently without clinical significance. Reference
181                intervals generally include 95% of test results obtained from a presumably healthy
182                population. As noted previously, the likelihood of test results outside reference ranges is
183                far greater among individuals with cancer and may not be of clinical significance with
184                respect to the treatment being studied. When appropriate for specific laboratory analytes,

188          C.     Routine Reassessment of Laboratory-Based Exclusion Criteria
189
190   Routine reassessment of laboratory value-based exclusion criteria should be conducted during
191   the course of clinical research and drug development as investigational drugs progress from
192   earlier to later phase clinical trials.
193
194      •   Eligibility criteria should be adjusted based on accumulating clinical experience.
195          First-in-human trials investigating first-in-class drugs or use of novel platforms should
196          generally incorporate conventional laboratory-related eligibility criteria as a
197          precautionary measure, as the clinical pharmacology and toxicity profiles of the novel
198          drug in humans are not known. For subsequent drugs in the same class, if there is known
199          clinical pharmacology and toxicity data on drugs with similar mechanisms of action,
200          these criteria can be revised early and often. Sponsors should carefully evaluate eligibility
201          criteria, especially as the trials move to late phases, removing or reducing restrictions that
202          had been incorporated in early phase trials when such restrictions are not scientifically
203          justified and would result in unnecessarily narrowing the study population.
