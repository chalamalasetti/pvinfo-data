17   I.       INTRODUCTION
18
19   The purpose of this guidance is to help sponsors in the clinical development of products 2 for
20   bowel cleansing in preparation for a colonoscopy 3. Specifically, this guidance describes the
21   Food and Drug Administration’s (FDA’s) current thinking about the necessary attributes of
22   clinical trials for drugs being developed under Section 505 of the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act
23   (21 U.S.C §355) and 21 CFR Parts 312 and 314 for use as bowel cleansing agents before
24   colonoscopy, including trial population, trial designs, efficacy considerations, and safety
25   assessments. 4
26
27   The contents of this document do not have the force and effect of law and are not meant to bind
28   the public in any way, unless specifically incorporated into a contract. This document is
29   intended only to provide clarity to the public regarding existing requirements under the law.
30   FDA guidance documents, including this guidance, should be viewed only as recommendations,
31   unless specific regulatory or statutory requirements are cited. The use of the word should in
32   Agency guidances means that something is suggested or recommended, but not required.

35   II.        BACKGROUND
36
37   Colorectal cancer is a leading cause of cancer death in U.S. adults (Siegel et al. 2017). A
38   colonoscopy is recommended as the preferred modality for colorectal cancer screening (Rex et
39   al. 2017).
40
41   The success of a colonoscopy is closely linked to the adequacy of preprocedure bowel cleansing,
42   and up to 25 percent of all colonoscopies are reported to have inadequate bowel preparation.
43   Inadequate bowel preparation results in lower adenoma detection rate, longer procedural time,
44   lower cecal intubation rate, increased electrocautery risk, and shorter intervals between
45   examinations (Johnson et al. 2014). Adequate bowel preparation is also important for
46   diagnosing, surveilling, and treating other gastrointestinal conditions.
47
48   The goal of bowel cleansing is to remove fecal matter and fluids from the colon to permit
49   adequate visualization of colonic mucosa (Wexner et al. 2006; Mamula et al. 2009). An optimal
50   bowel cleansing product is safe, efficacious, and well tolerated, and results in high rates of
51   patient compliance with the prescribed regimen.

54   III.       DEVELOPMENT PROGRAM
55
56              A.     Trial Population
57
58   Sponsors developing products for bowel cleansing for colonoscopy should consider the
59   following:
60
61          •   Include patients undergoing routine colonoscopy either for screening or diagnostic
62              purposes.
63
64          •   Enroll a population that is sufficiently broad and inclusive to assess safety and efficacy
65              across all relevant subgroups likely to use the product. For example, enroll adequate
66              numbers of patients older than 65 years of age, as well as adequate representation of
67              ethnic and racial minorities.
68
69          •   Include patients with stable chronic kidney disease when available nonclinical and
70              clinical data indicate that exposing patients with reduced kidney function to a bowel-
71              cleansing product would not place patients at unreasonable risk and/or adequate steps can
72              be taken to mitigate potential risks. Sponsors should provide adequate justification for
73              including/excluding patients with different levels of kidney function based on the stage of
74              product development, safety profile of the product, and understanding of the effect of
75              kidney function on elimination of the product, if absorbed.
76

78
79   Sponsors developing products for bowel cleansing for colonoscopy should consider the
80   following:

 85       •    Typically, trials to demonstrate efficacy of new bowel-cleansing products are designed as
 86            noninferiority trials with comparison to one or more approved bowel-cleansing products.

 92            –    An appropriate comparator is a U.S.-approved product, is widely used in U.S. clinical
 93                 practice at the time of the trial, and has demonstrated efficacy using split-dose
 94                 administration. Choice of active control is further discussed in the guidance for
 95                 industry Non-Inferiority Clinical Trials to Establish Effectiveness (November 2016). 5
 96
 97            –    In general, we recommend that both the active comparator and the new product be
 98                 administered in a split-dose administration schedule, consistent with current
 99                 recommendations to optimize efficacy. Sponsors intending to study the new product
100                 as a single-day dose should discuss the plan and justification (e.g., safety in a specific
101                 subpopulation, etc.) with the Division before trial initiation.
102
103            –    We recommend that a different active comparator be used across the two phase 3
104                 trials (e.g., large volume versus low volume, or two products with different active
105                 ingredients) to best characterize the efficacy and safety profile of the new product.

107            C.       Efficacy Considerations
108
109            1.       Efficacy Assessments
110
111   Sponsors developing products for bowel cleansing for colonoscopy should consider the
112   following:
113
114       •    Efficacy should be assessed during insertion of the colonoscope (i.e., before washing and
115            suctioning) to ensure that the effect measured is attributable to the bowel-cleansing
116            product, not to intraprocedural cleansing efforts of the colonoscopist. 6
117
118       •    All colonoscopy readings should be video recorded.

120          •   We recommend that each endoscopy video be reviewed and scored by at least one
121              independent central reader who is blinded to treatment assignment. However, alternative
122              proposals (e.g., adjudicating a random subset for quality control) may be discussed with
123              the Division.
124
125          •   The quality of the bowel preparation on insertion should be assessed primarily using air
126              insufflation, and colonoscopists should be instructed to minimize use of water flushing to
127              permit assessment of the effects of the bowel-cleansing product.
128
129          •   We recommend the following regarding selection of a bowel cleansing scale to assess
130              efficacy:
131
132              –   Efficacy scales used to define the categories of excellent, good, fair, and poor should
133                  be based on nonoverlapping criteria. Previously used scales 7 have some limitations
134                  (such as overlapping criteria between grades) but may be modified to improve clarity
135                  of definition or criteria. A recommended four-point scale that could be used is shown
136                  in Table 1 (see Appendix). Sponsors wishing to develop an alternative scale should
137                  meet with the Division early in development to ensure agreement is reached on the
138                  adequacy of the proposed scale.
139
140              –   Each colon segment should be scored individually. 8
141
142          •   We recommend the following primary endpoint:
143
144              –   Proportion of patients achieving successful bowel cleansing (defined as a score of
145                  excellent or good in every colon segment).
146
147              –   Because adequate visualization of the entire colon is important, particularly in
148                  screening colonoscopy to avoid missed lesions, a score of fair or poor in any segment
149                  will result in the patient being considered a failure for the primary efficacy analysis.
150
151          •   We recommend the following secondary endpoints:
152
153              – Cecal/ileal intubation rate
154              – Proportion of patients achieving excellent grade in all segments
155              – Time to reach cecum
156              – Adenoma detection rate
157              – Nonpolypoid adenoma detection rate 9

159              2.        Statistical Considerations
160
161   Sponsors developing products for bowel cleansing for colonoscopy should consider the
162   following:
163
164          •   Sponsors should prespecify a primary estimand of interest for each endpoint and justify
165              that it is meaningful and that it can be estimated with minimal and plausible assumptions
166              with the proposed analysis. 10
167
168          •   Sponsors should conduct the primary efficacy analysis in the population of all
169              randomized patients who received at least a partial dose (initiated the first dose),
170              excluding patients who did not undergo a colonoscopy for reasons other than safety or
171              lack of efficacy (e.g., procedure cancelled for scheduling reason, study colonoscopist
172              unavailable because of emergency). The reason should be clearly documented.
173              Excluding a large number of patients for these types of reasons would be evidence of
174              poor study conduct and may raise a review issue.
175
176          •   Sponsors should prespecify methods to handle missing values for the primary and
177              secondary endpoints in the statistical analyses and should align them with the targeted
178              estimand of interest.
179
180          •   Patients unable to complete preparation because of adverse events or patients whose
181              physician opted not to conduct the colonoscopy because of inadequate preparation should
182              be considered nonresponders.
183
184              –    Supplementary analyses should evaluate the probability of successful bowel cleansing
185                   in the subset of patients who completed the full dose of study medication.
186
187          •   Strategies for handling other types of missing data, including missing segmental data,
188              should be discussed with the Division and clearly specified in the SAP.
189
190          •   The noninferiority (NI) margin should be prespecified and informed by the maximal
191              degree of inferiority of the test product to the active control that can be considered
192              acceptable to support approval. 11 Sponsors should submit a detailed justification of the
193              proposed NI margin to FDA and reach agreement with FDA on this margin before
194              initiating a trial.

199            D.        Safety Considerations
200
201   Sponsors developing products for bowel cleansing for colonoscopy should consider the
202   following:
203
204        •   In-person study visits should occur at randomization, day of colonoscopy (before
205            procedure), and 48 to 72 hours after colonoscopy for all patients to assess vital signs and
206            blood chemistry.
207
208        •   For patients with clinically significant abnormal laboratory tests at 48 to 72 hours post-
209            colonoscopy, sponsors should conduct an additional study site visit for laboratory testing
210            7 days post-colonoscopy.
211
212            –    Thresholds to define clinically significant abnormal laboratory parameters that would
213                 mandate repeat visits, even in the absence of symptoms, should be specified in the
214                 protocol and agreed upon with FDA before trial initiation.
215
216        •   Patients with ongoing adverse events should be followed until resolution or stabilization
217            of symptoms. If the adverse event was a laboratory test abnormality, sponsors should
218            conduct repeat testing to document return to baseline or, if not resolving, until stable.
219
220        •   Sponsors should prospectively plan for safety analyses to compare treatment groups with
221            respect to risk (e.g., with a risk difference, relative risk, rate ratio, or hazard ratio) along
222            with a confidence interval for the chosen metric to help quantify the uncertainty in the
223            treatment comparison. Sponsors should stratify by study any analyses of integrated data
224            from multiple studies.
225
226        •   We recommend the following laboratory shift analyses:
227
228            –    Proportion of patients with a normal baseline laboratory value who had a post-
229                 baseline value outside the normal limit at the 48 to 72 hours post-colonoscopy visit.
230
231                     Sponsors should further summarize these results by severity grade of the
232                      electrolyte abnormality, using a standard rating scale such as the Common
233                      Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events.
234
235            –    Proportion of patients with possible acute kidney injury, defined as those patients
236                 who experienced an increase in serum creatinine from baseline of more than 0.3
237                 mg/dL within 48 to 72 hours.

      with its active control; later on, the same treatment is used as an active control for another NI trial. If this pattern

      and allow inferior treatments to be marketed.

296   Table 1 Elements That Should Be Included in the Grading Criteria of the Bowel Cleansing
297   Assessment Scale
           Grading                                            Criteria
          Excellent      No more than small bits of fecal residue or fluid that requires no washing
                         and no/minimal suctioning, resulting in clear visualization of the entire
                         colonic mucosa.
          Good           Small amounts of fecal residue or fluid that can be easily washed and
                         suctioned but still results in clear visualization of the entire colonic
                         mucosa.
          Fair           Enough feces to require extensive washing and suctioning, which may
                         prevent clear visualization of the entire colonic mucosa.
          Poor           Large amount of fecal residue; clear visualization of entire colonic mucosa
                         is not possible.
