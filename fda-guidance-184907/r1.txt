15   I.         INTRODUCTION
16
17   This guidance provides recommendations for (1) increasing enrollment of females in clinical
18   trials and non-interventional studies to help ensure the generalizability of results, (2) analyzing
19   and interpreting sex-specific data, and (3) including sex-specific information in regulatory
20   submissions of medical products. 2 Historically (Sosinsky et al. 2022), fewer females than
21   males have been included in clinical trials 3 of medical products, which has led to a lack of
22   information available for females and their health care providers regarding the benefits and risks
23   of such medical products in females. Over recent decades, there has been an increase in the
24   representation of females in clinical trials for drugs 4 and devices, 5 with greater availability of
25   sex-specific data. However, females remain underrepresented in some therapeutic areas (see,
26   e.g., Zhou et al. 2024), which can make it challenging to evaluate the benefits and risks of
27   medical products for females in these therapeutic areas (see, e.g., Zhou et al. 2023; Scott et al.

41   II.       BACKGROUND
42
43             A.       Terminology
44
45   For the purposes of this guidance, the term sex refers to a biological classification based on
46   anatomical, physiological, hormonal, and genetic (chromosomal) traits categorized as female or
47   male. This guidance focuses on biological differences that can impact outcomes in clinical trials
48   and non-interventional studies.

84              C.       Why Consider Sex Differences in Medical Product Development?
85
86   Differences in physiology between females and males can lead to differences in disease
87   manifestations, as well as differences in the pharmacokinetics (PK), pharmacodynamics (PD),
88   efficacy, and safety of medical products (Madla 2021). Consequently, it is important to
89   characterize the impact of sex as part of medical product development to determine if there may
90   be differences in PK, PD, effectiveness, and/or safety associated with use of the medical product.
91   Identification of a clinically relevant difference by sex may inform a benefit-risk assessment and
92   inform product labeling. Assessment of sex differences should occur throughout drug

 96   Sex differences in PK and PD may arise from physiological (e.g., hormonal, body composition),

 98   (the active ingredient in certain FDA-approved drug products indicated to treat certain patients

109   effectiveness of a medical product. For further discussion on statistical considerations for
110   analyzing potential differences among treatment populations, see section IV of this guidance.

124              A.       Recruitment, Enrollment, and Retention
125
126   Factors associated with the female sex may impact clinical trial enrollment. 20 Potential
127   participants who are pregnant and/or lactating may be excluded from studies based on the safety
128   profile and teratogenicity of the medical product. Some trials contain contraception
129   requirements, which may limit the enrollment of females of reproductive potential who prefer
130   not to use contraception.
131
132   Sponsors should evaluate whether the demographic distribution of the potential trial population
133   changes across different key time points (e.g., at screening, including evaluation of trial
134   inclusion/exclusion criteria; after consent; and at various follow-up time points) and whether
135   these changes have an impact on trial participation. For example, if the proportion of females
136   drops significantly after screening for inclusion/exclusion criteria, this may suggest a need to
137   reexamine the inclusion/exclusion criteria. Removing or limiting unnecessary criteria could
138   improve the participation rates of females in the trial. Sponsors should also consider consulting
139   with academic institutions, health organizations that focus on female health (including
140   community-based organizations), and contract research organizations to determine practices best
141   suited to reducing challenges in enrollment and retention. Sponsors should also engage with the
142   patient community to help develop strategies for addressing enrollment and retention
143   challenges. 21
144
145   FDA encourages the following practices to improve the recruitment, enrollment, and retention of
146   females 22 in clinical trials: 23

148          •   Identify sites where recruitment of females can be facilitated (e.g., clinics or social media
149              sites that target females).
150
151          •   Consider flexibility in follow-up visit scheduling to allow various opportunities that
152              match participants’ schedules, which can include evenings and weekends.

232   IV.       STATISTICAL CONCEPTS
233
234             A.     Overview 34
235
236   Analyzing sex differences in medical product performance is an important component of
237   assessing product safety and effectiveness and can inform what goes in the product labeling to
238   improve patient care. Analyzing sex differences may involve (1) characterizing the treatment
239   effects for females and for males and any clinically relevant differences or potential differences
240   in those treatment effects, (2) determining whether the product provides greater benefits or risks
241   for a particular sex, (3) determining whether particular benefits or risks exist only for a particular
242   sex, or (4) determining how relevant the treatment effect for a particular sex is to understanding
243   the treatment effect for another sex. Apparent sex differences may result in the need to mitigate
244   clinically significant differences in safety or effectiveness between females and males.
245
246   The optimal analysis approach will depend on the type of inference that is sought. For example,
247   different approaches may be appropriate for characterizing potential differences in treatment
248   effects between sexes in contrast to estimating the treatment effect within a given sex. Sex is one
249   of many potential demographic characteristics typically evaluated in subgroup analyses of a
250   clinical trial or non-interventional study. When many subgroup analyses are performed, some of
251   the estimated treatment effects may represent random highs and random lows, being far above or
252   far below the respective underlying subgroup treatment effect. Observed differential treatment

268              B.       Analyses for Differences in Treatment Effects Between Females and Males
269
270   Analyses to evaluate differences in treatment effects between females and males should include
271   calculation of an estimated difference in treatment effects, along with associated uncertainty
272   (e.g., a 95% confidence interval (CI) for the difference). Such analyses can also include a test
273   for a quantitative interaction of treatment by sex (i.e., a test for whether the treatment effect is
274   larger for females or for males (the two-sided alternative hypothesis) or whether those treatment
275   effects are similar (the null hypothesis)).
276
277   Unless the clinical trial or study provides statistical power near 100% for demonstrating a
278   positive average treatment effect in the overall population (which is unlikely), statistical tests for
279   detecting plausible magnitudes of differences in treatment effects by sex (i.e., tests of a
280   treatment-by-sex interaction) tend to be underpowered. The 95% CI for the difference in
281   treatment effects by sex may be very wide and may include large differences. In many cases, the
282   test for a treatment-by-sex interaction may only have sufficient power to detect large differences
283   in treatment effects by sex. There may be insufficient power for some smaller, but still clinically
284   important, differences in treatment effects by sex. Therefore, lack of statistical significance
285   when testing for differing treatment effects by sex is not evidence of absence of a clinically
286   meaningful difference in treatment effects by sex. See section III.B, Trial Design.
287
288   For some clinical trials and non-interventional studies, there may be adequate power for
289   statistical tests of treatment effects using sex-specific subgroup data and for testing the
290   interaction of treatment-by-sex. In general, the power for a test of a treatment-by-sex interaction
291   tends to be larger the more similar the subgroup sizes of females and males. Notably, trials and
292   studies often involve the evaluation of differences in treatment effects by many factors beyond
293   sex, including by demographic factors such as age, race, and ethnicity and by important disease
294   characteristics. The risk of incorrectly concluding that a treatment-by-factor interaction exists
295   increases as the number of factors increases if such tests are performed without adjusting
296   significance levels for the multiple tests. 35

298           C.       Analyses to Estimate Treatment Effects in Females and Males
299
300   Analyses should be planned and conducted to estimate treatment effects and corresponding
301   uncertainty (i.e., a 95% CI) in females and males. Traditionally, only data from a given sex have
302   been used when estimating the treatment effect size for that sex. Sponsors should consider
303   prespecified statistical approaches that incorporate the data from all participants when estimating
304   the treatment effect within a given sex. For example, the relevance of the data from males in
305   estimating the treatment effect for females depends on how similar the results from males are to
306   the results from females and how much data there are for females alone. Estimators of sex-
307   specific treatment effects have greater precision than estimators based solely on the data for a
308   given sex (Pennello 2018). As noted earlier, sex is only one of many factors for which subgroup
309   analyses are typically performed, such that subgroup estimated treatment effects are subject to
310   random highs and lows, being far above or far below the respective underlying subgroup
311   treatment effect. Prespecified statistical approaches, which can also simultaneously consider
312   multiple factors, should quantitatively address these random highs and lows and can reduce the
313   potential for misinterpretation and making incorrect decisions resulting from those
314   misinterpretations (Lipsky 2010).
315
316           D.       Reporting Results of Analyses
317
318   As mentioned in section II.B of this guidance, sponsors must include the number of participants
319   entered into the study to date in their annual reports for drug and biological products conducted
320   under an IND, tabulated by age group, gender, and race. Sponsors also must present safety and
321   effectiveness data in the clinical data section of an NDA by gender age, and racial subgroups. 36
322   Because the enrollment demographics of the clinical study may impact the generalizability of the
323   conclusions, for clinical studies of devices, FDA recommends that sponsors report the number
324   and proportion of study participants by sex who were treated or diagnosed with the device as part
325   of a clinical study as appropriate. Where statistical significance is achieved for an average
326   treatment effect for the overall population, the results for each subgroup by sex should be
327   examined to understand whether the finding for the overall population was driven by the results
328   in only one of the sexes. Any potential difference by sex should be investigated, explained, and
329   discussed with the Agency.
330
331   The clinical significance of the difference in observed treatment effects between females and
332   males should be considered. As sex may be associated with other factors (e.g., weight) that
333   influence the size of the treatment effect, sponsors should collect and evaluate data in the trial or
334   study on any factors that may impact the treatment effect’s size or contribute to differences in
335   treatment effect, including analysis of those factors that may confound or contribute to an
336   observed difference in treatment effect by sex. An assessment should also be made on any other
337   important differences by sex, such as adherence to the assigned treatment. Results from the
338   evaluation of sex differences may help inform product labeling, which may include the findings
339   of PK differences for females and males to help inform treatment decisions.

341              E.      Considerations if Differences in Treatment Effects Between Females and
342                      Males Are Anticipated at the Design Stage
343
344   If important differences in the treatment effect by sex are anticipated at the trial design stage,
345   sponsors should enroll an adequate number of participants from each sex to conduct an
346   informative benefit-risk assessment. Sponsors should prespecify statistical analyses for
347   evaluating and reporting differences in treatment effects between females and males.
348
349   When a clinically important treatment effect is more likely for one sex than another sex, an
350   early-phase trial (e.g., phase 1 or phase 2) should ideally be performed to obtain information on
351   differences by sex. If there is evidence of benefit from the early-phase trials for a particular sex
352   and uncertainty around benefit for another sex, pivotal trials 37 can be designed to establish
353   benefit for the sex for which there is evidence of benefit and also continue to study the effects of
354   the product for the sex in which there is uncertainty around the benefit.
355
356   In such a setting, where there is biological plausibility for benefit in a given sex and uncertainty
357   around benefit for another sex, a trial can include certain approaches to control the type I error
358   probability across testing in the overall population and testing separately within a given sex.
359   Such testing schemes are used due to concerns that adding data from the sex for which there is
360   large uncertainty around benefit to the data from the sex for which there is expectation of benefit
361   will reduce the probability of a statistically significant finding. With such an approach, if
362   statistical significance is achieved only for the sex where there was expectation of benefit, then
363   performing a statistical test after adding the data from another sex will only be capable of
364   determining whether there is evidence of an average treatment effect in the overall population.
365   The test in the overall population may be driven by results in the sex where benefit was expected
366   and does not identify a treatment effect within the sex for which there was uncertainty around
367   benefit. The estimated treatment effect and the estimated treatment effect’s reliability would
368   need to be considered before determining whether there is benefit for the sex for which benefit
369   was uncertain.
